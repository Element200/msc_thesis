\begin{abstract}
% Abstract paragraphs should be unindented. master's abstracts are limited to 150 words; the limit is 350 words for doctoral abstracts. Abstract text must fit on a single page.
The Mpemba effect is a phenomenon in which initially hotter systems can relax to thermal equilibrium faster than initially cooler systems, \textit{ceteris paribus}. The phenomenon defies both intuition and conventional laws of cooling in equilibrium systems, and its exact mechanism -- and even its very existence in its original setting of freezing water -- are controversial to this day. However, many systems do seem to display the Mpemba effect, strongly motivating a general theory of nonequilibrium cooling. In the last decade, advances to this end have included a general theoretical framework for Markov systems and unambiguous experimental evidence for the Mpemba effects in noninteracting one-dimensional colloids. 

Previous work has largely assumed that cooling systems are coupled to baths that are instantaneously quenched. We seek to correct this typically unphysical assumption by studying, numerically and experimentally, the effects of finite-rate quenches. In this thesis, we build on previous work with numerical simulations and analytic solutions. Experimentally testing these results leads to numerous challenges that we discuss and resolve.

\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}
The Mpemba effect, first described by Erasto Mpemba in 1969 with Denis Osborne~\cite{Mpemba1969}, is the counterintuitive phenomenon in which a hot system cools faster than a warm system of the same dimensions, material, etc. Although some claim that the phenomenon was known as far back as Aristotle~\cite{Burridge2016}, Mpemba independently discovered it while trying to make ice cream with milk that had not yet fully cooled, and his discovery has continued to stimulate physicists.

An explanation of the Mpemba effect in terms of quasistatic cooling defies common sense: a hot object seemingly would have to cool to the warm temperature before it could cool further; consequently, one would expect that it must take strictly longer. Newton's law of cooling, $\partial_t T \propto -(T(t)-T_c)$ for equilibrium temperature $T_c$, leads to exponential relaxation and fails to explain the effect. Indeed, the existence of the Mpemba effect in water was contested for several decades following the publication, with Burridge and Linden going so far as to argue in 2016 that the Mpemba effect did not truly exist~\cite{Burridge2016}.\footnote{Burridge later showed with Hallstadius that a slightly more permissive definition of the Mpemba effect did allow for its observation~\cite{Burridge2020} but maintained that the results from his 2016 paper with Linden held true.} However, more recent work has confirmed the existence of the Mpemba effect in water while controlling for many of the phenomena Burridge and Linden cite~\cite{Brownridge2025a}. 

Although the Mpemba effect in water remains contentious, it is uncontroversial that observations of `Mpemba-like' effects do in fact exist in other systems, both real and simulated. We list some of those effects here.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Clathrate hydrates}: Ahn et al.\ found that tetrahydrofuran (THF) hydrate displays the Mpemba effect~\cite{Ahn2016}. THF hydrate is the simplest kind of clathrate hydrate, which is a class of crystalline solid polymers that can trap or `enclathrate' certain guest molecules inside cages of hydrogen bonds. Ahn et al.\ showed that the rate of THF hydrate formation decreases with initial temperature over a certain range of initial temperatures.
    \item \textit{Magnetoresistant magnetites}: Chaddah et al.\ showed that a half-doped magnetite in an antiferromagnetic phase, LCMO (\ce{La{0.5}Ca{0.5}MnO3}), demagnetised faster in an antiferromagnetic phase when the initial magnetisation was higher~\cite{Chaddah2010}. 
    \item \textit{Polylactide crystallisation}: Hu et al.\ showed that polylactide crystallised faster when quenched from a higher temperature~\cite{Hu2018}. Polylactide is a polymer with long chains that must be in a certain conformation in order for the polymer to crystallise. The authors reasoned that the more rapid crystallisation resulted from polymer chains that are more mobile at higher temperatures and could therefore rearrange themselves into advantageous structures faster.
    \item \textit{Granular fluid simulations}: Using numerical simulations of granular fluids made of inelastically colliding rigid spheres, Lasanta et al.\ showed that the system could cool faster when quenched from a higher temperature~\cite{Lasanta2017}. Unlike the other systems discussed so far, theirs lacks a phase transition. They found that if the probability density describing the velocity distribution of their system has a high-enough excess kurtosis, it displays the Mpemba effect. Interestingly, they also predict an \textit{inverse Mpemba effect}, in which cooler systems can heat faster. 
\end{enumerate}
The authors of all the papers cited here put forward different mechanisms for the Mpemba effect in their systems, but its ubiquity motivates a search for a general explanation. Lu and Raz proposed such a mechanism for a \textit{Markov} Mpemba effect, using nonequilibrium statistical mechanics to show that if a system was Markov (i.e., its current state depends only on the previous state), there was a straightforward condition to predict the existence of a Mpemba effect~\cite{Lu2017}. Under their framework, the system is strongly out of equilibrium while cooling, a fact critical for explaining why the system would not need to pass through an intermediate temperature. Temperature characterises equilibrium (i.e., Boltzmann) distributions, and between the initial and final times, the system state typically does not have the form of a Boltzmann distribution and consequently has no well-defined single temperature. The lack of a single well-defined local temperature was discussed by Mpemba and Osborne in their original paper, as well: they observed that the temperature of their system was not uniform but had significant spatial gradients~\cite{Mpemba1969}. 

However, since a temperature was no longer well-defined during the cooling process, Lu and Raz needed a new way to quantify how `close' a system was to equilibrium. They did this using a \textit{distance function}, a function (or functional) of two distributions that returns a positive number that approaches zero as one distribution converges to the other. Additionally, the distance function needs certain properties to ensure that one could not artificially create a Mpemba effect by choosing the `right' distance function. Lu and Raz defined a broad class of distance functions, within which a system that showed the Mpemba effect with one distance function would show the Mpemba effect with any other choice of distance function, as well.

The first experimental confirmation of Lu and Raz's theory came with Kumar and Bechhoefer's~\cite{Kumar2020} work in 2020, where they used a \textit{feedback optical trap}, an optical trap that could impose almost arbitrary forces on a trapped particle by measuring the position of the particle and moving the trap centre in response~\cite{Kumar2018}. With this feedback trap, they studied mesoscopic silica beads relaxing under a specially chosen potential. Instead of heating their sample to the very large temperatures they needed, they created an effective initial temperature by choosing an initial position $x_0$ from a Boltzmann distribution $x_0\sim\pi(x;T_h)$ at high temperature $T_h$, moving the bead to this position, and then allowing it to diffuse under an asymmetric double-well potential $U(x)$. The potential was imposed by a feedback optical trap, a technique they first developed in~\cite{Kumar2018}. This procedure was repeated many times to construct an ensemble, which could be viewed in aggregate as a colloid of noninteracting, one-dimensional particles~\cite{Goldstein2019}, as we show in Fig.~\ref{fig:ensemble}. Since the initial condition was a Boltzmann distribution at high temperature, the distribution had a high \textit{effective temperature}. The effective bath temperature then immediately dropped to the actual bath temperature, implying an \textit{instantaneous quench}. This system in particular allowed for the observation of a \textit{strong} Mpemba effect, previously predicted in~\cite{Klich2019}, where the cooling in the hot system was exponentially faster for a system that started from a higher temperature than it was for a system that started from a lower temperature. They later observed~\cite{Kumar2022} the inverse Mpemba effect that Lasanta et al.\ and Klich et al.\ had predicted~\cite{Lasanta2017, Klich2019}. 

% However, because of certain physical constraints that their system had, their potential had a somewhat artificial `box' that limited how large the initial position was allowed to be, and the Mpemba effect depended on the size of their box as well as the initial temperature. This becomes especially problematic when one tries to extend their results beyond the infinite quenches previously considered, as a high noise temperature enables the particles to escape the region of observation in an experiment. Consequently, the work in this thesis is twofold: we outline new potentials, exploring classes of potential that display strong Mpemba effects with no need for a box; and we implement these results in previous experiments, adding finite quenches using random forces.
In this thesis, we expand on Kumar and Bechhoefer's work to consider finite-rate quenches. The cooling of any real system involves a finite quench, since a bath is usually coupled to a system via a boundary through which heat must diffuse. These quenches may occur at variable rates depending on factors such as thermal conductivity and surface area. We know from previous work that a very fast (i.e., instantaneous) quench must show a Mpemba effect under certain conditions. However, if the system evolves quasistatically (i.e., if its distribution has a Boltzmann form for some temperature $T(t)$ at every time), it can never display the Mpemba effect because of the intermediate-temperature problem. Thus, we postulate a \textit{critical quench rate}, above which the Mpemba effect exists and below which it does not. Extending previous work to incorporate time-dependent quenches creates many additional constraints on experiments, which we will explore and discuss.

The outline of the rest of the thesis is as follows: The second chapter of this thesis will discuss the background theory, terminology, and definitions we use throughout. The third chapter will discuss the experimental setup and procedure, including the software that controls the experiment. The fourth chapter will discuss results from simulations and experiments, including theoretical constraints that are placed on previous experiments in order that their techniques can be extended to the systems we are interested in studying. Finally, the fifth chapter will summarise results from this thesis and sketch ideas for future work. We also present in Appendix A experimental results from a project that is somewhat outside the main thrust of this thesis.

\chapter{Theory}
In this chapter, we describe nonequilibrium statistical physics of fast quenches, including the theoretical approach of Lu and Raz~\cite{Lu2017} from 2017, the framework of Kumar and Bechhoefer~\cite{Kumar2020} from 2020, and some of the extensions relevant to finite-rate quenches. 

\section{The master equation}
The master equation describes the most general framework for the dynamics of \textit{Markov systems}. The dynamics of a probability distribution is Markov if its future state depends only on its current state and not on prior states: the system has no `memory'. Given a discrete probability distribution $p = [p_1, p_2, \dots]^\intercal$, the evolution of $p$ obeys
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = \mathbb W p,
\end{equation}
where the linear operator $\mathbb W$ has matrix elements
\begin{align}
    \mathbb W_{nm} &= \begin{cases}
        W_{nm},& n\neq m\\
        -\sum_{l\neq m} W_{lm},& n = m
    \end{cases}\\
    &= W_{nm}-\delta_{nl}\sum_{l\neq m} W_{lm}.
\end{align}
Here, $W_{nm}$ represents the transition rate \textit{from} state $m$ \textit{to} state $n$, and $\delta_{nm}$ is the Kronecker delta. The off-diagonal entries $\mathbb W_{nm}$ represent influxes from other states to $n$, and the diagonal entries represent effluxes from $n$ to other states. Each column in the master equation adds to zero by construction, ensuring that $\partial_t\sum_n p_n = 0$ and thus that probability is conserved at all times. A master equation need not have a stationary distribution, but if a steady state $\pi$ exists, then the system has \textit{detailed balance}: $\mathbb W_{mn}\pi_n = \mathbb W_{nm}\pi_m$ for all pairs $i,j$, meaning the influxes and effluxes of \textit{each state} are matched rather than just the net fluxes~\cite{vanKampen2011}. Thus, $\mathbb W\pi=\mathbf 0$. This formulation of the master equation applies only to discrete-state systems but can easily be applied to continuous systems by changing all indices to continuous variables and all sums to integrals. The operator $\mathbb W$ then takes the form
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = \mathbb W p = \int_{\mathbb R} [\underbrace{W(x|x')p(x)}_{\mathrm{influx}}-\underbrace{W(x'|x)p(x)}_{\mathrm{efflux}}]\dd{x'}.\label{eq:continuousmaster}
\end{equation}

\section{The Fokker-Planck equation}
The master equation can be recast by change of variables. Letting $r = x-x'$ and defining the jump-rate functions $W(x;r)$ such that 
\begin{align*}
    W(x';r) &{:=} W(x'|x)\\
    W(x-r; r) &= W(x'|r+x'),
\end{align*} 
we have 
\begin{align*}
    W(x;-r) &= W(x|-r+x)\\
    &= W(x|x')
\end{align*}
and $p(x-r, t) = p(x', t)$. $W(x;-r)$ represents the rate at which jumps of size $-r$ are made to land in state $x$. We lose no information by rewriting the master equation this way, as the only state that is a (signed) distance $-r$ away from $x$ is $x'$. Thus, Eq.~\ref{eq:continuousmaster} becomes
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{t} p(x,t) = \int_{\mathbb R} [W(x;-r)p(x,t)-W(x-r;r)p(x-r,t)]\dd{r}.\label{eq:rephrasedMaster}
\end{equation}
We now assume that $W(x;-r)$ is sharply peaked in $(-r)$ and that $W(x;r)p(x,t)$ is analytic in $x$. Consequently, only small jumps are likely at small times, and $(-r)$ is most probably small. We can then Taylor-expand $W(x-r;r)p(x-r,t)$ in $(-r)$:
\begin{equation}
    W(x-r;r)p(x-r,t) = W(x;r)p(x,t) - r\pdv{r} [W(x;r)p(x,t)]+\frac 12 r^2\pdv[2]{r}[W(x;r)p(x,t)]+ \dots.\label{eq:jumpMomentTaylorExpansion}
\end{equation}
We have
\begin{equation}
    \left[ \int_{\mathbb R} W(x;-r)\dd{r}-\int_{\mathbb R}W(x;r)\dd{r} \right]p(x,t) = 0 = \pdv{p}{t}.
\end{equation}
The two integrals in the bracket equal each other because $\int_a^b f(x)\dd{x} = \int_a^b f(a+b-x)\dd{x}$. Thus, substituting in the expression Eq.~\ref{eq:jumpMomentTaylorExpansion}, we are left with
% \marginnote{\tiny{This is what I referred to as King's rule earlier (which, it turns out, is jargon exclusive to Indian high-school calculus; `King' refers to the playing card, not a person). The reason this holds is because regardless of the properties of an integrable function, the signed area under any curve is the same whether you integrate forwards along $x$ or backwards (the RHS essentially just recentres and flips the curve). It's quite a handy trick.}}
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = \int_{\mathbb R}\left[ \sum_{\nu=1}^\infty \frac{(-r)^\nu}{\nu!}\pdv[\nu]{x}[W(x;r)p(x,t)] \right]\dd{r}.
\end{equation}
We exchange the integral and summation, and define the jump moments 
\begin{equation}
    c_\nu(x) = \int_{\mathbb R} r^\nu W(x;r)\dd{r}.
\end{equation}
Then we can recast the master equation as
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = \sum_{\nu=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^\nu}{\nu!}\pdv[\nu]{x}[c_\nu(x)p(x,t)].
\end{equation}
This infinite sum is called the \textit{Kramers-Moyal expansion}~\cite{vanKampen2011}. If we keep only two terms, we are left with the Fokker-Planck equation,
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = -\pdv{x}[c_1(x)p(x,t)] + \frac 12\pdv[2]{x}[c_2(x)p(x,t)],
\end{equation}
which describes the probability density of Brownian objects diffusing under a force. There is not a good reason to truncate the series to order two, as there is no small parameter that allows us to ignore higher-order terms. Van Kampen~\cite{vanKampen2011} provides a more rigorous derivation of the Fokker-Planck equation from the master equation. However, the Pawula theorem~\cite{Pawula1967} shows that for the probability to be normalisable, we must either have one, two, or infinitely many nonzero $c_\nu$ in the Kramers-Moyal expansion; we cannot self-consistently truncate the series at three terms, for example. The important feature, however, is that the Fokker-Planck equation is a type of master equation, and thus when we limit our analysis to the FPE, Lu and Raz's general results on the master equation still apply, as they also showed~\cite{Lu2017}.

If we define $c_1(x)=F(x)/\gamma$ with $F$ the force and $\gamma$ the drag coefficient\footnote{$\gamma$ has dimensions $\mathrm{[mass/time]}$.} (characteristic of a linear drag $F_d = -\gamma \dot x$) and $c_2(x)= 2D(x)$, we are left with an expression for the probability distribution of a Brownian particle in a medium of viscosity $\gamma$, experiencing force $F$ and diffusing with diffusivity $D$. This form will be more useful when describing actual diffusing silica microspheres. If the medium is homogeneous, $D$ is independent of $x$, and
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = -\frac 1{\gamma}\pdv{x}[F(x)p(x,t)]+D\pdv[2]{p}{x}.\label{eq:FPE}
\end{equation}
We also often use the fluctuation-dissipation relationship~\cite{vanKampen2011},
\begin{equation}
    D = \frac{\kB T}{\gamma},\label{eq:fluctuationDissipation}
\end{equation}
where $\kB $ is the Boltzmann constant and $T$ is the temperature. 

\section{Eigenfunction expansion of the $\mathbb{W}$-operator}
The master equation suggests the separation \textit{ansatz} 
\begin{equation}
    p(x,t) = v(x)\e^{-\lambda t}.\label{eq:separationAnsatz}
\end{equation}
Plugging this into the master equation, we have
\begin{align}
    -\lambda \e^{-\lambda t}v(x) &= \e^{-\lambda t}\mathbb W v(x)\\
    \mathbb W v(x) &= -\lambda v(x).\label{eq:eigenvalueFPE}
\end{align}
Equation~\ref{eq:eigenvalueFPE} is an eigenvalue equation and allows us to expand the solution $p(x,t)$ in an eigenbasis:
\begin{equation}
    p(x,t) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n v_n(x)\e^{-\lambda_n t}.
\end{equation}
If all probabilities normalise to $\int_{\mathbb R} p(x,t)\dd{x} = 1$, the eigenfunctions $v_n(x)$ are members of a Hilbert space with $\ev{u_m,v_n} \propto \delta_{mn}$. If $p(x,t)$ is defined on $x\in\mathbb R$, the inner product is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \ev{u_m,v_n} = \int_{\mathbb R} u_m(x)v_n(x)\dd{x}.
\end{equation} 
Here, $u_m(x)$ denotes the \textit{left eigenfunction} of $\mathbb W$, which equals the right eigenfunction of the adjoint operator $\mathbb W^\dagger$, satisfying $\mathbb W^\dagger u_m = -\lambda_m u_m$. Since $\mathbb W\neq \mathbb W^\dagger$ in general, $u_m(x) \neq v_m(x)$.\footnote{We may, however, transform $v_m$ to express the Fokker-Planck equation as a Schr\"odinger equation. Doing so makes the propagator of time evolution Hermitian, and the left and right eigenfunctions of the modified equation will be identical up to complex conjugation. A Hermitian operator has purely real eigenvalues, and since eigenvalues are preserved under similarity transformations, all $\lambda_n\in\mathbb R$. Thus, no periodicity is allowed in the solution to the FPE in one dimension. See Risken for details~\cite{Risken1996}.} We can use this fact to isolate the coefficients $a_n$ by taking the inner product of $p(x,t)$ with $u_n(x)$:
\begin{align}
    \ev{u_n,p(x,t)} &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k \ev{u_n,v_k}\e^{-\lambda_k t}\\
    &= a_n\ev{u_n,v_n}\e^{-\lambda_n t}\\
    \therefore \ev{u_n,p(x,0)} &= a_n\ev{u_n,v_n}\\
    a_n &= \frac{\ev{u_n,p(x,0)}}{\ev{u_n,v_n}}\label{eq:an}
\end{align}
Thus, the initial condition $p(x,0)$ determines the coefficients in the series.\footnote{We also require \textit{natural boundary conditions} to ensure that a stationary solution exists: $\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty} j(x,t) = 0$, where the probability current $j(x, t) := F(x)p(x,t)/\gamma-D\partial_x p(x,t)$ for all times $t$. In addition, to ensure that $\ev{u, \mathbb W v} = \ev{\mathbb W^\dagger u, v}$, we must also require the Neumann boundary conditions $\eval{\pdv{u}{x}}_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}=0$ while solving the conjugate FPE~\cite{Risken1996}.} 

Setting the left-hand side of the master equation to zero, we are left with the steady-state equation
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb W \pi(x) = 0 = 0\cdot \pi(x).
\end{equation}
In other words, the steady-state equation is an eigenvalue equation with zero eigenvalue.\footnote{The fact that the master equation must have at least one zero eigenvalue is perhaps most obvious in the discrete-state case. Since each column of $\mathbb W$ sums to zero, the \textit{rows} of $\mathbb W^\intercal$, which has the same eigenvalues as $\mathbb W$, must sum to zero. Thus, $\mathbb W^\intercal \mathbf u= \mathbf 0 = 0\mathbf u$, where $u_n = 1$ for all $n$. } Because of the $\e^{-\lambda_n t}$ terms in the infinite series, the eigenfunction expansion of the master equation is always absolutely convergent, provided $\lambda_n>0$ for all $n>1$, which is readily shown~\cite[Ch.~5]{Risken1996}. Thus, we may freely order terms in the sum so that $\lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \dots$, and $\lambda_1=0$, the smallest possible value. In the particular case of the Fokker-Planck equation with uniform diffusivity in space, we are left with the equation 
% \marginnote{\tiny{Doesn't the Perron-Frobenius theorem only apply to matrices with positive terms? $\mathbb W$ has a negative diagonal.}}
\begin{align}
    \frac 1{\gamma}\pdv{x}[F(x)\pi(x)] &= \frac{\kB T}{\gamma}\pdv[2]{\pi}{x}\label{eq:boltzmannderiv1}\\
    F(x)\pi(x) &= \kB T\pdv{\pi}{x} \label{eq:boltzmannderiv2}\\
    \pi(x) &= \frac 1{Z(T)} \exp[-\frac{U(x)}{\kB T}]\label{eq:boltzmannDistro},
\end{align}
where we define the normalisation constant
\begin{equation}
    Z(T) = \int_{\mathbb R} \exp[-\frac{U(x)}{\kB T}]\dd{x},
\end{equation}
and the potential $U(x)$ is defined so that 
\begin{equation}
    F(x) = -\dv{U}{x}.
\end{equation}
The integration constant between line (\ref{eq:boltzmannderiv1}) and (\ref{eq:boltzmannderiv2}) must be zero since the probability current and the probability $\pi$ vanish at $\pm\infty$. 
Equation~\ref{eq:boltzmannDistro} defines the \textit{Boltzmann distribution}. More generally, for any Markov system with detailed balance where each state $x$ has energy $E(x)$, the Boltzmann distribution for a system in a bath of temperature $T_i$ is given by
\begin{equation}
    \pi(x;T_i) = \frac 1{Z(T_i)} \e^{-\frac{E(x)}{\kB T_i}}.
\end{equation}
For brevity, we will often denote $\pi_i(x) = \pi(x;T_i)$

We can now explain the theory of Lu and Raz~\cite{Lu2017}. We say that a system has temperature $T$ if its probability distribution of states is given by the Boltzmann distribution
\begin{equation}
    \pi(x;T) = \frac 1{Z(T)} \exp[-\frac{U(x)}{\kB T}].
\end{equation}
We suppose the system has temperature $T_c$ at equilibrium ($t\rightarrow\infty$) and temperature $T_h$ at $t=0$. The evolution of $p(x,t)$ under the master equation $\partial_t{p}=\mathbb W p$ with the initial condition 
\begin{equation}
    p(x,0)=\pi(x;T_h)\label{eq:initialCondition}
\end{equation} 
and steady state 
\begin{equation}
    \lim_{t\rightarrow\infty} p(x,t) = \pi(x;T_c)\label{eq:equilibriumCondition}
\end{equation}
then defines what it means for a nonequilibrium system to cool. This, then, gives us a way to resolve the problem we stated in the introduction: since $p(x,t)$ need not be Boltzmann for $0<t<\infty$, the system does not, in general, have a well-defined temperature between $t=0$ and $t\rightarrow\infty$. Figure~\ref{fig:coolingTheory} shows an example of cooling in a double-well potential. Initially, the Boltzmann distribution at high temperature (a) looks flat and wide. Probability mass then starts to pool in the two wells (b), when peaks start to rise. At long times (c), the equilibrium Boltzmann distribution is reached. However, we now have another problem: the Mpemba effect (and most `Mpemba-like' effects) relied on a phase transition such as freezing or demagnetisation to make precise the statement that a hot system could cool `faster' (\textit{ceteris paribus}). For example, Mpemba and Osborne~\cite{Mpemba1969} used the freezing time to determine whether the Mpemba effect occurred in water, whereas Ahn et al.~\cite{Ahn2016} used the formation time of THF hydrate. If the time for the phase transition to occur decreases with an increase in initial temperature, they claim that a Mpemba effect occurred. However, phase transitions that involve nucleation are highly sensitive to small differences in the experimental setup and thus hard to characterise reproducibly. Moreover, some Mpemba-like effects, such as the granular simulations Lasanta et al.\ considered, have no phase transition at all~\cite{Lasanta2017}. Indeed, since a Markov system only reaches the equilibrium temperature asymptotically and does not have a phase transition, it is not obvious how to define what it means for one system to cool `more quickly' than another. Consequently, a more precise definition of the Mpemba effect is warranted. Lu and Raz therefore introduced another tool, a \textit{distance function}.

Metrics are often used in analysis to quantify how `far apart' two elements of a set are. For example, the $\sup$-norm, $\mathscr D_{\mathcal L_{\infty}}:\mathbb H\times\mathbb H\rightarrow\mathbb R_{\geq 0}$ where 
\[\mathscr D_{\mathcal L_{\infty}}(f_1,f_2)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}{\abs{f_1(x)-f_2(x)}}\] 
and $\mathbb H$ is the set of all continuous functions on $\mathbb R$ quantifies how far apart two continuous functions $f_1$ and $f_2$ are. Such a definition allows one to make precise statements about convergence in real function-space.

The approach Lu and Raz~\cite{Lu2017} used was similar: define a function of two probability distributions (or a functional\footnote{A functional is an operator that takes in a function and returns a number.} of two probability densities) that returns a positive number whose magnitude quantifies how different they are. The distance function returns zero only if the two distributions are identical. Additionally, to preserve intuitive ideas of distance to equilibrium, they defined criteria that distance functions must obey. More specifically, a distance function $\mathscr D$ is a function that takes in two probability distributions $p_1,p_2$ and returns a number $\mathscr D[p_1, p_2] \geq 0$ with the following properties: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Positivity:} $\mathscr D[p_1,p_2] \geq 0$, and $\mathscr D[p_1,p_2] = 0 \iff p_1=p_2$;
    \item \textit{Monotonic increase with initial temperature:} \\$\mathscr D[\pi(x;T_h), \pi(x;T_c)] > \mathscr D[\pi(x;T_w), \pi(x;T_c)]$ if $T_h>T_w>T_c$ (i.e., we consider systems with high temperatures `farther' from the equilibrium distribution);
    \item \textit{Monotonic decrease with time:} Given a cooling system whose distribution is $p(x,t)$, $\mathscr D[p(x,t_1),\pi(x;T_c)] \geq \mathscr D[p(x,t_2),\pi(x;T_c)]$ if $t_1<t_2$ (i.e., systems always relax \textit{towards} equilibrium, never away);
    \item \textit{Convexity and continuity:} $\mathscr D[p, \pi(x;T_c)]$ is smooth and convex in $p$. 
\end{enumerate}
Note that Lu and Raz do not require $\mathscr D$ to be a metric, as $\mathscr D$ need not be symmetric in its two arguments nor obey the triangle inequality. However, $\mathscr D$ serves the same purpose as a metric: it puts a number on how far two distributions are from each other, allowing us to precisely quantify how fast one distribution converges to another. The second condition captures the idea that a hot distribution is `farther' from equilibrium than a warm distribution. The third condition captures the idea that, if the system is always cooling, it must approach equilibrium with time. The final condition is a bit technical but is required so that the distance function can be Taylor expanded in one of its arguments and truncated without the remaining terms dominating the series. 

Examples of the distance function $\mathscr D$ include
\begin{itemize}
    \item The $\mathcal L_1$ distance\footnote{More generally, the $\mathcal L_k$ distance is defined as $\mathscr D_{\mathcal L_p}[p_1,p_2]=\left[ \int_{\mathbb R} \abs{p_1(x)-p_2(x)}^k\dd{x} \right]^{1/k}$. However, we will rarely find norms with $k>1$ to be useful for describing the Mpemba effect.}, 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathscr D_{\mathcal L_1}[p_1,p_2] = \int_{\mathbb R} |p_1(x)-p_2(x)|\dd{x};\label{eq:L1definition}
    \end{equation}
    \item The Kullback-Leibler divergence, 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathscr D_{\mathrm{KL}}[p_1,p_2] = \int_{\mathbb R} p_1(x)\ln\left[ \frac{p_1(x)}{p_2(x)} \right]\dd{x}.\label{eq:KLdefinition}
    \end{equation}
    % \item The entropic distance from Lu and Raz~\cite{Lu2017}:
    % \begin{equation}
    %     \mathscr D_{\mathrm{ent}}[p_1,p_2] = \frac{\ev{U}_{1}-\ev{U}_2}{\kB T} - \int_{\mathbb R} \left[ p_1(x)\ln [p_1(x)]-p_2(x)\ln [p_2(x)] \right]\dd{x},
    % \end{equation}
    % where $\ev{f}_i = \int_{\mathbb R} f(x)p_i(x)\dd{x}$.
    % \marginnote{\tiny{The definition of the entropic distance stated here is accurate to Lu and Raz's definition. I did have a note earlier that this was analogous to the free energy difference, but I think we discussed that the KL divergence was closer to the nonequilibrium FE.}}
\end{itemize}

Since we always calculate the distance with respect to the steady-state Boltzmann distribution (at temperature $T_c$), we define a new functional for brevity, 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c[p(x,t)] = \mathscr D[p(x,t),\pi(x;T_c)].
\end{equation}
We will often indicate in a superscript which specific distance function we use (eg. $\mathcal D_c^{\mathrm{KL}}$ for $\mathscr D_{\mathrm{KL}}$); when no superscript is used, $\mathcal D_c$ refers generically to any distance function that obeys the Lu-Raz properties we defined in this section.

This theoretical machinery now allows for a precise definition of the Mpemba effect: two Markov systems characterised by distributions $p_h(x,t)$ and $p_w(x,t)$ that cool to the same distribution $\pi_c$ start in states $p_h(x,0) = \pi(x;T_h)$ and $p_w(x,0)=\pi(x;T_w)$, exhibit a Mpemba effect if there exists a finite $t^*$ for which 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c[p_h(x,t)] \leq \mathcal D_c[p_w(x,t)],\label{eq:mpembaCondition}
\end{equation}
for all $t>t^*$, for some distance function $\mathcal D_c$. Since 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c[p_h(x,0)] > \mathcal D_c[p_w(x,0)]
\end{equation}
by construction, the distance curves of a system displaying a Mpemba effect must cross an odd number of times\footnote{Nothing forbids multiple crossings, but a reversal needs an odd number of crossings. Liu et al.~\cite{Liu2026} have predicted a double-crossing, in a double-well potential similar to those we outline here: in such a potential, there is no Mpemba effect.}, crossing for the last time at $t=t^*$, when $\mathcal D_c[p_h(x,t^*)]=\mathcal D_c[p_w(x,t^*)]$~\cite{Klich2019}. Figure~\ref{fig:mpembaFPE}(a) shows the two distance curves crossing, whereas Fig.~\ref{fig:mpembaFPE}(b) does not. We therefore say that the potential in (a) displays the Mpemba effect, and the potential in (b) does not. The level and time of crossing can vary based on the choice of distance function. However, since the Mpemba effect describes a physical phenomenon, its existence should not depend on the specific choice of distance function. In the next section, we demonstrate that, provided the four properties described here hold, the Mpemba effect is agnostic to the choice of $\mathscr D$. We will later present a counterexample that shows how ignoring the axioms described here can potentially lead to claims of spurious Mpemba effects.

\section{Conditions for the existence of the Mpemba effect}
Consider two systems, $p_h(x,t)$ and $p_w(x,t)$, for which $p_h(x,0)=\pi(x;T_h)$, $p_w(x,0)=\pi(x;T_w)$, and identical steady states $p_h(x,\infty)=p_w(x,\infty)=\pi(x;T_c)$. Both systems have eigenfunction expansions given by
\begin{equation}
    p_0(x,t) = \pi_c(x) + \sum_{n=2}^\infty a_n(T_0) v_n(x)\e^{-\lambda_n t}.
\end{equation}
Here, we use $p_0$ to denote the system that starts at initial temperature $T_0\in\{T_h,T_w\}$.
As before, we have ordered our series so that $\lambda_2<\lambda_3<\dots$. As the $\e^{-\lambda_3 t}$ term decays exponentially faster than the $\e^{-\lambda_2 t}$ term, we can truncate the series to two terms for sufficiently large times:\footnote{More precisely, we can ignore all $e^{-\lambda_3t}$ and higher terms if $t\gg t_c = \ln(|a_3/a_2|)/(\lambda_3-\lambda_2)$, the critical time at which $|a_3|e^{-\lambda_3 t}=|a_2|e^{-\lambda_2t}$. At $t=t_c$, the $e^{-\lambda_2 t}$ term starts to exceed higher-order terms.}
\begin{equation}
    p_0(x,t) \approx \pi_c(x) + a_2(T_0) v_2(x)\e^{-\lambda_2 t}.
\end{equation} 
Note that only $a_2$ depends on the initial temperature. The condition we require for the Mpemba effect is $\mathcal D_c[p_h(x,t^*)] \leq \mathcal D_c[p_w(x,t^*)]$. With this approximation, we can now use the smoothness of $\mathcal D$ in $p$ to then make the following approximation, since the $a_2v_2(x)\e^{-\lambda_2t}$ term will be small at a suitably large time:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c\left[ \pi_c+a_2v_2\e^{-\lambda_2 t} \right] \approx \mathcal D_c\big[\pi_c\big] + a_2\e^{-\lambda_2 t}\ev{\fdv{\mathcal D_c}{v_2}\big[\pi_c\big], v_2},
\end{equation}
where $\fdv{}{f}$ denotes the functional derivative with respect to the function $f(x)$. Here, we use the form of a Taylor expansion for functionals,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal F\big[ f+\epsilon g \big] = \mathcal F\big[f\big] + \epsilon \ev{\fdv{\mathcal F}{g}\big[f\big],g} + \order{\epsilon^2}.
\end{equation}
We notice that in this equation, $\mathcal D_c\big[{\pi_c}\big]=0$ by the first property. Thus,
\begin{align}
    \mathcal D_c\big[\pi_c+a_2(T_0)v_2\e^{-\lambda_2 t}\big] \approx a_2(T_i)\e^{-\lambda_2 t}\ev{\fdv{\mathcal D_c}{v_2}\big[\pi_c\big],v_2}
\end{align}
for initial temperature $T_i$. With $V := \ev{\fdv{\mathcal D_c}{v_2}\big[\pi_c\big],v_2}$, we have 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c\left[ \pi_c+a_2(T_i)v_2\e^{-\lambda_2 t}\right] \approx a_2(T_i)V\e^{-\lambda_2 t}.
\end{equation}
Equating this to our previous expression, we see
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c\big[p_i(x,t)\big] \approx V\e^{-\lambda_2 t}a_2(T_i).
\end{equation}
The condition in Eq.~\ref{eq:mpembaCondition} for the Mpemba effect then approximately reduces to
\begin{equation}
    V\e^{-\lambda_2 t^*}a_2(T_h) \leq V\e^{-\lambda_2 t^*}a_2(T_w),
\end{equation}
for some $t^*<\infty$. However, although $\e^{-\lambda_2t^*}$ is strictly positive, $V<0$ is possible if $a_2(T_h),a_2(T_w)<0$ as well. Thus, $a_2(T_w)<a_2(T_h)$ is not implied but rather
\begin{equation}
    \abs{a_2(T_h)} \leq \abs{a_2(T_w)}.
\end{equation}

Put differently, the condition reduces to the statement that $\abs{a_2(T)}$ is nonmonotonic in $T$.  Since we have not used any particular form of $\mathcal D_c$ beyond the properties we required, the Mpemba effect is independent of the choice of distance function.\footnote{This argument is not quite as rigorous as Lu and Raz's, as it ignores the convexity that they required of the distance function. If the distance function is not convex, the terms that we truncated may sum to a quantity that goes to zero more slowly than $a_2V\e^{-\lambda_2t}$. See Lu and Raz for details~\cite[SI]{Lu2017}.} For example, if we choose the $\mathcal L_1$ distance, then
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c^{\mathcal L_1}[\pi_c+a_2v_2\e^{-\lambda_2t}] = \int_{\mathbb R}\abs{\pi_c+a_2v_2\e^{-\lambda_2t}-\pi_c}\dd{x} = \abs{a_2}\e^{-\lambda_2t}\int_{\mathbb R}\abs{v_2(x)}\dd{x}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \fdv{\mathcal D_c^{\mathcal L_1}}{v_2} = \sgn(v_2(x)),
\end{equation}
yielding~\cite{Kumar2020}
\begin{equation}
    \ev{\fdv{\mathcal D_c^{\mathcal L_1}}{v_2}[\pi_c],v_2} = \int_{\mathbb R}\sgn(v_2(x))v_2(x)\dd{x} = \int_{\mathbb R}\abs{v_2(x)}\dd{x}.
\end{equation}
In this proof, the second term in the eigenfunction expansion dominates the behaviour. However, this approximation can break down if there exists $T_h^*>T_w>T_c$ for which $a_2(T_h^*)=0$ and $a_2(T_w)\neq 0$. In this case, the third term dominates the series at long times:
\begin{equation}
    p_h \approx \pi_c + a_3(T_h^*)v_3\e^{-\lambda_3 t}.
\end{equation}
Repeating the previous analysis before but using $a_3\e^{-\lambda_3 t}v_3$ as the small term, we arrive at the approximation
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c\big[p_h\big] \approx a_3(T_h^*)\e^{-\lambda_3 t}\ev{\fdv{\mathcal D_c}{v_3}\big[\pi_c\big],v_3}\label{eq:strongMpembaph}.
\end{equation}
Comparing this with 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c\big[p_w\big] \approx a_2(T_w)\e^{-\lambda_2 t}\ev{\fdv{\mathcal D_c}{v_2}\big[\pi_c\big], v_2}\label{eq:strongMpembapw},
\end{equation}
we see that $\mathcal D_c\big[p_w\big] \gg \mathcal D_c\big[p_h\big]$ for any time large enough that Eq.~\ref{eq:strongMpembapw} holds. Equation~\ref{eq:strongMpembaph} implies that systems with this special condition display cooling that is exponentially faster. Klich et al.~\cite{Klich2019} refer to this as the \textit{strong} Mpemba effect. For convenience, we always use the $\mathcal L_1$ norm, defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:L1definition}, in this thesis. To demonstrate that the $\mathcal L_1$ norm is a valid distance function, we note that since the $\mathcal L_1$ norm satisfies the properties of a metric, positivity is automatically satisfied. To prove the third property, we expand the probability density $p(x,t)$ in eigenfunctions, as in Eq.~\ref{eq:eigenvalueFPE}. We see that 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c^{\mathcal L_1}[p(x,t)] = \int_{\mathbb R} \sum_{k=2}\abs{a_k(T_h)v_k(x)}\e^{-\lambda_kt}\dd{x}.
\end{equation}
Differentiating with respect to time,
\begin{equation}
    \dv{t}\mathcal D_c^{\mathcal L_1}[p(x,t)] = \int_{\mathbb R} \sum_{k=2}\left[ -\lambda_k\abs{a_k(T_h)v_k(x)} \right]\e^{-\lambda_kt}\dd{x}.
\end{equation}
Since each eigenvalue is positive, each term in the sum, and therefore the whole integrand, is strictly nonpositive everywhere. Thus, the integral is nonpositive as well, and the $\mathcal L_1$ norm is monotonically nonincreasing with time. To see that the $\mathcal L_1$ distance is convex, we evaluate the distance function for two probability density functions (PDFs), $p_1(x,t)$ and $p_2(x,t)$ and use the triangle inequality:
\begin{align}
    q\mathcal D_c^{\mathcal L_1}[p_1(x,t)]+(1-q)\mathcal D_c^{\mathcal L_1}[p_2(x,t)] &= \int_{\mathbb R}\left[ \abs{qp_1(x,t)-q\pi_c(x)} +\abs{(1-q)p_1(x,t)-(1-q)\pi_c(x)}\right]\dd{x}\\
    &\geq \frac 12\int_{\mathbb R}\abs{p_1(x, t)+p_2(x, t)-2\pi_c(x)}\dd{x}\\
    &\qquad = \int_{\mathbb R}\abs{\frac{p_1(x, t)+p_2(x, t)}2-\pi_c(x)}\dd{x}\\

One important note is that although the existence of the Mpemba effect with one distance function implies its existence for every other distance function, the crossing time of the distance curves $t^*$ may depend on the choice of $\mathcal D_c$; indeed, $t^*$ need not even be upper-bounded (although always finite); the crossing may occur at a fairly short time but at a time far greater than any experiment could run for for another legitimate choice of distance function. To exclude this possibility, Vu and Hayakawa~\cite{Vu2025} recently described a \textit{thermomajorisation ordering} $\prec_{\pi_c}$ of all possible probability distributions $p(x,t)$. This ordering also quantifies how far a distribution is from $\pi_c$, and one can thus say that the \textit{thermomajorisation Mpemba effect} occurs if $p_h(x,t^*) \prec_{\pi_c} p_w(x,t^*)$ for some $t^*$, guaranteeing a crossing exists for any distance function at $t^*$ at latest. The thermomajorisation Mpemba effect implies the Mpemba effect, but the converse is not true, and the thermomajorisation Mpemba effect upper-bounds the time $t^*$ at which we can say that the hot system has cooled `more' than the warm system. Thus, at least a subset of all systems displaying the Mpemba effect must display the effect within a finite time.

The distance function requires access to the probability distribution of the system we are studying. However, finding the probability density function (PDF) associated with a thermodynamic system is not always tractable. For example, an $N$-spin Ising lattice has $2^N$ possible states, implying that the vector containing the probabilities associated with every possible state grows exponentially in memory. This issue has motivated the use of other quantities to detect the Mpemba effect. Equilibrium kinetic theory often defines the temperature of a gas in terms of the average kinetic energy of its molecules: $\ev{\text{K.E.}}=d\kB T/2$ for $d$ degrees of freedom by the equipartition theorem~\cite{Blundell2017}, suggesting $T\propto \ev{U}$ for a system with purely kinetic energy. While the temperature is not well-defined between the start and end times, the average energy
\begin{equation}
    \bar E(t) = \ev{U}_{p(x,t)}=\int_{\mathbb R} U(x)p(x,t)\dd{x}
\end{equation}
certainly is. Moreover, the average energy of an equilibrium system does grow monotonically with temperature. Several authors have thus used crossings (or the lack thereof) in the average energy to argue for the existence or nonexistence of a Mpemba effect~\cite{Ohga2024,BaityJesi2019,Vadakkayil2021}. However, the average energy does not obey the third rule we required of distance functions: it is not guaranteed to decrease monotonically with time and can thus erroneously suggest a Mpemba effect when none exists. To show that the average energy is a poor choice of distance function, we consider the system from Kumar and Bechhoefer's~\cite{Kumar2020} work, with a high value of the asymmetry parameter $\alpha=|x_{\max}/x_{\min}|$. As we will discuss in the next section, the first stage of cooling in a double-well potential is a fast stage in which the particle falls into the closest possible well, before a slow stage, in which particles hop over the barrier until equilibrium is reached. Thus, if the particle is much more likely to be on the right side of the barrier, there can then be a transient stage of cooling at some transient time $t_t$ with \textit{more probability mass in a low-energy well than in equilibrium}, thus making the average energy lower than it should be in equilibrium. Thus, $\ev{U}_{p(x,t_t)} < \ev{U}_{\pi(x;T_c)}$. We illustrate this behaviour in Fig.~\ref{fig:averageEnergy}, using a \num{10000}-particle simulation with the same parameters Kumar and Bechhoefer chose, with $\alpha=9$~\cite{Kumar2020}.\footnote{I came up with the idea for this counterexample and then did the appropriate simulations. I then created what is shown here as Fig.~\ref{fig:averageEnergy} (Fig.~7 in Ref.~\cite{Teza2023}) and was acknowledged for it in the article.} In the figure, the initial state, denoted by the red PDF, is more or less uniform and cools to the black PDF, overlaid in all plots as dashed lines. We see that, at an intermediate time, the relative height of the taller peak in the blue PDF is slightly higher than it would be at equilibrium. The relative numbers of particles in each well at the intermediate time perfectly line up with the equilibrium average energy, and the energy difference is thus equal to zero \textit{even though the system has not finished relaxing}. Thus, the difference in average energies between $p(x,t)$ and the equilibrium state can be zero even if $p(x,t)\neq \pi(x;T_c)$, violating the assumption of positivity. Moreover, plotting the $\mathcal L_1$ distance $\mathcal D_c^{\mathcal L_1}[p(x,t)]$ for the same system, we see that the system does not actually demonstrate a Mpemba effect, as Kumar and Bechhoefer observed for large values of $\alpha$~\cite{Kumar2020}. The average energy fails as a signature of the Mpemba effect because, for larger values of $\alpha$, the transient state will contain more probability mass in the lower-energy well. Thus, the average energy cannot in general serve as a distance function for identifying the Mpemba effect.

\section{The Langevin equation}
The Fokker-Planck equation, Eq.~\ref{eq:FPE}, describes the probability density of Brownian particles but not the fluctuating random trajectories of individual particles. The probability densities can be estimated from trajectories but will only match the results of the FPE in the infinite-particle limit. To evaluate statistics for a finite number of particles, we instead use the \textit{Langevin equation}, a stochastic differential equation related to the FPE, to model our experiment. 

The Langevin equation is derived from Newton's second law applied to a Brownian particle experiencing a drag with friction coefficient $\gamma$ but with an added term to account for random noise. Thus, the particle experiences two forces, a deterministic force $F(x)$ and a random force arising from thermal fluctuations due to interactions between the particle and surrounding fluid molecules. The scale of the random force is set by the particle diffusivity, which obeys the fluctuation-dissipation relationship with drag constant $\gamma$. 
If the force $F(x)$ were zero, we would expect that the average distance the particle travels after time $t$ is zero, with a variance $2Dt=2t\kB T/\gamma$ in one dimension.\footnote{In $d$ dimensions, the variance is $2dDt$.}

The Langevin equation extends Newton's second law to systems with thermal fluctuation forces:
\begin{equation}
    m\ddot x = -\gamma\dot x + F(x) + \sqrt{2\gamma\kB T}\eta(t).\label{eq:underdampedLangevin}
\end{equation}
Here, $\eta(t)$ is Gaussian white noise, with $\ev{\eta}=0$ and $\ev{\eta(t)\eta(t')}=\delta(t-t')$\footnote{This relation implies that the dimensions of $\eta$ are $[\mathrm{time^{-1/2}}]$.}. If the system is \textit{overdamped}, we may ignore the left-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:underdampedLangevin} and divide throughout by $\gamma$ to arrive at the overdamped Langevin equation
\begin{equation}
    \dot x(t) = \frac{F(x(t))}{\gamma} + \sqrt{2D}\eta(t),\label{eq:continuousLangevin}
\end{equation}
The trajectory defined by $x(t)$ will, with probability one, be continuous everywhere and yet differentiable nowhere~\cite{Gusak2009}. We have also used the fluctuation-dissipation relationship, $D=\kB T/\gamma$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:fluctuationDissipation}). Here, we first discretise Eq.~\ref{eq:continuousLangevin} using a finite-difference approximation of the derivative to obtain, for small $\Delta t$,
\begin{equation}
    x(t+\Delta t) \approx x(t) + \frac{F(x(t))\Delta t}{\gamma} + \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\xi(t), \label{eq:discreteLangevinForces}
\end{equation}
where $\xi(t) = \int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta(t')\dd{t'}$ is a dimensionless random number. This equation has a direct correspondence with Eq.~\ref{eq:FPE}. We may then restate Eq.~\ref{eq:discreteLangevinForces} as the discrete dynamical system
\begin{equation}
    x_{n+1} = x_n + \frac{F(x_n)}{\gamma}\Delta t + \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\xi_n, \label{eq:discreteLangevin}
\end{equation}
with $t_n = n\Delta t$, $\xi_n\sim\mathcal N[0,1]$, and $\ev{\xi_m\xi_n}=\delta_{mn}$. We use the It\^o convention for integrating stochastic differential equations while integrating the Langevin equation.\footnote{The It\^o convention arises more naturally in mathematics and avoids correlations between the noise $\eta(t)$ and the position $x(t)$, at the cost of breaking the chain rule of calculus. By contrast, the Stratonovich convention preserves familiar integration rules and better represents physical situations where $\eta$ has short but nonzero time correlations. It implies a correlation between $\eta(t)$ and $x(t)$ that can make certain calculations tricky; see Gardiner~\cite[Ch.~7]{Gardiner2021} for details. However, the distinction between the two does not matter much if the noise term is additive and independent of state.} An individual trajectory is not by itself particularly insightful, but we can construct empirical probability densities if we have a large number of them. In the limit of an infinite number of trajectories, the empirical probability density solves the FPE, Eq.~\ref{eq:FPE}~\cite{vanKampen2011}. For each trial, we draw $x_0\sim \pi(x;T_h)$, which accounts for the initial condition.

To account for experimental realities, some modifications are desirable. In our experiment, we will use a \textit{feedback trap} to impose the potential $U(x)$. Feedback traps allow us to generate near-arbitrary time-varying forcefields by measuring the position of a particle and applying the force it experiences based on the current position measurement. More precisely, we generate a force at each timestep based on previous measurements of the position $\bar x_{n-1}$ by changing the value of $X_n$ in an approximately-quadratic potential $U_m(x_n|X_{n-1}) = k(x_n-X_{n-1})^2/2$ (where $k$ is the trap strength) so that $F_m(x_{n}|X_{n-1})=F(x_{n-1}, t_{n-1})$. We cannot impose the force $F(x_n, t_n)$ at time $t_n$ since the timestep $\Delta t$ also acts as a delay between the measurement of $\bar x_n$ and the movement of $X_{n-2}$ to $X_{n-1}$. Since the force imposed at time $t_n$ is $F_m(x_n|X_{n-1})$, the feedback force is not equal to the true force $F(x_n)$ in general although, as we will show in Ch.~3, it approaches the true force in the limit where $\Delta t\ll \gamma/k=t_r$, the \textit{relaxation time}. This condition amounts to requiring that the particle position $x$ not change too much in time $\Delta t$. We note that the force is calculated based on the measured position $\bar x_n$ rather than the true position $x_n$, as $\bar x_n$ differs from $x_n$ by some small normally distributed random number $\sigma_m\zeta_n$, called the \textit{measurement noise}. Here $\zeta_n\sim\mathcal N[0,1]$, with $\ev{\zeta_n\zeta_{n'}}=\delta_{nn'}$, and $\sigma_m$ sets the scale of the measurement noise. We then modify the discrete Langevin equation~\ref{eq:discreteLangevin} into the coupled equations
\begin{align}
    x_{n+1} &= \frac{F(\bar x_n)}{\gamma}\Delta t + \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\xi_n \label{eq:discreteLangevinMeasurementNoise}\\
    \bar x_{n+1} &= x_n + \sigma_m\zeta_n.
\end{align}
Putting the two equations together, we see that the argument of $F$ is $x_{n-1}$, not $x_n$:
\begin{equation}
    x_{n+1} = \frac{F(x_{n-1}+\sigma_m\zeta_{n-1})}{\gamma}\Delta t + \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\xi_n.
\end{equation}
Provided $\sigma_m$ is small, we may expand the force to obtain
\begin{equation}
    x_{n+1} \approx \frac{F(x_{n-1})}{\gamma}\Delta t + \frac{F'(x_{n-1})}{\gamma}\sigma_m\zeta_{n-1}\Delta t + \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\xi_n.\label{eq:measurementNoiseLangevin}
\end{equation}
If $\sigma_m$ is small and $\Delta t\ll t_r$, simulations based on Eq.~\ref{eq:discreteLangevin} (ideal discretisation) and Eq.~\ref{eq:measurementNoiseLangevin} (discretisation with delay and measurement noise) reveal no noticeable difference in the statistics of interest.

Using the Langevin equation rather than the Fokker-Plank equation for simulations is particularly desirable given the similarity between the analysis on integrated Langevin trajectories and those obtained from an experiment: while the FPE predicts distance curve crossings and helps one understand the relaxation dynamics, the Langevin equation better represents the effects of a finite ensemble size and measurement noise, which are both present in experiments. We will compare the results from simulations with experimental results in Ch.~4. An example of the Mpemba effect seen in Langevin simulations and accompanying FPE solutions is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:mpembaLangevin}(a).

Once we have a large number of trajectories, we compute the empirical PDF at every timestep. The analysis outlined in the previous section is then applied to the empirical PDFs. One of the key differences between the Langevin solutions and Fokker-Planck solutions is the existence of a \textit{noise floor}, resulting from the finite ensemble size. We see in Fig.~\ref{fig:mpembaLangevin} that if $N$ is too small, the Mpemba effect can be hidden by the noise floor. The finite number of repetitions can also cause small deviations from the theoretical solutions, especially close to the noise floor. The fact that the empirical cumulative density function (CDF) can never exactly equal the true CDF results in a noise floor that scales as $\order{\sqrt{N_b/N}}$~\cite{Kumar2020} with $N_b$ the number of histogram bins and $N$ the ensemble size.\footnote{The $1/\sqrt{N}$ convergence is actually a little more complicated and does not necessarily work for any choice of distance function. Kumar and Bechhoefer demonstrated $1/\sqrt N$ scaling for the $\mathcal L_1$ distance of a uniform distribution~\cite{Kumar2020}. According to the central limit theorem, the empirical \textit{cumulative density function} $P_N(x,t)$ does converge to the true CDF $P(x,t)$ with $\sqrt N[P_N(x,t)-P(x,t)] \rightarrow \mathcal N[0,P(x,t)(1-P(x,t))]$ almost surely, which does lower-bound the $\mathcal L_1$-distance since 
\[P_N(x,t)-P(x,t) = \int_{-\infty}^x \left[ p_N(x',t) - p(x',t) \right]\dd{x'},\]
and 
\begin{align*}
    \sqrt N\abs{P_N(x,t)-P(x,t)} &= \sqrt N\abs{\int_{-\infty}^x \left[ p_N(x',t) - p(x',t) \right]\dd{x'}}\\
    &\leq \sqrt N\int_{-\infty}^x \abs{p_N(x',t) - p(x',t)}\dd{x'}
\end{align*}
by the triangle inequality. Thus, $\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \sqrt N\abs{P_N(x,t)-P(x,t)}\leq \mathscr D_{\mathcal L_1}[p_N,p]$, demonstrating that the $\mathcal L_1$ distance has a best-case convergence of $1/\sqrt N$ but could potentially converge more slowly. As Fig.~\ref{fig:mpembaLangevin} demonstrates, the power law scaling does hold empirically for the $\mathcal L_1$ distance over any value of $N$ we can reasonably use in the lab. Thus, we shall take the scaling law as a given.} We see in Fig.~\ref{fig:mpembaLangevin}(a) that the noise floor may be potentially be so high as to entirely occlude the crossing. This fact will constrain our choice of potential, as discussed in Ch.~4.

\section{Previous work}
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{{./Figures/chapter2/asymmetricDoubleWellPotential/asymmetricWellPotential.pdf}}
    \caption[Bechhoefer-Kumar potential]{\small{The potential Kumar and Bechhoefer~\cite{Kumar2020} used, described in Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential}. We also illustrate the box constraint and the maximum slopes.}}
    \label{fig:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential}
\end{figure}
We have shown that to demonstrate the Mpemba effect, one needs to find a potential $U(x)$ for which $a_2(T)$ is non-monotonic. A simple quadratic potential will not do: solving the FPE with $F(x)=-kx$ and initial condition $p(x,0)=\pi(x;T_h)$ yields the analytic solution
\begin{align}
    p(x,t) &= \sqrt{\frac{k}{2\pi \kB [T_c+(T_h-T_c)\e^{-2t/t_r}]}}\exp[-\frac{kx^2}{2 \kB [T_c+(T_h-T_c)\e^{-2t/t_r}]}]\label{eq:harmonicFPE}\\
    &= \pi\left( x; T_c+(T_h-T_c)\e^{-2t/t_r} \right)
\end{align}
To convert this expression into an eigenfunction expansion, we expand in powers of $\e^{-t/t_r}$ to obtain
\begin{equation}
    p(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\e^{-(x/\sigma)^2/2} + \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\left[ \left( \frac x\sigma \right)^2-1 \right]\left( \frac{T_h-T_c}{T_c} \right)\e^{-(x/\sigma)^2/2}\e^{-t/t_r}+\order{\e^{-2t/t_r}},
\end{equation}
with $\sigma = \sqrt{\kB T_c/k}$.

From this, we find $\lambda_n = (n-1)k/\gamma$ and $a_2(T_h) = (T_h-T_c)/T_c$. As $a_2$ is monotonic in $T_h$, the Mpemba effect cannot exist in a harmonic potential~\cite{Walker2021}. Alternatively, we note that Eq.~\ref{eq:harmonicFPE} is Gaussian at every time $t$, corresponding to a system at temperature $T_c+(T_h-T_c)\e^{-2t/t_r}$. Since the evolution of the system is always Boltzmann, we may expect that it should never yield the Mpemba effect due to the intermediate-temperature problem. As discussed earlier, Kumar and Bechhoefer used a \textit{feedback optical trap} to impose more esoteric potentials than the approximately harmonic potential an optical trap generates. 

% A feedback optical trap generates a virtual potential using an optical trap, both of which we will describe in more detail in Ch. 3.  
A real medium cannot be heated to an arbitrarily high temperature.\footnote{Kumar and Bechhoefer's experiment required temperatures as high as $T_h=1000~T_c$, which in the real world would be around \qty{3e5}{\kelvin}: certainly not physically sustainable in water, which boils at \qty{373}{\kelvin}.} Instead, Kumar and Bechhoefer used a trick to ensure that the initial state is always a Boltzmann distribution at some temperature $T_h$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Choose a position $x_0\sim\pi(x;T_h)$ and place the particle at $x=x_0$. 
    \item Release the particle at $t=0$ and allow the particle to diffuse under the potential $U(x)$ long enough that the distribution $p(x,t)$ relaxes to steady state at the end of the protocol time $\tau$.
    \item Repeat for a large number of times $N$ to create an ensemble.
\end{enumerate}
At time $t=0$, the bath cools instantaneously to $T=T_c$. This algorithm thus sets the initial condition $x_0\sim \pi(x;T_h)$, as we do in numerical simulations. 

Although feedback optical traps allow for near-arbitrary $F(x,t)$, there are a few physical constraints placed on the force that we will need to account for when designing a potential. The exact reason for the constraints will be explained further in Ch. 3. 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The feedback optical trap cannot generate arbitrarily high forces. In reality, the maximum force is capped: 
    \begin{equation}
        \abs{F} \leq F_{\max}.\label{eq:forceConstraint}
    \end{equation} 
    \item The feedback time $\Delta t$, which also serves as the measurement interval, should be much smaller than the characteristic timescale of the system, which near a local minimum is the relaxation time. Thus, $\Delta t \ll t_r = \gamma/k = \gamma/\abs {F'(x)}$ for $F(x) \approx -kx$ near a local minimum. 
    \begin{equation}
        \abs{F'(x)} \ll \gamma/\Delta t.\label{eq:derivativeConstraint}
    \end{equation} 
    \item The measured displacement of the particle $x$ will be accurate only if $x$ is within a certain small domain $x\in [X_{\min},X_{\max}]$, where $X_{\min}<0<X_{\max}$ is called the \textit{calibration region}. For potentials that grow asymptotically in either direction, it is unlikely at low temperature that a particle will ever leave the calibration region. However, as $T_h$ increases, the domain containing most of the probability mass of $\pi_h(x)$ will grow, meaning that particles will have a higher chance of escaping the calibration region. To ensure the accuracy of all measurements, Kumar and Bechhoefer imposed a constraint on the initial distribution $p(x,0)$, where all initial positions had to be drawn from a \textit{box}: a domain $\mathcal B = [x_{\min}, x_{\max}]$ chosen such that $\mathcal B\subset [X_{\min},X_{\max}]$. This constraint is equivalent to using a potential $U_{\mathcal B}(x)$, where
    \begin{equation}
        U_{\mathcal B}(x) = \begin{cases}
            U(x),& x\in\mathcal B\\
            \infty,& x\notin\mathcal B
        \end{cases}.
    \end{equation}
    Of course, since we cannot actually impose arbitrarily high forces, we cannot actually impose $U_{\mathcal B}(x)$ for $x\notin\mathcal B$. This construction therefore violates the assumptions of the Mpemba effect, since the Boltzmann distribution describing the initial state uses a different potential ($U_{\mathcal B}(x)$) from the final state ($U(x)$), and thus $p(x,0)\neq \pi(x;T_h)$. However, if $x_0\in\mathcal B$, it is very unlikely that the particle would move to a region where the potential is higher; the only particles that are likely to leave are those that have an initial state very close to the boundaries of $\mathcal B$.\footnote{Kumar and Bechhoefer noted that $x\notin\mathcal B$ for only \qty{0.003}{\percent} of the total experiment time~\cite{Kumar2020} and observed no meaningful difference between simulated distance curves of initial conditions prepared with and without a box.} Since $x\in\mathcal B$ for almost all times $t$, we can thus approximate $U(x)\approx U_{\mathcal B}(x)$ throughout the protocol. However, the difference between $U$ and $U_{\mathcal B}$ may not be small when we consider finite-rate quenches. 
\end{enumerate}

With these constraints in mind, Kumar and Bechhoefer used a \textit{tilted asymmetric double-well potential},
\begin{align}
    U_{\mathrm{KB}}(x) &= \begin{cases}
        F_{\max}(x-x_r)+U_0(x_r),& x > x_r\\
        U_0(x),& x_l \leq x \leq x_r\\
        -F_{\max}(x-x_l)+U_0(x_l),& x < x_l\\
    \end{cases}\\
    \nonumber\\
    U_0(x) &= E_b\left[ 1-\left( \frac{x}{x_w} \right)^2 \right]^2 + \frac 12 \Delta E\left( \frac{x}{x_w} \right),\label{eq:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential}
\end{align}
% \marginnote{\tiny{I use the same definition of $U_0$ later on to define the potential with asymmetric slopes. There were one or two places where there were conflicting definitions, and I've fixed those up.}}
plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential}.

Here, $E_b=2~\kB T_c$\footnote{The natural unit of energy to use at these scales is the thermal energy $\kB T_c \approx$ \qty{4}{\zepto\joule}, which scales the energy in the Boltzmann distribution. This unit is not well-defined, since the temperature may vary between experiments; but scaling with respect to the ambient temperature $T_c$ is arguably a feature rather than a limitation, since if we were to use a fixed unit often used in the literature like \qty{1}{\pico\newton\nano\metre}$=$\qty{1}{\zepto\joule} to determine the parameters of our potential, the results between different experiments would not be the same. Similarly, we choose lengths where the distance between wells for a potential \textit{with zero asymmetry}, $x_s=2x_w=1$. We do use seconds as the unit of time, but we could also scale time by choosing units where the \textit{relaxation time}, which we define in Ch.~3, is unity.} is called the \textit{barrier energy} and determines the height of the barrier at $x\approx 0$ relative to the average well depth; $\Delta E=1.3~\kB T_c$ is called the \textit{tilt energy} and determines the difference in depths of the two wells; $F_{\max} = 50~\kB T_c/x_s$ is the maximum force described earlier, and $x_l$ and $x_r$ are chosen so that $U(x)$ is $\mathcal C^1$ continuous everywhere. Note that the `asymmetry' in this context refers only to the fact that $\abs{x_{\max}}\neq \abs{x_{\min}}$; the tilt energy $\Delta E$ between the two wells should be thought of as a different type of asymmetry.
This potential has two wells, with the left well higher than the right. At equilibrium, the probability mass in the left well will be smaller than that in the right. The two dominant eigenmodes of cooling from the Fokker-Planck equation (characterised by $\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_3$) then have an intuitive physical explanation, as outlined in Ch\'etrite et al.~\cite{Chetrite2021}. The first stage of cooling involves a \textit{fast relaxation}, characterised by the high rate $\lambda_3$\footnote{Higher eigenmodes also contribute to the fast stage, but $\lambda_3$ is the slowest of them all and therefore the rate-determining step of the first stage of relaxation.}, where nearly all of the probability mass immediately falls into the nearest well. The second stage of cooling, the \textit{slow stage} characterised by the lower rate, $\lambda_2$, involves particles hopping over the barrier until the equilibrium mass ratios are reached. Brownian particles hopping over a barrier is a well-studied phenomenon. For overdamped dynamics, the rate at which particles hop over the barrier is called the \textit{Kramers rate}~\cite{Risken1996}, 
\begin{equation}
    r(T) = \frac{\sqrt{\omega_w\omega_b}}{2\pi} \exp[-\frac{(E_b-E_w)}{\kB T}],
\end{equation}
where for any arbitrary twice-differentiable potential $U(x)$ with minimum at $x_w$ and maximum at $x_b$ (so we may approximate the potential as $m\omega_w^2/2$ and $E_b-m\omega_b^2/2$), $E_b = U(x_b)$, $E_w=U(x_w)$, and $\omega_b = \abs{\partial_x^2 U(x_b)}/m$, and $\omega_w = \abs{\partial_x^2U(x_w)}/m$. For the double-well potential, the energy difference in the exponential is given approximately by $E_b\pm\Delta E/2$, depending on whether we choose the deeper or shallower well. Thus, we expect that the transition rate from the shallower to the deeper well, $r_+ \propto e^{-(E_b-\Delta E/2)/\kB T}$, and the transition rate from the deeper to the shallower well $r_- \propto e^{-(E_b+\Delta E/2)/\kB T}$, are related to $\lambda_2$.
 
Choosing an initial condition so that the probability masses in both wells are already at their equilibrium ratios at the end of the fast stage then corresponds to the condition for the strong Mpemba effect, $a_2=0$. Vucelja and Walker formalised this intuition, by defining the right-well equilibrium probability mass~\cite{Walker2022}
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_R(T) = \int_{x_b}^{x_{\max}} \pi(x;T)\dd{x},
\end{equation}
where $x_b$ is the position of the barrier (the local maximum). Walker-Vucelja showed that for $\lambda_2 \rightarrow 0$, the idea we outlined here for the strong Mpemba effect can then be restated as
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_R(T_h) = \Pi_R(T_c).\label{eq:VuceljaMpemba}
\end{equation}
% We demonstrate the equivalence of this condition to the non-monoticity of $a_2$ in Fig.~[a2figure]. 
The box $\mathcal B$ is critical to Kumar and Bechhoefer's result: without it, $\Pi_R(T)$ grows monotonically in $T$ and asymptotes at $\Pi_R(\infty)=1/2$. In particular, they defined an \textit{asymmetry parameter} $\alpha = \abs{x_{\max}/x_{\min}}$ and found that for specific choices of $\alpha$, the strong Mpemba effect could be achieved when $T_h \gg T_w$. Mathematically, $a_2$ is actually a function of both $T$ and $\alpha$, and a special value of $\alpha$ is chosen so that $a_2(T_h,\alpha)=0$. 

Two recent, related preprints~\cite{Liu2026,Liu2026a} argue that the box is \textit{necessary} to observe the Mpemba effect in a double-well potential. We shall test that claim in Ch. 4.

\section{Finite-rate quenches}
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{{Figures/chapter2/quenchplots/quenches.pdf}}
    \caption[Various quench functions]{\small{Various quench functions including the Newtonian quench (Eq.~\ref{eq:newtonianQuench}), the linear quench (Eq.~\ref{eq:linearQuench}), the Kovacs `quench', and the two trivial quenches, Eqs.~\ref{eq:quasistaticQuench} and \ref{eq:instantaneousQuench}. each with an initial temperature $T_0/T_c=100$ and cooling rate $\Lambda\tau=6.5$ (where the protocol time $\tau=$ \qty{0.1}{\second}).}}\label{fig:assortedQuenches}
    % DONE: Make lines for linear, Newtonian, and Kovacs quenches thick
\end{figure}
Physically, the approach taken thus far corresponds to an \textit{instantaneous} quench, a system that is initially at equilibrium in a bath of temperature $T_h$ and starts relaxing after the bath instantaneously cools to $T_c$. However, real systems usually exist in baths that gradually cool, often with an exponential relaxation, as in Eq.~\ref{eq:harmonicFPE}. The assumption that the bath temperature drops instantly, potentially by several orders of magnitude, is in general\footnote{Cooling rates as high as \qty{1e10}{\kelvin\per\second} have been achieved through \textit{splat cooling}, for example by using a shockwave to shoot liquid metal through a hole in a crucible, causing it to atomise, and having it land upon a cold substrate~\cite{Ruhl1967}; thus, it is not \textit{impossible} to achieve such rapid cooling.} rather unphysical. The approach outlined here thus applies to small particles in a bath but not to bulk objects. In a bulk object, heat would have to diffuse from the bath into the bulk through a boundary, meaning that the temperature of a point within the surface would lag behind the bath temperature, depending on how fast heat can flow through the object. For example, a cold piece of meat placed on a hot pan needs to cook for several minutes before the interior temperature is the same as the surface temperature. 

Many recent publications have considered gradually time-varying bath temperatures for various reasons. We review here some more recent examples.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Teza et al. considered how coupling to an external bath through a `boundary term' affects anomalous relaxation dynamics~\cite{Teza2023}. 
    \item The effects of finite cooling have also been considered by implementing \textit{cooling protocols}, specific functions describing the bath temperature that can cause the system to cool considerably faster. Intuitively, it is fairly evident that one can shorten the cooling time by having the system cool to a lower temperature $T_C$ than the desired temperature $T_c$. Then, when the system temperature has dropped to $T=T_c$, the bath temperature may be instantaneously changed from $T=T_C$ to $T=T_c$. This protocol is formally known as the \textit{Kovacs effect} and was first explored by Militaru et al.~\cite{Militaru2021}, who used an optically levitated nanoparticle to demonstrate it. Furthermore, Militaru et al.\ considered a system that was near critical damping (described by the more general \textit{Klein-Kramers equation}~
    \cite{Risken1996} instead of Eq.~\ref{eq:FPE}); they use the kinetic and potential energy of the particle to demonstrate a crossing. We explored the shortcomings of measuring a Mpemba-like effect using the energy in Sec.~2.6.1.
    \item Gal and Raz considered a system that could heat more rapidly after an initial \textit{cooling} protocol~\cite{Gal2020}. They considered a four-state system which demonstrated that precooling the bath could cause the system to heat faster.
    \item The \textit{Pontus-Mpemba effect}, introduced by Nava and Egger, tries to find systems that not only cool faster but take less time to heat~\cite{Nava2025}. Their protocol consists of having a system start at bath temperature $T_c$, equilibrating at temperature $T_h$, and then cooling again to $T_c$. Thus, a full thermal cycle is completed faster by a system that reaches a higher temperature.
    \item Santos tried to describe the Mpemba effect by solving the space-independent heat equation $\partial_t T = -\alpha_T (T(t-t_d)-T_c(t))$, for a system with memory~\cite{Santos2025}. Here, $t_d$ is a `delay time' that allows the system at time $t$ to depend on a past state and $T_c(t)$ is a time-dependent bath temperature. Santos showed that Newton's law of cooling for a system with memory could yield a Mpemba effect or a Kovacs effect with an appropriate choice for the cooling protocol $T(t)$. In particular, Santos considered a specific type of quench where $T_c$ decayed exponentially with a nonzero quench time $t_q$, $T_c(t)\propto \e^{-t/t_q}$.  Although Santos does not use any of the theoretical machinery Lu and Raz built to describe the Mpemba effect, the idea of a `quench function' is particularly interesting.
\end{enumerate}

Thus, we consider how the dynamics of our system change if the bath temperature slowly decays with time. Since the Mpemba effect is a feature of nonequilibrium systems, one would expect that a system that is sufficiently slowly quenched should not display the Mpemba effect. Similarly, a quench that is sufficiently fast would display the Mpemba effect in a system that displays a Mpemba effect with an instantaneous quench. This therefore motivates a search for the critical rate where the hot and warm systems reach equilibrium at almost the same time. More specifically, we find the supremum of quench rates for which a crossing exists. To find this requires that we create a new version of Kumar and Bechhoefer's experiment.

The na\"ive approach to add finite quench rates to Kumar and Bechhoefer's~\cite{Kumar2020} result is to simply make the diffusivity $D = \kB T_c/\gamma$ a function of time:
\begin{equation}
    D(t) = \frac{\kB T_c}{\gamma}h(t; T_0),
\end{equation}
where $h(t; T_0)$ is the dimensionless \textit{quench function}, the ratio of the bath temperature at time $t$ to the equilibrium temperature, $h(t; T_0) = T(t)/T_c$, given that the system started at temperature $T_0$. The quench function is always a function of the initial temperature $T_0$ at which the initial Boltzmann distribution is set to. For our purposes, the quench function must have the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $h(t; T_0)=T_0/T_c\ \forall\ t\leq 0$ (to ensure that the system is in equilibrium at temperature $T_0$ at $t=0$);
    \item $h(\infty; T_0) = 1$ (the bath must be fully quenched at a sufficiently large time);
    \item $\partial_t{h} \leq 0$ (the bath temperature strictly does not increase, otherwise it would not be a quench);\footnote{If we ignore this condition, we can obtain the quench protocol for the Kovacs effect by considering $h(t)$ that drops below one and then asymptotically rises~\cite{Militaru2021}.}
    \item $\partial_{T_0}{h} > 0$ if $h(t;T_0)>1$ (the quench function should take longer to reach one for higher $T_0$; this keeps us from using a quench function that itself displays the Mpemba effect).
\end{enumerate}

    \end{tikzpicture}
    \caption[Sketches of the Newtonian quench function $h_{\mathrm N}(t; T_0, \Lambda)$]{\small{Newtonian quench function $h_{\mathrm N}(t; T_0, \Lambda)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:newtonianQuench}). (a) The effect of varying $\Lambda$ (as a fraction of a constant protocol time $\tau$) with constant $T_0=10~T_c$. (b) The effect of varying $T_0$ with constant $\Lambda\tau=65$.}}\label{fig:newtonianQuench}
    % DONE rescale and relabel axes to $t/\tau$.
\end{figure}
We will use a quench function corresponding to Newtonian cooling, an exponential decay given by
\begin{equation}
    h_{\mathrm N}(t;T_0, \Lambda) = \begin{cases}
        \frac {T_0}{T_c},& t \leq 0\\
        \left( \frac{T_0}{T_c} - 1 \right)\e^{-\Lambda t} + 1,& t > 0
    \end{cases}.\label{eq:newtonianQuench}
\end{equation}
This quench function is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:newtonianQuench}. The quench rate $\Lambda$ here controls the rate of cooling. In general, since the defining properties of a quench function do not specify how quickly $h(t)$ approaches one, a parameter to define a cooling rate is useful. We require one more property of the parameter $\Lambda$ to ensure that it actually does function as a cooling rate:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[5.] $\partial_{\Lambda} h(t;T_0,\Lambda)<0$ (increasing $\Lambda$ makes $h$ smaller in the same time $t$).
\end{enumerate}

Note that the time dependence of $D(t)$ on the right-hand side of the Fokker-Planck equation invalidates the separation \textit{ansatz}, Eq.~\ref{eq:separationAnsatz}. We therefore cannot use Lu and Raz's condition to determine the existence of a Mpemba effect given a nonzero quench rate. We also note that as $\Lambda\rightarrow\infty$, $h_{\mathrm{N}}$ approaches the instantaneous quench,
\begin{equation}
    h_{\mathrm{I}}(t;T_0) = \begin{cases}
        \frac{T_0}{T_c},& t \leq 0\\
        1,& t > 0
    \end{cases}.\label{eq:instantaneousQuench}
\end{equation}
Similarly, as $\Lambda\rightarrow 0$, $h_{\mathrm{N}}$ approaches a \textit{quasistatic} quench, where
\begin{equation}
    h_{\mathrm{Q}}(t;T_0) = T_0/T_c. \label{eq:quasistaticQuench}
\end{equation}
The quench rate $\Lambda$ cannot be equal to zero, as this would violate our second assumption about convergence to steady state. However, for any nonzero $\Lambda$, the quench will decay to one after a sufficiently long time (even if this time is much greater than the protocol time $\tau$). We may view a quasistatic quench as one that allows the system to come to equilibrium at every instant in time. Since the Mpemba effect is a nonequilibrium phenomenon, no Mpemba effect can exist in this system. We illustrate the two `trivial' quenches (i.e., quench protocols where the bath temperature does not change) in dashed red and blue lines in Fig.~\ref{fig:assortedQuenches}. The Kovacs protocol, although not technically a quench, is illustrated in gray.

We can choose a potential that displays a Mpemba effect under an instantaneous quench ($\Lambda \gg 0$). Since no potential can display the Mpemba effect under a sufficiently slow quench ($\Lambda \rightarrow 0$), we deduce that some critical $\Lambda^*$ exists below which the Mpemba effect exists, and above which there is no Mpemba effect. 

If we applied this to the system Kumar and Bechhoefer used to demonstrate the strong Mpemba effect, we would, however, find major discrepancies. Recall the discrete-time Langevin equation~\ref{eq:discreteLangevin} has two terms: the deterministic term $F(x_n)\Delta t/\gamma$ and the stochastic term $\sqrt{2\kB T(t)\Delta t/\gamma}\xi_n$. For large $T$, the stochastic term can be much greater than the deterministic term; in that case, we may approximate Eq.~\ref{eq:discreteLangevin} as 
\begin{equation}
    x_{n+1} \approx \sqrt{2D\Delta t}~\xi_n,
\end{equation}
which is the expression for free diffusion. This means that particles are free to leave the box $\mathcal B$, and we can no longer approximate $U(x) \approx U_{\mathcal B}(x)$; consequently $p(x,0)$ is not close to the Boltzmann distribution of the potential we impose. This discrepancy can lead to experimental anomalies such as an observed increase in the distance function, even though it is always supposed to decrease with time. The reason for such an anomaly is that the conditions we outlined earlier implicitly assumed that probability currents at the boundaries of $\mathcal B$ vanish, an assumption that no longer holds if particles may escape the domain. In simulations, one can normally take care of this problem by setting appropriate boundary conditions, but there is no way to do this in an experiment. We thus need to find a new potential that displays the Mpemba effect without relying on a box.

We claimed earlier that the Newtonian quench, Eq.~\ref{eq:newtonianQuench}, was a natural choice for a quench function. But why this particular quench function, given that the Mpemba effect in our system relies on non-Newtonian cooling? We could, for example, choose a linear quench, 
\begin{equation}
    h_L(t; T_0, \Lambda) = \max(T_0/T_c(1-\Lambda t), 1)\label{eq:linearQuench}
\end{equation}
We will motivate our choice for a quench using a physical argument for how the bath might cool if it were actually cooling in a real medium. 

Consider a one-dimensional system living on top of an infinitely wide plate of height $h$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:quenchIntuition} that is at equilibrium at temperature $T_0$. The plate has thermal diffusivity $\alpha_T$ and thickness $\ell$. At $t=0$, the plate is placed atop the XY plane, which is a heat reservoir held at temperature $T_c$.\footnote{We may perhaps assume the thermal diffusivity of the plane $\alpha_T'\gg \alpha_T$ so that its temperature $T_c$ can be considered constant over the timescale of cooling.} Heat will then flow uniformly along the $z$ axis through the plate until the surface containing the system has cooled from temperature $T_0$ to temperature $T_c$. To find how the temperature of the plate evolves, we solve the heat equation with boundary conditions:
\begin{align}
    \pdv{T}{t} &= \alpha_T\pdv[2]{T}{z}\\
    T(0, t\geq 0) &= T_c\\
    T(z>0, 0) &= T_0\\
    \eval{\pdv{T}{z}}_{z=\ell} &= 0.
\end{align}
% \marginnote{\tiny{I'm using $T_0$ instead of $T_h$ because I use $T_0$ implies an arbitrary initial temperature rather than $T_h$, which more implies to me the existence of a warm temperature $T_w$}}
The first boundary condition enforces that the XY plane is a thermal reservoir. The second enforces the initial temperature of the system. The final boundary condition enforces that no heat flows beyond $z=\ell$: one may imagine that the entire setup lives inside a perfectly insulating medium. 

Solving, we find that 
\begin{align}
    T(z,t) &= T_c + \frac 2\ell (T_0-T_c)\sum_{n=0}^\infty \left[ \frac{\sin\left( \frac{(2n+1)\pi z}{2\ell} \right)}{\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{2\ell}} \right]\e^{-\frac{(2n+1)^2\pi^2 \alpha_T t}{4\ell^2}}.
\end{align}
Notice, when $t=0$, the series expansion is identical to the Fourier series of a square wave of height $T_0-T_c$, which represents the initial condition that the plate is at a uniform temperature $T_0$.

Defining
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda_{2n+1} = \frac{(2n+1)^2\pi^2 \alpha_T}{4\ell^2} = (2n+1)^2\Lambda_1,
\end{equation}
we see that the series can be represented as
\begin{align}
    T(z,t) &= T_c + \frac 2\ell (T_0-T_c) \left[ \frac{\sin\left( \pi z/2\ell\right)}{\pi/2\ell}\e^{-\Lambda_1 t} + \frac{\sin\left( 3\pi z/2\ell\right)}{3\pi/2\ell}\e^{-9\Lambda_1 t} +\dots\right]\\
    &= T_c + 2(T_0-T_c) \left[ \frac{\sin\left( \pi z/2\ell\right)}{\pi/2}\e^{-\Lambda_1 t} + \frac{\sin\left( 3\pi z/2\ell\right)}{3\pi/2}\e^{-9\Lambda_1 t} +\dots\right]
\end{align}
We evaluate this equation at $z=\ell$ to get the temperature of the system, $T(t)=T_ch(t)$:
\begin{align}
    T(\ell,t) -T_c &= \frac 4\pi (T_0-T_c) \left[ \sin\left( \pi /2\right)\e^{-\Lambda_1 t} + \frac 13\sin\left( 3\pi/2\right)\e^{-9\Lambda_1 t} +\dots\right]\\
    &= \frac 4\pi (T_0-T_c) \left[ \e^{-\Lambda_1 t} - \frac 13\e^{-9\Lambda_1 t} + \frac 15 \e^{-25\Lambda_1 t}-\dots\right].\label{eq:heatEqn}
\end{align}
The term in the brackets has the form of a series $x-x^9/3+x^{25}/5-\dots$. We note that for all $t\geq 0$, the ratio test reveals that the series is absolutely convergent for all times. We also note that at $t=0$, the series is identical to the Taylor series expansion of $\arctan(1)=\pi/4$, which cancels the prefactor. Since $\Lambda_3 = 9\Lambda_1$, the second term is expected to be much smaller than the first for sufficiently large times, so the series may be truncated to one term at sufficiently large times, yielding the expression
% \marginnote{\tiny Should I add a plot of $T(z,t)$ at different times?}
\begin{equation}
    T(\ell,t) -T_c \sim (T_0-T_c)\e^{-\Lambda_1 t}.
\end{equation}
Dividing throughout by $T_c$, we find
\begin{equation}
    h(t; T_0, \Lambda_1)-1 \sim (T_0/T_c -1) \e^{-\Lambda_1 t}.
\end{equation}
This scaling relation justifies the form of $h_{\mathrm N}(t; T_0, \Lambda)$ we used earlier. Since the parameter $\Lambda_1$ depends only on parameters such as the thermal diffusivity $\alpha_T$ and the plate thickness $\ell$, we may vary $\Lambda_1$ independently of the system eigenvalues $\lambda_n$, thus quenching the system at any speed we desire.

\section{Optical traps}

Optical traps, first described by Ashkin et al.\ in 1986, focus a laser beam to hold objects in place without physically touching them~\cite{Ashkin1986}. The working principle of an optical trap depends on the length scale, in particular the ratio of the length scale $L$ of the trapped object to the wavelength $\lambda$ of the laser~\cite{Jones2015}. 

For $L \gg \lambda$ (the \textit{ray optics regime}), an optical trap functions because of Newton's laws and the momentum of photons. As photons refract at the interface between media, their direction -- and therefore their momenta -- change. By Newton's second law, a change in the momentum of photons implies that a force acted on them, and by Newton's third law, the photons must also exert a reaction force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. The net reaction force is given by~\cite{Jones2015}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf F = \sum_{k} \left[ \frac{n_iP_i^{(k)}}{c}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_i^{(k)} - \frac{n_tP_{t,0}^{(k)}}{c}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{t,0}^{(k)} - \frac{n_iP_{t,1}^{(k)}}{c}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{t,1}^{(k)} + \dots\right],
\end{equation}
% \marginnote{\tiny{I put the stuff about gradient and scattering here instead of near the Rayleigh regime because the texts I reference do. Also, it is not obvious from the equation itself that the forces would on balance act as a restoring force}}
for a sphere of diameter $L$ and optical density $n_t$, with incident power of the $k^{\mathrm{th}}$ ray $P_i^{(k)}$, refracted power $P_{t,0}^{(k)}$, transmitted power (after the ray leaves the sphere) $P_{t,1}^{(k)}$, and other contributions from reflections and the reflected rays' refraction and further reflection. The unit vector $\hat{\mathbf r}_i^{(k)}$ and $\hat{\mathbf r}_t^{(k)}$ represents the direction the $k^{\mathrm{th}}$ incident and transmitted rays travel in. The net forces can be split into a \textit{scattering force}, which pushes the sphere away from the beam along the optical axis due to radiation pressure, and a \textit{gradient force}, which acts against the displacement of the sphere. For distances less than one particle radius from the trap centre, the reaction force always opposes the displacement of a sphere from the focus of a beam. 

For $L \ll \lambda$ (the \textit{Rayleigh regime}), light can be described as an electromagnetic wave and the sphere as a collection of dipoles. Solving Maxwell's equations for such a system yields~\cite{Jones2015}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf F_{\mathrm{DA}} = \underbrace{\frac 14 \Re(\alpha_d)\nabla \abs{\mathbf E}^2}_{\text{Gradient}} + \underbrace{\frac{2\pi\Im(\alpha_d)}{\lambda\epsilon_0c}\mathbf S}_{\text{Scattering}} - \underbrace{\frac {\pi c\Im(\alpha_d)}{\lambda\epsilon_0}\nabla\times\mathbf s_d}_{\text{Spin-curl}},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_d$ is the complex polarisability, $\mathbf E$ the electric field, $\mathbf S = \Re(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H)/2$ the Poynting vector, and $\mathbf s_d = i\epsilon_0\mathbf E\times\mathbf E^*/(2\omega)$ the time-averaged spin density. The spin-curl term, a non-conservative force arising from a non-homogeneous electric field polarisation, is usally small and can be ignored. Thus, we are left with a gradient and scattering term, as before. 

The systems we consider are \textit{mesoscopic}: the characteristic length, which is set by the diameter of the silica beads we trap, is $d \approx$ \qty{1.5}{\micro\metre}. The trapping laser has a wavelength $\lambda = $ \qty{0.532}{\micro\metre}. Since the radius of the beads is similar to the wavelength of the laser, neither the ray-optics regime nor the Rayleigh regime completely explains the behaviour of the bead. More complete theories such as Mie theory exactly solve the Maxwell equations with appropriate boundary conditions as an infinite series to completely explain the gradient and scattering forces~\cite[Ch.~5]{Jones2015}. 

In the experiments reported here, the gradient force exceeds the scattering force for small displacements\footnote{The scattering force is the reason the trap centre differs slightly from the focus of the beam. Since the scattering force is nonconservative, its existence suggests that the `particle in a potential' approximation we suggest here is not quite accurate. Roichman et al.~\cite{Roichman2008} showed that the scattering forces can generate steady-state toroidal probability currents in the probability density function of the position of the bead. However, as discussed in Ref.~\cite{Roichman2008}, the effects of scattering are usually small enough in setups such as those we discuss here that we may ignore them. }, and a potential that has the following properties is generated:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For \textit{small} displacements from the trap centre, the potential is approximately quadratic, behaving as a spring with force constant $k$.
    \item For \textit{very large} displacements from the trap centre, the beam does not pass through the sphere at all. Thus, the force must be zero.
\end{enumerate}
The precise form of $F(x)$ depends on the beam profile and the length regime; for a Gaussian beam in the Rayleigh regime, a simple dipole approximation of the bead yields~\cite{Harada1996}
\begin{equation}
    F(x, y=0, z=0)\propto -x\exp[-2(x/w_0)^2], 
\end{equation} 
$w_0$, called the `beam waist', is related to the width of the Gaussian beam. Thus, $U(x)\sim -\e^{-x^2}$. We plot a sketch of the trap potential in Fig.~\ref{fig:opticalTrapPotential}(a).

The theory we described requires potentials that are considerably more complicated than the quadratic potentials that optical traps impose. Prior work created double-well potentials using various tricks. Visscher et al.~\cite{Visscher1993} scanned a laser back and forth rapidly between two positions to generate a double-well potential in one dimension that could trap and manipulate \textit{E. coli} bacterial filaments. Mu et al.~\cite{Mu2010} used a $\pi$-phase plate to generate a double-well potential in magneto-optical tweezers to trap cold atoms. They found that errors in the phase difference in the phase sheets that made up the plate could alter the depths of the two wells. Tang et al.~\cite{Tang2021} used a spatial light modulator in a \textit{holographic} optical tweezer setup to generate asymmetric double-well potentials in two dimensions for nanoscale objects. However, the Mpemba effect as outlined here is very sensitive to parameters such as the tilt energy $\Delta E$. These parameters need to be controlled precisely and not be subject to large uncertainties. Moreover, the double-well potentials at mesoscopic scales that other techniques generate are limited. In time-shared traps, the barrier height and well distance cannot be independently controlled. If multiple traps or spatial light modulators are used to create a double-well potential, then diffraction limits the scale of the potential. Consequently, the distance between the two wells must be large: for example, Ginot et al.~\cite{Ginot2022} needed about \qty{640}{\nano\metre} between the two wells in a time-shared optical trap, whereas the separation between wells in our system will be closer to \qty{64}{\nano\metre}. Since diffusion times are proportional to the square of lengths, the change in scale implies that our equilibration times are \num{100} times faster. Experiments then take minutes instead of hours, reducing drifts that are a much more significant source of error. To conduct experiments at the fast timescales we require, the scale of the potential is often smaller than the wavelength of the laser by nearly an order of magnitude. Thus, we need more precise control over the forcefield an object experiences.

We therefore outline a \textit{feedback trap}, a device that allows one to generate precisely controlled arbitrary potentials.

% \marginnote{\tiny{DONE: Add sketch of feedback trap functioning}}
Suppose we could move the trap centre $X$ defined in the previous section arbitrarily quickly based on instantaneous measurements of the position of the bead, $x$. We will describe how we can actually move the trap centre in the next section. We will assume the trap centre is always close enough to the bead centre that the quadratic approximation of the potential is reasonably accurate. The force the trap exerts on the bead at any point in time is given by $F_m(x|X)=-k(x-X)$, where $k$ is the trap constant of the beam, determined by the beam waist $w_0$. Suppose we desire a potential $U(x)$ with a force profile $F(x)=-U'(x)$. Then we could equate the expression for $F_m$ and $F$ to obtain the expression $X(x) = x+F(x)/k$. Thus, at every time, the particle would actually experience a force of $F_m(x|X)$, as if it were experiencing a force $F(x)$. An example of how to move the trap to impose a double-well potential is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:feedbackTrap}. 

So far, we have implictly assumed that we can measure $x$ and change $X$ instantaneously. In real experiments, discrete measurements of the position $x_n$ are made at intervals $\Delta t$, the measurements are noisy, and the trap position $X_n(x_{n-1})$ depends on the previous measurement. Moreover, the feedback force the particle experiences is approximately constant\footnote{The feedback force is only approximately constant over the course of a timestep because of relaxation in the harmonic trap, which is not too significant for $\Delta t/t_r \ll 1$; see Kumar et al.~\cite{Kumar2018}.} until the next update. 
% \marginnote{\tiny{I'm using $\Delta t/t_r$ explicitly instead of defining the feedback constant as $\alpha$ because I already use $\alpha$ elsewhere to refer to the asymmetry parameter in Kumar and Bechhoefer (2020 Nature).}}

In this case, we call the potential the bead experiences a \textit{virtual potential}~\cite{Jun2012}. This method of trapping, where forces are dynamically imposed once per timestep rather than continuously with the changing position of the particle, is called \textit{active trapping}. Since any force can serve to provide feedback, a diverse set of active traps exist in the literature. Active trapping techniques include electrokinetic traps~\cite{Cohen2005}, hydrodynamic traps~\cite{Narayanamurthy2017}, acoustic traps~\cite{Melde2016}, opto-thermoelectric traps~\cite{Lin2018}, and optical traps~\cite{Kumar2018}. 

Gavrilov et al.~\cite{Gavrilov2014} used the feedback trap of Jun and Bechhoefer~\cite{Jun2012}, which in turn built on the work of Cohen et al.~\cite{Cohen2005}, and showed that the difference between the virtual potential and the true potential is insignificant if the feedback time $\Delta t \ll t_r$, where the relaxation time $t_r=\gamma/k$. We usually quantify the difference between the virtual and true potentials with $\Delta t/t_r$, and require $\Delta t/t_r\ll 1$. The approach outlined here yields two constraints on the form of the force $F(x)$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For an arbitrary force function $F_m$, the equation \begin{equation}
        F_m(x|X)=F(x)\label{eq:feedbackCondition}
    \end{equation} 
    must have solutions for $X$. If, as in a real optical trap, $|F_m|$ were upper-bounded by a maximum force $F_{\max}$, Eq.~\ref{eq:feedbackCondition} would have no solutions for any $|F(x)|>F_{\max}$. Thus, we must restrict the codomain of $F(x)$ to $[-F_{\max}, F_{\max}]$. 
    \item The condition $\Delta t/t_r\ll 1$ constrains on the second derivative of the potential $U(x)$. If we express the force $F(x)$ in the general form $k(x)x$, with $k(x)=-F(x)/x$ an arbitrary function that we can always construct for $x\neq 0$, we know the feedback will only work if $\abs{k(x)}\Delta t/\gamma \ll 1$. At worst, $k_{\max}\Delta t/\gamma \ll 1$, where $k_{\max} = k(x^*)$ for $\partial_x k(x^*)=0$. To find the maximum value of $k$, we set its derivative equal to zero and solve:
    \begin{align}
        \pdv{x} k(x^*) &= -\frac{1}{x^*}\eval{\pdv{F}{x}}_{x^*} + \frac{F(x^*)}{(x^*)^2} =0\\
        \therefore\ \eval{\pdv{F}{x} }_{x^*} &= \frac{F(x^*)}{x^*}\\
        &=-k(x^*).
    \end{align}
    Consequently, $k_{\max} = -\partial_x F(x^*) = \abs{\partial_x^2 U(x^*)}$. Then $k_{\max}\ll \gamma/\Delta t$ implies
    \begin{equation}
        \abs{\partial_x^2 U} \ll \gamma/\Delta t.
    \end{equation}
    The feedback constraint can thus only be satisfied if $\partial_x^2 U$ is not too high.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Optical feedback traps}
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{{./Figures/chapter3/opticalFeedbackTrap/diagram.pdf}}

Virtual potentials using feedback traps were first realised in microscopic systems by Cohen and Moerner with their `Anti-Brownian ELectrokinetic' (ABEL) trap to cancel the Brownian motion of a trapped Brownian object~\cite{Cohen2005, Cohen2006}.

% \marginnote{\tiny{DONE: add manufacturer/model numbers for all components}}
Jun and Bechhoefer extended their results to generate near-arbitrary virtual potentials with an accuracy and stability sufficient for thermodynamic measurements on single colloidal particles~\cite{Jun2012}. Their feedback used electric forces: two electrodes were connected across a sample cell filled with a dilute colloid of \qty{200}{\nano\metre} fluorescent beads in water. Feedback was enabled with a camera observing the trapped bead and an image-processing algorithm to determine the required feedback force. However, since the system used electrodes to generate forces, there were nontrivial electrochemical effects that created large drifts. Moreover, the feedback time of \qty{10}{\milli\second} limited the performance of their trap. 

To address these issues, Kumar and Bechhoefer built an \textit{optical} feedback trap using an optical trap to generate the force, a quadrant photodiode (QPD, First Sensor, QP50-6-18u-SD2) for measuring the two-dimensional position, and a Field-Programmable Gate Array (FPGA, National Instruments, NI PCIe-7857) for computing the feedback force, implemented using an acousto-optical deflector (DTSXY-250-532, AA
Opto Electronic) to move the beam by very small angles~\cite{Kumar2018a}.\footnote{AODs are crystals that can deflect the first-order diffraction peak of a laser beam by different angles depending on the frequency of the soundwave passed through it.} The technique was independently developed in the same year by Albay et al.~\cite{Albay2018}. We present a simplified schematic of the optical feedback trap setup in Fig.~\ref{fig:opticalFeedbackTrap}, omitting the various mirrors and lenses that we use to align, collimate, and focus the two laser beams. The experiment we outline here was originally set up by Kumar~\cite{Kumar2022a}, modified by Saha~\cite{Saha2023a}, and set up in the configuration outlined in Fig.~\ref{fig:opticalFeedbackTrap} by Basak~\cite{Basak2026}. The experimental work presented in this thesis largely involves the software that controls the experiment and the software that processes the data and does not modify the hardware. Two lasers are used in the setup: a high-power (\qty{500}{\milli\watt}) \qty{532}{\nano\metre} \textit{trapping laser} (HUBNER Photonics,
Cobolt Samba, max. power: \qty{1.5}{\watt}) to trap a Brownian particle, a \qty{1.5}{\micro\metre} (coefficient of variation $\approx$ \qtyrange{10}{15}{\percent}) silica bead in water, and a low-power ($\approx$ \qty{2.5}{\milli\watt}) \qty{660}{\nano\metre} \textit{detection laser} (Thorlabs, LP660-SF50), which is deflected by the bead and falls on the QPD. Dichroic filters (Edmund optics) ensure that the lasers' respective paths are appropriately contained. Additionally, a blue LED is used to illuminate the sample, so that a microscope camera (FLIR BFS-U3-04S2M-CS) can view the setup, although the camera output is not used in the feedback loop. The FPGA is a circuit made of programmable logic gates, which allows for extremely fast execution of code, with a feedback latency of \qty{10}{\micro\second} (i.e., loop rate of \qty{100}{\kilo\hertz}). We program the FPGA using LabVIEW's FPGA module. 

Because of the maximum force constraint on optical trap forces, we must restrict the target force to $[-F_{\max}, F_{\max}]$, as we did in Ch.~2. In practice, we choose $F_{\max}$ to be a value where the quadratic approximation of the trap potential is accurate\footnote{Slight deviations from quadraticity can also be corrected for with the calibration steps we outline here.}, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:nonlinearCalibration}.

\subsection{Sample preparation and trapping}
A sample is prepared using colloidal beads in a solution of water and sodium azide. Sodium azide kills bacteria, which may otherwise attach themselves to beads and cause spurious trap calibration. A sample cell is prepared using spacer wires about \qty{100}{\micro\metre} in diameter and a coverslip (\qtyproduct{18 x 18}{\milli\metre}) on a microslide (\qtyproduct{75 x 25}{\milli\metre}), using nail polish to ensure a waterproof seal. Imperfect sealing leads to evaporation, which will cause drifts that manifest as $1/f$ noise in the power spectrum of a trapped bead (see below). Roughly \qty{35}{\micro\litre} of the colloid is pipetted into the cell, ensuring no air bubbles remain. The sample cell may then be loaded into the trap, set between the two objectives. 

The sample cell is moved by an XYZ stage (DS25-XYZ, Newport) until the focal point of the trapping laser lies inside the sample cell, just above the coverslip. A bead may then be trapped. The camera helps both to find the focus -- using the interference pattern the laser makes on the coverslip -- and to find a bead to trap. Only a single bead should be trapped. The stage is then adjusted in the $z$ direction so that the bead is well above the coverslip, to reduce the effect of vertical fluctuations in the horizontal diffusivity, an effect that has a magnitude of about $h/d$ where $h$ is the distance above the coverslip and $d$ the bead diameter~\cite{Sharma2010}. The bead concentration should be low enough that the probability for another bead to fall into the trap over the duration of an experiment is small but also high enough that finding a bead is not too difficult. The dilution of a new sample is about \qty{50}{\micro\litre} of the master solution in \qtyrange{1}{1.5}{\milli\litre} of \qty{0.8}{\molar} aqueous solution of sodium azide, but the dilution is adjusted to limit the number of beads in the field of view at any given time. Ideally, the field of view should contain only one bead. 

Since the sample cell is placed vertically, we define a coordinate system with the $z$ axis along the beam path and the $y$ axis aligned with the direction of gravity. The transverse direction $x$ is used for all experiments in this thesis.

The QPD is a device with four photodiodes with output voltages $V_{\mathrm I}$, $V_{\mathrm{II}}$, $V_{\mathrm{III}}$, and $V_{\mathrm{IV}}$ that outputs three signals: 
\begin{align}
    V_x &= V_{\mathrm{I}}+V_{\mathrm{IV}}-(V_{\mathrm{II}}+V_{\mathrm{III}})\nonumber \\
    V_y &= V_{\mathrm{I}}+V_{\mathrm{II}}-(V_{\mathrm{III}}+V_{\mathrm{IV}})\nonumber \\
    V_{\mathrm{sum}} &= V_{\mathrm{I}}+V_{\mathrm{II}}+V_{\mathrm{III}}+V_{\mathrm{IV}}.\label{eq:QPDvoltages}
\end{align}
We first block the QPD and find the dark noise $\ev{V_x}$, $\ev{V_y}$, and $\ev{V_{\mathrm{sum}}}$ and subtract these values from the QPD signal to get $\Delta V_x$, $\Delta V_y$, and $\Delta V_{\mathrm{sum}}$. We then scale the responses to $s_x=\Delta V_x/\Delta V_{\mathrm{sum}}$ and $s_y=\Delta V_y/\Delta V_{\mathrm{sum}}$ to compensate for variations in the intensity of the diode laser.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{{./Figures/chapter3/qpd/qpd.pdf}}
    \caption[Schematic diagram of a QPD]{\small{A schematic diagram of a QPD, illustrating the four voltage outputs. The three signals the circuit outputs are calculated using Eqs.~\ref{eq:QPDvoltages}}}
\end{figure}

The AOD is a crystal that deflects the first-order diffraction mode of the laser refracting through it by a small angle that depends on the frequency of a sound wave passed through the crystal, perpendicular to the direction of incidence of the laser. Consequently, the deflection produced by the AOD, as well as the response of the QPD, are not linear. Also, as we discussed earlier, the harmonic approximation for the potential is imperfect, although this effect is small compared to the nonlinearity of the QPD response. However, a single linearisation step can compensate for all these issues. The AOD is given a triangle wave voltage input signal, $V_{\mathrm{in}}(t)$, moving the bead along a trajectory that should everywhere be piecewise linear in time. Since the response of the bead $x(t)$ is in general not linear with $V_{\mathrm{in}}$, the transfer function must be computed and estimated. We first find $\delta V_{\mathrm{in}}$ as a function of $\delta s_x$. Here, we express the deviation $\delta q$ of some quantity $q$ as $\delta q = q-\ev{q}$.\footnote{Since $V_{\mathrm{in}}$ is a voltage signal with an offset of \qty{5}{\volt}; the \textit{deviation} of $V_{\mathrm{in}}$ is more relevant to computing the response. In principle, other measured quantities should have a mean of zero as well, but we subtract the average to account for possible drifts over time.} We then fit $\delta V_{\mathrm{in}}(\delta s_x)$ to a fifth-order polynomial $\delta V_{\mathrm{in}}(t)\sim P_5(\delta s_x(t))$. The nonlinear fit only works if the amplitude of the signal $V_{\mathrm{in}}$ is chosen so that $\delta V_{\mathrm{in}}$ is injective in the response $s_x$. Injectivity would allow for a simple function to relate $\delta V_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $\delta s_x$ that we can approximate with a polynomial. For our experiments, the maximum amplitude happens to be around \qtyrange{0.10}{0.15}{\volt}. If the input amplitude is too large, the peaks of $V_{\mathrm{in}}$ will correlate with a drop in $s_x(t)$ as the bead leaves the area through which the detection laser passes. It is also important that the response $s_x(t)$ be symmetric about the $y$ axis with zero offset: if not, the alignment mirrors need to be slightly adjusted. The signal is also multiplied by a calibration constant $C'$ converting the scaled voltage output of the QPD to a length unit, $x=C'P_5(\delta s_x)$. The precise length unit does not matter since every quantity will be scaled by the same reference length; thus, the length unit is chosen for convenience to be the distance between the two wells when $\Delta E=0$, $x_s = 2x_w\approx$ \SI{59.5(0.1)}{\nano\metre} in length; $C'\approx 67x_s$. Provided no optical components are moved between experiments, it is unnecessary to find the voltage-to-length factor during each experiment. The physical meaning of $P_5(s) = C_0+C_1s+C_2s^2+C_3s^3+C_4s^4+C_5s^5$ is unimportant, as it only ensures that voltage measurements have a fixed correspondence with length measurements; consequently, some of the coefficients of the polynomial may change significantly between different calibrations. As such, the polynomial fit will safely interpolate $\delta s_x$ values between $\delta V_{\mathrm{in}}$ between $[-0.1,+0.1]$ \unit{\volt} but should not be used for extrapolation. The position $x$ now varies linearly with $V_{\mathrm{in}}$ and thus we can safely use the results from Section 3.2. As we perform the fit, we pay particular attention to the area around $s_x=0$; the particle spends most of the time close to the origin, so it is important that the calibration be its best near the origin. Once calibration is complete, the segments of the signal should look like sharp peaks and valleys, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:nonlinearCalibration}. If the peaks are not sharp enough, the calibration is repeated. 

\subsection{Measurement of trap constant and diffusivity}

In the toy model of a feedback trap, we assumed that the force constant $k$ was known: this is essential to knowing precisely how far to move the trap centre in order to apply a desired force. However, we do not know what the trap constant is in the scaled and calibrated length units. Furthermore, knowledge of the diffusivity is also necessary, since the energies associated with the virtual potential are scaled with $\kB T_c$, which is proportional to $D$. Since units are chosen so that $\kB T_c := 1$, finding $D$ amounts to finding $1/\gamma$. Recalling that $k/\gamma = t_r$, and defining a \textit{corner frequency} $f_c = 1/t_r$, we may then find the corner frequency, as well as the trap constant in order to determine the diffusivity.

To measure $k$ and $\gamma$, we record the trajectory of the bead over \qtyrange{20}{30}{\second}. The trajectory, $x(t)$, is expected to be an \textit{Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process}~\cite{Risken1996}. We may thus extract the trap constant in three separate ways:

\subsubsection{Variance estimation}
By the equipartition theorem, the variance $\sigma_x^2$ of a particle in thermal equilibrium at temperature $T_c$ is
\begin{equation}
    E = \ev{\frac 12 kx^2} = \frac 12 k\sigma_x^2 = \frac 12 \kB T_c.\label{eq:equipartition}
\end{equation}
Thus, the trap constant is estimated to be $k = \kB T_c/\sigma_x^2$. However, the observed position variance combines measurement noise of the QPD as well as fluctuations in the particle's motion, and thus the trap constant estimated using this technique is somewhat flawed. In practice, the position $\bar x$ we measure is the sum of the true position $x$ and a measurement noise $\zeta\sim\mathcal N[0,\sigma_m]$: $\bar x=x+\zeta$. The variance we measure is then the sum of variances, $\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}^2 = \sigma_x^2+\sigma_m^2$, which leads to a biased estimate of $k$ unless $\sigma_m$ is known.

\subsubsection{Probability densities}
If the trap force is assumed to be quadratic, $U=kx^2/2$, the equilibrium probability density 
\begin{align}
    \pi(x; T_c) &= \frac 1Z\exp[-\frac{kx^2}{2\kB T_c}]\\ \label{eq:harmonicEquilibrium}
    kx^2/2
    &= -\ln{Z}-\ln[\pi_c(x)]
\end{align}
Thus, computing the empirical probability density from the trajectory $x(t)$ and fitting its logarithm over the range where a quadratic fit is good will yield estimates of $k$. This method also leads to biased estimates of $k$ due to measurement noise.

\subsubsection{Power spectral density estimation}
The power spectral density (PSD) of the particle's trajectory is given by
\begin{equation}
    S_n = \frac 1\tau\ev{\hat x_n^2},
\end{equation}
where $\hat x_n$ denotes the Fourier transform of $x(t)$, and the index $n$ implies that the discrete Fourier transform is used, evaluated at $f_n=n/(N\Delta t)$ for sampling time $\Delta t=$ \qty{10}{\micro\second} and $N$ the number of samples of the trajectory $x(t)$. Note that the fast Fourier transform returns $N$ values of $\hat x_n$. For an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process for a particle in a trap with stiffness $k$, friction coefficient $\gamma$, and diffusivity $D$, the PSD is given by a Lorentzian~\cite{BergSoerensen2004},
\begin{equation}
    S(f) = \frac 1{2\pi^2}\frac{D}{f_c^2+f^2} + S_m,
\end{equation}
where the corner frequency $\omega_c = 2\pi f_c= k/\gamma = 1/t_r$. Here, $S_m$ is the measurement noise power spectral density which is constant for white noise. However, since there is a finite sampling frequency, the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem applies and thus the signal is aliased at or above $f_s/2 = 1/(2\Delta t) = $\qty{50}{\kilo\hertz}~\cite{Press2007}. The formula must therefore be modified to account for aliasing. By using the generating function of a discrete-time Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process $x_{n+1}=cx_n+(D\Delta t/(2\pi f_c))^{1/2}\eta_n$, one finds~\cite{BergSoerensen2004}
\begin{equation}
    S_n = \frac 1{2\pi f_c}\frac{(1-c^2)D\Delta t}{1+c^2 - 2c\cos(2\pi n/N)} + \bar S_m,~\label{eq:aliasedLorentzian}
\end{equation}
% TODO: explain Z-transform
with $c=\e^{-2\pi f_c/f_s}$ and $\bar S_m = \int S_m\dd{f}$ over a frequency interval $f_s$. One can easily show that $S_n\rightarrow S(f)$ as $\Delta t\rightarrow 0$.

The power spectral density is calculated from the discrete position data using \textit{Bartlett's method}: the data (consisting of $N$ position measurements) are divided into $n=20$ nonoverlapping segments, each of size $\lfloor N/n\rfloor$~\cite{Press2007}. 

We then fit the PSD to Eq.~\ref{eq:aliasedLorentzian}. The parameters we obtain are multiplied by a factor of $n/(n-2)$ in order to correct for the bias linked to the number of degrees of freedom~\cite{BergSoerensen2004}. 

Adding measurement noise to the expression for the PSD only shifts up the PSD by a constant, $S_m$. Thus, measurement noise can be estimated by simply adding a constant to the fit. This fact makes the PSD more reliable than the other two techniques outlined here, where it is not possible to estimate the measurement noise through the data alone, without resorting to other tools such as the autocorrelation function.

The aliased Lorentzian is the most accurate way of estimating the trap constant, and we therefore use the estimates we get from fitting the aliased Lorentzian as parameters in the software. At low frequencies, there is generally excess noise, often referred to as $1/f$ noise~\cite{Weissman1988}. The cause of such a discrepancy may be sample contamination, improper sealing, or accidentally trapping more than one bead. In practice, we fit only a section of the PSD to Eq.~\ref{eq:aliasedLorentzian} to avoid the region with significant $1/f$ noise. The $1/f$ noise is negligible for frequencies above some frequency $f^*$ (roughly \qty{1}{\hertz} in Fig.~\ref{fig:trapConstantInference}) where the $1/f$ noise amplitude equals the thermal spectrum of the bead's motion. If $f^*<1/\tau_{\mathrm{tot}}$ for the maximum single-trajectory time $\tau_{\mathrm{tot}}=$ \qty{150}{\milli\second}, the noise can usually be corrected by subtracting the offset from the trajectory.

\subsection{Drift correction}
Over the course of the protocol, the low-frequency noise discussed above can lead to a beam offset. The mean position of the bead increases or decreases over periods of time much greater than the protocol time $\tau$. If the drift is too large, the bead will leave the region of sensitivity of the detector laser; this cannot be corrected for with software. However, if drifts are small during a single relaxation protocol, we can correct for them by finding the mean of the particle's position when it experiences no feedback and subtracting the mean from all position readings for the rest of the protocol. Over the \qty{50}{\milli\second} this part of the protocol takes, the average is computed from position readings using the recursive algorithm $\mathtt{xavg}_{n+1} = (n\cdot\mathtt{xavg}_n + x_n)/(n+1)$, logged, and subtracted~\cite[Eq.~8.27]{Bechhoefer2021}.

In previous versions of the experiment, the target initial distribution function $\pi(x_0;T_0)$ was sampled using the transformation method~\cite[Sec.~7.3.2]{Press2007}. A cumulative density function computed from the Boltzmann distribution of the potential for initial temperature $T_0$ is first loaded into the memory of the FPGA. The CDF $\Pi(x;T_0)$ is evaluated over the domain $\mathcal B$ for a mesh size of \num{512} and loaded to the FPGA as an array, $\mathtt{CDF}$. A uniformly distributed random number $r\sim\mathcal U[0,1]$ is first generated. We then find the index $n$ for which $\mathtt{CDF}_n < r \leq \mathtt{CDF}_{n+1}$ using bisection search. These values correspond to the entries $\mathtt{x}_n$ and $\mathtt x_{n+1}$ respectively. We then use linear interpolation to approximately solve for $x_0\in[\mathtt x_n, \mathtt x_{n+1}]$:
\begin{equation}
    x_0 = \frac{\mathtt x_{n+1}-\mathtt x_n}{\mathtt{CDF}_{n+1}-\mathtt{CDF}_n}(r-\mathtt{CDF}_n)+\mathtt{x}_n.
\end{equation}
As we rely on linear interpolation to estimate $x$, the accuracy of the position chosen will depend on the size of the mesh used to generate $\mathtt{CDF}_n$. A higher mesh size will cause the initial distribution to be closer to the target distribution $\pi(x_0;T_0)$. Since the memory on the FPGA is limited, the mesh size must be capped (to 512 in this work). 

Once $x_0$ is selected, the bead needs to be positioned at $x=x_0$. The trap centre is moved to $x_0$, and the stiffness is increased to a larger value $k'$ so that the bead moves to the new position faster. The particle is allowed to equilibrate in this position for \qty{30}{\milli\second} before the next part of the protocol is active.

However, we found this method to be inaccurate. If the trap stiffness is not infinite, the bead would occupy a position normally distributed with mean $x_0$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{\kB T_c/k'}$. Consequently, the initial probability density would not be $\pi(x; T_0)$ but actually $\pi_0*\mathcal N[x_0, \sqrt{\kB T_c/k'}]$, where $*$ denotes convolution:
\begin{equation}
    (p*q)(x) = \int_{\mathbb R} p(y)q(x-y)\dd{y}.
\end{equation}
The effect of the nonzero standard deviation would be to slightly flatten out the initial distribution (see Fig.~\ref{fig:flattenedPDFs}). The effect is particularly noticeable when $T_0$ is low.

To avoid the flattening, we instead use a first-passage approach. A small value $\epsilon$ is chosen, and the trap centre is moved to $x_0$. When the position of the bead is $x \in [x_0-\epsilon, x_0+\epsilon]$, the bead is `released' and the next part of the protocol begins. Choosing $\epsilon$ is a tradeoff between precision and time: if $\epsilon$ is too low, the bead is unlikely to fall into the domain within the maximum number of timesteps allocated for this purpose; if it is too high, the initial PDFs will be distorted by the aforementioned convolution. In general, we pick $\epsilon \approx \sigma_m$, the measurement noise. The variance in $x_0$ is thus at least $2\sigma_m^2$.

\subsection{Protocol}
The feedback force $F(x)$ is now applied for the remainder of the protocol. \section{Data analysis}
The data we have collected is one very long trajectory with several columns.
Throughout the protocol, we record the following variables:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Position}: the position of the bead, in units of $x_s$.
    \item \textit{Time}: the current loop number. Previous versions of the experiment directly recorded the time in seconds, but since we use 32-bit floating point numbers to represent data, floating point error is not insignificant.
    \item \textit{Drift}: the recursively averaged position while the protocol is in the harmonic well calibration state.
    \item \textit{Force}: the force in $k_BT_c/x_s$ sent to the AOD. This is useful for checking whether the force is accurate.
    \item \textit{$x_0$} or \textit{$\eta$}: the initial position found by inverse transform sampling if we use the first-passage technique, or the noise at each time if we use a noisy potential to determine the initial position. If we use first-passage methods, this variable is useful for checking whether inverse transform sampling is working correctly; if we use noise, this variable is useful for checking if the noise force is properly distributed.
    \item \textit{State}: an integer indicating the current status. We need this variable to ensure that any analysis routines can separate the protocol from the calibration and positioning steps.
    \item \textit{Temperature}: an integer indicating which initial temperature we are using. This variable was unnecessary in previous iterations of this experiment as we would take separate datasets for each temperature, but we found that interweaving the temperature data together minimised the risk that drifts would affect one dataset but not the others.
\end{enumerate}
The data takes the form of one very long trajectory. To run analysis on it, we need to split the data into `chunks' with a more readable matrix-like format. The \texttt{Xarray} library in Python is particularly helpful in this regard.\footnote{\texttt{Xarray.DataArrays} and \texttt{Xarray.Datasets} are multidimensional spreadsheet-like objects labelled by `dimensions', of which there are three: $T$, the temperature; $n$, the ensemble index; and $t$, the time. These are often used by climate scientists to track multiple variables like temperature, pressure, etc. at various geographical coordinates over time.} After the data are properly formatted, it is saved to disk for later use. The raw output of the optical trap is quite large, containing redundant information and variables that we do not need after the first processing steps and is therefore slow to load; preprocessing the data thus makes analysis much more convenient. Because simulations use the same data structure to store their output, the analysis pipeline for simulations and data is identical. We histogram the data using a custom \texttt{Numba}-optimised function designed for the data format used to compute the empirical PDF and calculate the distance function at every point in time. 

We always take the distance function with respect to the empirical steady-state PDF, which is calculated by histogramming all or most\footnote{We only use the data from the times where $p_c(x,t)\approx \pi(x;T_c)$. If this is not so, the distance curve representing the evolution of the initially `cold' system will meaningfully change over the protocol duration. We exclude these times \textit{post-hoc} and recompute the distance in this case, although the existence of such a change in the distance curve implies errors in the positioning code that need to be fixed.} of the data at $T_0=T_c$ into one single low-noise histogram, rather than the PDF calculated from the Boltzmann distribution. In practice, we find that Mpemba crossings are more apparent when we use this technique, which compensates for systematic errors in creating the potential.

Once the analysis is complete, the quality of the data is easily assessed by looking at the distance curve for $T_0=T_c$. If the forces are accurately computed, the distance will not change significantly beyond noise.\footnote{To use the KL divergence instead of the $\mathcal L_1$ distance, we note that computing the KL divergence with empirical PDFs can be tricky, as bins of $p_2$ with zero counts will cause $\mathscr D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ to diverge. To avoid such divergences, we add a `pseudocount' to each empty bin and renormalise the PDF~\cite[Sec.~3.6]{Jurafsky2009}. If, instead, $p_1(x_i)=0$ has any zero counts, we define $p_1(x_i)\log(p_1(x_i)/p_2(x_i)):=0$. Often while computing the KL divergence numerically, expressing the integrand of Eq.~\ref{eq:KLdefinition} as $p_1\log(p_1)-p_1\log(p_2)$ instead of using a fraction in the log avoids a number of computational bugs.} The first-passage approach often displays a phenomenon where the $T_0=T_c$ curve very slightly decreases during the first few milliseconds after the quench, indicating either that the trap calibration was inaccurate or that drifts were significant over the course of the protocol.

\section{Computing solutions to the Fokker-Planck equation}
In order to identify whether a potential has an $a_2$ coefficient that evolves non-monotonically with temperature, we must find the $a_2$ coefficient at various temperatures and solve the Fokker-Planck equation. Standard finite-difference techniques will not do for this purpose, as they do not conserve probability or enforce positivity. Instead, we discretise the Fokker-Planck operator into a $N_m\times N_m$ $\mathbb W$-matrix, following Grima and Newman's technique~\cite{Grima2004}: 
\begin{align}
    \mathbb W_{j,j+1}(t) &= \frac{D(t)}{\Delta x^2}\exp[\frac{U(x_{j+1})-U(x_j)}{2\kB T(t)}]\\
    \mathbb W_{j,j-1}(t) &= \frac{D(t)}{\Delta x^2}\exp[\frac{U(x_{j-1})-U(x_j)}{2\kB T(t)}]\\
    \mathbb W_{j,j} &= -\mathbb W_{j,j+1}-\mathbb W_{j,j-1}\\
    \mathbb W_{1,0} = \mathbb W_{N_m,N_m+1} &= 0\\
    x_j &= x_{\min}+j\Delta x\\
    \Delta x &= \frac{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}{N_m}\label{eq:GrimaNewman}
\end{align}
Here, $\mathbb W_{i,j}$ is the matrix element corresponding to the $(i,j)^{\mathrm{th}}$ matrix element and $N_m$ is the discretisation size of the domain $[x_{\max}, x_{\min}]$. Grima and Newman argued that their approach accounts for an important curvature term that the finite-difference method neglects, and thereby conserves probability. 

We may now use standard numerical libraries to find eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the $\mathbb{W}$-matrix, treating $D(t) = \kB T(t)/\gamma$ as constant, and use Eq.~\ref{eq:an} to find $a_2(T)$. We can also use the discretised $\mathbb W$-matrix to solve the Fokker-Planck equation: expressing $p_n(t) = p(n\Delta x, t)$, we can solve for an arbitrary time using the Euler method $p_n(t+\Delta t) \approx p_n(t)+\sum_{m}\mathbb W_{n,m}p_{m}\Delta t$ or fourth-order Runge-Kutta methods to solve for $p(x,t)$~\cite{Press2007}.\footnote{The success of the numerical methods described here depends heavily on the choice of time discretisation, $\Delta t$, and often requires very small values of $\Delta t$ to ensure that the probability remains properly normalised throughout. Further work, not within the scope of this thesis, could greatly improve efficiency by discretising space and time with adaptive mesh sizes.} Since $\mathbb W$ is tridiagonal, it is fastest to explicitly compute the matrix-vector product manually in a loop, which can later be optimised by a Python compiler such as \texttt{Numba}:
\begin{align*}
    p_1(t+\Delta t) &= p_1(t) + [\mathbb W_{1,2}(t)p_2-\mathbb W_{2,1}(t)p_2]\Delta t\\
    p_k(t+\Delta t) &= p_k(t) + [\mathbb W_{k,k+1}(t)p_{k+1}-(\mathbb W_{k-1,k}(t)+\mathbb W_{k+1,k}(t))p_k + \mathbb W_{k,k-1}(t)p_{k-1}]\Delta t,\\ &\quad 2\leq k \leq N_m-1\\
    p_{N_m}(t+\Delta t) &= p_{N_m}(t) + [\mathbb W_{N_m,N_m-1}(t)p_{N_m-1}-\mathbb W_{N_m-1,N_m}(t)p_{N_m}]\Delta t.\label{eq:sparseFPE}
\end{align*}
However, since $N_m=\order{1000}$, standard numerical algorithms to compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors are fast enough for our purposes. It is also useful for the sake of efficiency to precompute
\begin{align}
    S_{n,n+1} &= \exp[\frac{U(x_{n+1})-U(x_n)}{2\kB T_c}]\\
    S_{n,n-1} &= \exp[\frac{U(x_{n-1})-U(x_n)}{2\kB T_c}].
\end{align}
and then calculate
\begin{align}
    \mathbb W_{n,m}(t) &= \frac{D_ch(t)}{\Delta x^2}(S_{n,m})^{\frac 1{h(t)}},
\end{align}
in each loop, not wasting work computing the diagonal term since Eq.~\ref{eq:sparseFPE} only requires the $\mathbb W_{n,n-1}$ and $\mathbb W_{n,n+1}$ terms.

The curves shown for the harmonic and quartic potentials in Figs.~\ref{fig:mpembaFPE} and \ref{fig:mpembaLangevin} were computed with this technique.

The technique outlined in Ch.~3 for choosing a high effective initial temperature was to manually position a particle and then release it. The technique is subject to errors arising from measurement noise and the tolerance we set for the initial position. Moreover, we cannot extend the technique to noninstantaneous quenches. In this section, we outline an alternative approach: we raise the particle's diffusivity by adding artificially generated noise to the force at each timestep, a technique pioneered by Martinez et al.~\cite{Martinez2013}. Later, Chupeau et al.~\cite{Chupeau2018} used a similar technique to achieve more rapid equilibration in a quadratic potential by increasing the diffusivity. We may use this technique to both ensure the initial distribution has the desired temperature and also to implement finite quenches. We impose the double-well force $F(x)$ but also add a noise force $\eta$ to the force, whose standard deviation scales with $\sqrt{\kB (T(t)-T_c)}$ for desired temperature $T(t)=T_ch(t)$ at time $t$. In experiments, the net effect of this is to replace the positioning step with a step where the particle diffuses in the same potential until it equilibrates but with a much higher diffusivity.

The effect of these `noise temperatures' imposed by programmatically generated noise forces on the Langevin dynamics of a particle is explored in a recent preprint by Basak et al.~\cite{Basak2026}; indeed, we may also use these precise dynamics to vary the diffusivity with time over the course of the protocol. In other words, we define a \textit{calibration} domain $\mathcal B' \subset \mathbb R$ such that

To identify candidate potentials, we perform a number of tests before running a simulation. The Mpemba effect exists only if the $a_2$ coefficient associated with the potential is nonmonotonic. However, even potentials with nonmonotonic $a_2$ coefficients may not display the Mpemba effect prominently enough to resolve the crossing of distance curves. Thus, the first criterion is to choose only potentials that demonstrate a strong Mpemba effect, because exponentially faster cooling accentuates the Mpemba effect. We therefore search for potentials where $a_2(T_h) = 0$ for some sufficiently small $T_h$. The asymmetric double-well potential fails to satisfy these criteria, as $a_2(T)$ is monotonic unless computed over a finite domain, the box $\mathcal B$. We can satisfy the criterion simply by checking whether there exists $T^*>T_c$ such that $a_2(T^*)=0$ for some sufficiently low nonzero $T^*$. 

Secondly, we will also choose $T_w$ to maximise $a_2(T_w)$. While not strictly necessary, ensuring that the second component of the eigenfunction expansion is as large as possible will make the difference between the two distance curves most prominent. Thus, we try to
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maximise $\int_{\mathcal B'} \pi(x;T_h)\dd{x}$ for a calibration domain $\mathcal B' \approx [-6x_s,+6x_s]$, the region over which the nonlinear calibration we performed is accurate.
    \item Minimise $T_h>T_c$ for which $a_2(T_h)=0$;
    \item Maximise $a_2(T_w)$, where $T_c<T_w<T_h$.
\end{itemize}

To modify the potential used previously, we altered slightly the expression for the strong Mpemba effect that we discussed previously, $\Pi_R(T_h)/\Pi_R(T_c)=1$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:VuceljaMpemba}). Because of the constraints on maximum slopes, we may view any potential we choose as a perturbation on the absolute value function $U_{\mathrm{abs}}(x)=F_{\max}|x|$. The details of $U(x)$ matter progressively less at higher and higher temperatures; consequently, we may approximate
\begin{align}
    \Pi_R(T_h) &\rightarrow \frac{\int_{0}^{\infty} \exp(-F_{\max}|x|/\kB T_h)\dd{x}}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp(-F_{\max}|x|/\kB T_h)\dd{x}}\\
    &= \frac 12.
\end{align}
If, however, we changed the right slope so that it was some fraction $f=\frac{F_{\mathrm{right}}}{F_{\max}}$ of the maximum slope, this formula is modified:
\begin{align}
    \Pi_R(T_h) &\rightarrow \frac{\int_{0}^{\infty} \exp(-fF_{\max}x/\kB T_h)\dd{x}}{\int_{-\infty}^{0} \exp(F_{\max}x/\kB T_h)\dd{x}+\int_{0}^{\infty} \exp(-fF_{\max}x/\kB T_h)\dd{x}}\\
    &= \frac 1{1+f},
\end{align}
as $T_h\rightarrow\infty$. Thus, the probability mass on either side of the barrier will not be equal even asymptotically. The rate $\lambda_3$ corresponds to the `fast' mode of cooling discussed earlier, where all of the probability mass falls into the closest well to it. Recall that if this ratio matched with the equilibrium mass ratio, we could expect a strong Mpemba effect. Indeed, we find that this condition allows us to reduce the high temperature necessary from $T_h = 1000~T_c$ to $T_h \approx 12~T_c$. 

The potential we thus choose is defined as follows:
\begin{align}
    U(x) &= \begin{cases}
        fF_{\max}(x-x_r)+U_0(x_r),& x > x_r\\
        U_0(x),& x_l \leq x \leq x_r\\
        -F_{\max}(x-x_l)+U_0(x_l),& x < x_l\\
    \end{cases}\\
    U_0(x) &= E_b\left[ 1-\left( \frac{x}{x_w} \right)^2 \right]^2 + \frac 12 \Delta E\left( \frac{x}{x_w} \right).\label{eq:asymmetricSlopesDoubleWellPotential}
\end{align}
As before, we choose $x_r$ and $x_l$ to satisfy $U_0'(x_r)=-fF_{\max}$ and $U_0'(x_l)=F_{\max}$ and $x_w=x_s/2\approx$ \qty{32}{\nano\metre}. While solving the FPE, we use the domain $[x_{\min},x_{\max}]=[-3x_s, 6x_s]$, which is large enough that most of the probability mass at the temperature $T_h$ is contained within it. We find that $\Delta E=0.6~\kB T_c$, and $f=0.3$ more or less optimise the conditions we outlined earlier, with $T_h\approx 12~T_c$ and $T_w\approx 3~T_c$, for a strong Mpemba effect and visible with about \num{5000} trials. All other parameters are identical to those of Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential}. We plot the modified potential in Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetricForcePotential}.

We now use Langevin simulations to see the effects of a finite-rate Newtonian quench $h(t;T_0,\Lambda) = (T_0/T_c-1)e^{-\Lambda t}+1$, where $T_0$ is the initial temperature and $\Lambda$ the quench rate. We compare the values of $\Lambda$ to the eigenvalues of the instantaneously quenched Fokker-Planck operators, $\lambda_k$. We find that $\Lambda=\lambda_2$ consistently displays no Mpemba effect. For $\Lambda=\lambda_3$, the hot and warm systems seem to approach the noise floor at similar times but still shows a crossing. When $\Lambda\geq \lambda_4$, the system always displays a Mpemba effect. This result is consistent with our intuition about the eigenmode each eigenvalue characterises: $\lambda_2$ is related to the Kramers rate, which should not affect the fast dynamics of the particle, but $\lambda_3$ is the rate-determining mode in the fast dynamics of the particle. Thus, we hypothesise that there is a critical quench rate $\Lambda^* < \lambda_3$. However, this result is only qualitative and possibly depends on the choice of parameters we used for the potential.

On solving the Fokker-Planck equation we find that the crossing is predicted to lie between $\lambda_3$ and $\lambda_2$. We confirm these results in Fig.~\ref{fig:finiteRateQuench} with numerical simulations. We find for the particular choice of $U(x)$, Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricSlopesDoubleWellPotential}, the value of $\Lambda^*\tau \approx 69$.\footnote{The value of $\Lambda^*$ was estimated by finding by eye where the initially hot and initially warm curves overlay each other exactly, amounting to an approach that looks for the greatest $\Lambda$ for which the system displays a Mpemba effect. However, a more precise definition would leverage the knowledge that $\Lambda^*>\lambda_2$ to argue that the effect of the quench is negligible beyond a timescale comparable to $1/\Lambda$, expand the state in eigenfunctions $p(x,t\gg 1/\Lambda)\approx \pi(x;T_c)+a_2v_2(x)e^{-\lambda_2t}$, with $a_2(T_0;\Lambda)=\ev{u_2,p(x,1/\Lambda)}/\ev{u_2,v_2}$, and finding the value of $\Lambda^*$ for which $\partial_{T_0}a_2(T_0,\Lambda^*)=0$.}

Unfortunately, we could not complete experiments to completely explore finite-rate quenches. However, the simulations and analytic solutions\footnote{We generate the analytic curves by first taking the empirical PDF at $t=0$ for each starting condition, performing a cubic interpolation to increase the mesh size (to ensure the solver converges but maintaining $\mathcal C^2$ continuity), and then using the Grima-Newman method we described earlier~\cite{Grima2004} to propagate the solution. We do this instead of overlaying the results with the target PDF as the initial condition to account for potential errors in positioning. Thus, these theoretical curves are not a direct comparison to the theoretical curves we compared the simulations with but rather a confirmation that the dynamics of the system do indeed obey the Fokker-Planck equation with time-dependent diffusivity.} we have shown here consistently match the experiments so closely that we think the results fairly convincing nonetheless.

More analysis is needed to find whether the critical quench rate continues to show the same behaviour in double-well potentials.\footnote{We restrict the discussion here to double-well potentials because different shapes of potential may have eigenfunctions that behave differently. In the one-dimensional triple well, for example, each of the eigenvalues are considerably smaller because there are multiple slow modes of cooling involving hopping between different pairs of wells. Thus, intuition about $\lambda_3$ being the dominant rate in the fast step likely does not apply well in such cases.} However, the observation that $\lambda_2>\Lambda^*>\lambda_3$ implied by the results here holds well for different choices of parameters that produce Mpemba effects, suggesting a certain generality.

\section{Is the 1D Mpemba effect an artefact of non-analyticity?}
The piecewise nature of the potentials used here may be of some concern, in that physical potentials are presumably analytic functions. Although we have shown here that infinite walls are not necessary for the existence of the Mpemba effect, our potential is nonanalytic at the two points, $x_l$ and $x_r$, where the potential is once but not twice differentiable everywhere. Such nonanalyticities may be present in effective potentials of coarse-grained models but not fundamental potentials, which are the sum of analytic terms. One may yet wonder whether a fully analytic potential on $\mathbb R$ shows the Mpemba effect or whether the Mpemba effect is an artefact of the piecewise nature of the potential.\footnote{\textit{Analytic} on some open set $\mathcal S$ here means that a function is equal to its infinite Taylor-series expansion $\forall\ x\in\mathcal S$.} A recent preprint, Liu et al.~\cite{Liu2026}, has argued that the Mpemba effect does not exist in analytic potentials. In this section, we show that the Mpemba effect is more a consequence of the physical shape of the potential, and that a fully analytic approximation to the potential defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricSlopesDoubleWellPotential} has a Mpemba effect.

To test whether analytic potentials can show the Mpemba effect, we first rewrite Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricSlopesDoubleWellPotential} as a single-line definition:
\begin{equation}
    U(x) = \mathrm{rect}\left( \frac{2x-X_r-X_l}{X_r-X_l} \right)U_0(x)+\mathrm{ReLU}(fF_{\max}(x-X_r))+\mathrm{ReLU}(-F_{\max}(x-X_l))
\end{equation}
Here, we have used two \textit{non-analytic} functions to define $U(x)$, the \textit{rectangle} function and the \textit{Rectified Linear Unit} function\footnote{$\mathrm{ReLU}$ is a popular choice of nonlinear function in many modern machine learning applications.}:
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{rect}(u) &= \begin{cases}
        1, u\in [-1,1]\\
        0, u\notin [-1,1]
    \end{cases}\\
    \mathrm{ReLU}(u) &= \begin{cases}
        u, u > 0\\
        0, u \leq 0
    \end{cases}.
\end{align}
We also use $X_l$ and $X_r$ as parameters instead of $x_l$ and $x_r$. While they are related, these allow us to avoid having extra step function terms $U(x_l)\sgn(x_l-x)$ and $U(x_r)\sgn(x-x_r)$ to ensure $\mathcal C^0$ continuity.

To construct an analytic approximation $U_a(x)\approx U(x)$, then, we need only construct analytic approximations to the two nonanalytic functions.\footnote{Constructing (potentially nonanalytic) functions or distributions as the limit of a series of analytic functions is familiar in physics: the Dirac delta $\delta(x)$, for example, can be expressed as the limit of a sequence of progressively narrower Gaussians $\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0}\e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}$, and the step function $\theta(x)$ can be expressed as the limit of a sequence $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\tanh(kx)$.} One way of approaching this is as follows:
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{rect}_n(u) &= \e^{-u^{2n}}\\
    \mathrm{ReLU}_n(u) &= \frac 1n\ln(1+\e^{nu})
\end{align}
We see that while
\begin{align}
    \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{rect}_n(u) &= \mathrm{rect}(u)\\
    \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{ReLU}_n(x) &= \mathrm{ReLU}(x),
\end{align}
meaning that the \textit{limit} is non-analytic, even though the functions are analytic for finite $n\in\mathbb N$. Thus, we can simply replace the nonanalytic functions in the potential definition with their analytic counterparts and use some sufficiently large value of $n$. We could alternatively choose a low value of $n$ but leave $X_l$ and $X_r$ as free parameters. We then sample $U(x)$ and choose $X_l$ and $X_r$ using a curve fit as parameters that make $U_a(x; X_l, X_r)$ very close to $U(x)$. We use this technique to construct the potential in Fig.~\ref{fig:analyticPotentials}, with
\begin{align}
    U_a(x) &= \mathrm{rect}_n\left( \frac{2x-X_r-X_l}{X_r-X_l} \right)U_0(x)+\mathrm{ReLU}_n(fF_{\max}(x-X_r))+\mathrm{ReLU}_n(-F_{\max}(x-X_l))\label{eq:analyticPotential}\\
    U_0(x) &= E_b\left[ 1-\left(\frac x{x_w}\right)^2 \right]^2 + \frac 12 \Delta E\left(\frac x{x_w}\right),
\end{align}
with $E_b=2~\kB T_c$, $\Delta E=0.3~\kB T_c$, $f=0.5$, $F_{\max}=50$, $x_w=0.5$, and $n=5$. With these analytic approximations, we find that the analytic potential $U_a(x)$ closely matches the piecewise potential $U(x)$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:analyticPotentials}(a)) and the $T_h$ value found by calculating $a_2(T_h)=0$ is approximately the same. If non-analyticity were necessary for a potential to display the Mpemba effect, we would expect $a_2(T)$ to be monotonic for the analytic approximation. Therefore, a potential need not be analytic nor have infinite walls to display the Mpemba effect. Liu et al.~\cite{Liu2026} used a slightly non-standard definition of analyticity that also required an infinite radius of convergence on $\mathbb C$. This definition excludes any potential that is not a pure polynomial. The functions described here do converge everywhere on $\mathbb R$ but can diverge at some values in $\mathbb C$. For example, the function $\log(1+e^{nx})/n$ diverges at $x=\pm i\pi/n$, giving the function a radius of convergence of $r=|i\pi/n|=\pi/n$ on the complex plane. Thus, they do not have infinite radii of convergence. Liu et al.\ demonstrated that no pure polynomial potential can demonstrate the Mpemba effect; rather, the potential must either have a box $\mathcal B$, be defined piecewise, or (as we have shown) use non-polynomial functions such as logarithms, exponentials, or inverses. Crucially, we have shown that the Mpemba effect can be found in a potential that has different asymptotic behaviour at $x=+\infty$ and $x=-\infty$, which is allowed even in a perfectly analytic potential.

\chapter{Conclusions}
In this thesis, we have outlined techniques to achieve finite-rate quenches. We have shown via simulation and solutions to the Fokker-Planck equation that the finite-rate quench constrains the choice of potential, because of the finite maximum force output of an optical trap and because of the inaccuracy of the previously-used `box' in finite quenches. We have also shown that a box is unnecessary to the Mpemba effect. Furthermore, non-analyticities are not required. Finally, we have provided preliminary results about the critical quench rate. We present here further possible extensions to the work presented in this thesis.

\section{Finding the critical quench rate}
\subsection{Experimentally finding the critical quench rate}
The work presented here has considered both analytic solutions and numerical simulations in finding the critical quench rate. We have also found the ideal potential to demonstrate the effect in our experimental setup. However, we still need to demonstrate the effects of quenching experimentally. In principle, both the software and hardware required to show the effect do currently exist. However, the experimental setup cannot yet accurately impose noise temperatures with artificially generated noise. Thus, the effects of quenching cannot be explored until it is possible to use artificial noise to impose an initial condition with reasonable accuracy.

\subsection{Coordinate transformations}
One of the key constraints on the potentials we discussed thus far was how the maximum temperature was constrained by the initial temperature $T_h$ since a sufficiently large temperature would cause force clipping. In this subsection, we consider a generalisation of the technique Basak et al.~\cite{Basak2026} used that allows for the implementation of arbitrary quench functions by scaling down the force and transforming the time coordinate associated with each timestep. Since there are no constraints on scaling down the force, the problem of force clipping ceases to matter. Such techniques could be useful in the event that performing quenches experimentally with noise proves intractable.

Weitsman~\cite{Weitsman1976} considered the problem of the diffusion equation with time-varying diffusivity,
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = D_ch(t)\pdv[2]{p}{x}.
\end{equation}
He showed that using the time transformation $\tilde t(t) = \int_0^t h(t')\dd{t'} = H(t)$, the equation can be recast as $\partial_{\tilde t} p = D_c\partial_x^2p$, which is much easier to solve. We apply the same trick to the Fokker-Planck equation:
\begin{align}
    \pdv{p}{t} &= -\pdv{x}\left[ \frac{F(x)}{\gamma}p(x,t) \right] + D_ch(t)\pdv[2]{p}{x}\\~\nonumber\\
    \pdv{\tilde p}{\tilde t} &= -\pdv{x}\left[ \frac{F(x)}{\gamma H^{-1}(\tilde t)}\tilde p(x,\tilde t) \right] + D_c\pdv[2]{\tilde p}{x},
\end{align}
where $\tilde p(x,\tilde t) = p(x,t)$. Here, we must be careful to use $h(t(\tilde t)) = h(H^{-1}(\tilde t))$, passing as the argument of $h$ the inverse function $H^{-1}(\tilde t(t))=t$. In doing this, we may define a new force $\tilde F(x,\tilde t) =F(x)/h(H^{-1}(\tilde t))$, yielding the Fokker-Planck equation $\partial_{\tilde t}\tilde p = -\partial_x(\tilde F(x,\tilde t) \tilde p)/\gamma + D_c\partial_x^2\tilde p$. Since $h(t)\geq 0$ for all $t>0$, $H(t)$ is monotonically increasing and therefore guaranteed to be invertible. While we have not eliminated the time dependency on the right-hand side, we have made the problem considerably easier to tackle with the experimental constraints we have: instead of needing a high diffusivity, we only need a low force for a longer time, and the feedback optical trap can generate low forces easily. The time we measure in the lab is relabelled to $\tilde t$ and mapped to $t$ after the experiment is complete.

Basak et al.~\cite{Basak2026} showed that higher effective temperatures yielded faster equilibration, provided the forces are proportionally increased; we have shown here that the converse is also true: decreasing the force and increasing the time of the experiment has the same effect as increasing the bath temperature. Indeed, it is easily seen that choosing Eq.~\ref{eq:instantaneousQuench} as the form of $h(t)$, $\tilde F(x)=F(x)/(T_h/T_c)$ and $\tilde t = t/(T_h/T_c)$, gives exactly what they found. We need only perform a post-hoc transformation on the time to convert a much easier force to impose in the lab.\footnote{Such techniques are already familiar to eliminate space-dependent diffusivity. By making the transformation $\tilde x(x)=\int_{x_0}^x \sqrt{D/D(x')}\dd{x'}$, space-dependent diffusivity $D(x)$ is replaced with an arbitrary positive constant $D$~\cite{Risken1996}.} For the form Eq.~\ref{eq:newtonianQuench}, we find that for all $t>0$, 
\begin{equation}
\tilde h(\tilde t; T_0, \Lambda) = W\left( \frac{\Delta T}{T_c} \e^{\Delta T/T_c-\Lambda\tilde t}\right)+1,
\end{equation}
where $\Delta T=T_0-T_c$ and $W$ is the Lambert $W$-function, defined by $W(x\e^x):=x$. If we want a protocol time $\tau$ (chosen so that $\Lambda\tau \gg 1$) in transformed time units, we must use a protocol time $\tilde\tau$ in laboratory frame units with $\tilde h(\tilde \tau; T_0, \Lambda)=h(\tau; T_0, \Lambda)$. Thus,
\begin{align}
    W\left( \frac{\Delta T}{T_c} \e^{\Delta T/T_c-\Lambda\tilde\tau}\right)+1 &= \frac{\Delta T}{T_c} \e^{-\Lambda\tau}+1\\
    \frac{\Delta T}{T_c} \e^{\Delta T/T_c-\Lambda\tilde\tau} &= \frac{\Delta T}{T_c} \e^{-\Lambda\tau}\exp[\frac{\Delta T}{T_c} \e^{-\Lambda\tau}]\\
    \frac{\Delta T}{T_c}-\Lambda\tilde\tau &= -\Lambda\tau+\frac{\Delta T}{T_c} \e^{-\Lambda\tau}\\
    \tilde\tau &= \tau-\frac{\Delta T}{T_c}\left( \frac{\e^{-\Lambda\tau}-1}{\Lambda} \right).
\end{align}
We have used the property $y=W(x) \implies x=ye^y$ to get from the first to the second line in this derivation.
By construction, $\Lambda\tau\gg 1$. Thus, the exponential vanishes and we are left with
\begin{equation}
    \tilde\tau \approx \tau+\frac{\Delta T}{T_c}\Lambda^{-1}.
\end{equation}
If $T_0\gg T_c$, then $\tilde\tau$ might be intractably large. However, we have found potentials in this thesis for which the maximum value of $T_0$ is $T_h=\order{10~T_c}$. The slowest quench we might try in the lab is $\Lambda=\lambda_2$. Usually, $1/\lambda_2\approx\tau/20$ is fairly typical. Thus, the time we would need to run the protocol would be at worst only about twice as long as usual, or roughly \qty{120}{\milli\second} for $\Delta T=20~T_c$.

\section{Two-dimensional potentials}
The original toy model Lu and Raz used was a particle in a periodic triple-well landscape, which they approximated as a three-state system. In one dimension, we cannot impose a periodic landscape, but if the functionality of the optical feedback trap is extended to use both the $x$ and the $y$ directions, we can use a potential where we vary the polar angle of the particle instead of the $x$-displacement. We can thus apply the much more theoretically tractable results of a three-state system to inform the parameters we choose, allowing us to test theories of quenching on discrete master equations. Furthermore, Hayakawa showed in a recent preprint that radially symmetric two-dimensional potentials behave as if they have a wall at $r=0$ even if no points of non-analyticity exist, thus suggesting that polynomial potentials in two-dimensional systems may display the Mpemba effect~\cite{Hayakawa2026}. Finally, in two dimensions, the forces we can impose with our experimental setup need not be conservative. Nonconservative forces can be expressed as $\mathbf F = -\nabla U + \nabla\times\mathbf A$, where $\mathbf A$ is some purely rotational \textit{vector potential}. A nonconservative force would allow for complex eigenvalues, with stable circulating probability currents in the steady-state probability density. For all these reasons, extending the results of Lu-Raz to two-dimensional systems would be interesting.

\section{Outlook}
The work presented in this thesis is but a sample of the many fascinating problems we considered over the last three years in the rapidly evolving study of relaxing nonequilibrium systems. It is remarkable that a simple experiment that may be done at home by a middle-schooler demonstrating an effect that may or may not even actually exist as originally stated can inspire such diverse theoretical and experimental studies on problems so simple as heating and cooling. Although the work we present here does not yet provide answers to the underlying mechanism (if any) of the Mpemba effect in water, considering finite-rate quenches deepens our understanding of nonequilibrium cooling.

We considered throughout this thesis a phenomenon wherein an initially hotter system cools faster than a warm system. But in the years since Lu and Raz first published their theory~\cite{Lu2017}, there has been considerable work on the \textit{quantum Mpemba effect}. Several versions of the quantum Mpemba effect have been proposed~\cite{Teza2023,Ares2025}. One by Ares et al.~\cite{Ares2023} in 2023,\footnote{`Quantum-Mpemba-like' effects had already been found nearly five years prior to Ref.~\cite{Ares2023}: Keller et al.\ found that a 12-ion system simulating a spin-$1/2$ chain could symmetrise faster if it started out in a state with a higher asymmetry parameter~\cite{Keller2018}, but these were not called quantum Mpemba effects then.} was demonstrated experimentally in a trapped-ion quantum simulator by Joshi et al.~\cite{Joshi2024} in 2024. In their construction, an isolated system is considered rather than a system coupled to a bath. The Hamiltonian operator that evolves the system obeys a certain symmetry, eg.~$\mathcal U(1)$, $\mathcal{SU}(2)$, or -- particularly relevant to the discussion here -- $\mathcal Z_2$.\footnote{$\mathcal Z_2$ denotes symmetry under reflection.} The system is initialised in an asymmetric state, often a pure state. The asymmetry of the system is quantified by some \textit{entanglement asymmetry} $\Delta S$, analogous to the distance function, and the Mpemba effect is said to occur if $\Delta S$ goes to zero faster for an initially `more asymmetric' state. It may seem on its face misleading to call such a phenomenon a quantum \textit{Mpemba} effect given that it occurs in a system that need not start out in a Boltzmann distribution, violating one of Lu-Raz's properties that we discussed in Ch.~2, but anomalous symmetrisation seems so aesthetically similar to the Mpemba effect that it makes sense to refer to it in those terms. The existence of anomalous symmetrisation perhaps warrants curiosity as to whether there are other properties vanishing at equilibrium that nevertheless cause a system to relax faster if they are initially higher -- perhaps `anomalous non-stationarity', for example -- and if they are related in some deeper way to the Mpemba effect. To this end, a more precise theory generalising the Mpemba effect is motivated.

Recently, Summer et al.~\cite{Summer2025} proposed that the different Mpemba effects could be united using a tool from quantum information theory, \textit{resource theories}~\cite{Chitambar2019}. A detailed discussion of resource theories is beyond the scope of this appendix, but suffice to say for our purposes that they define a set of \textit{free states} $\mathfrak F$ (i.e., a set of states that can be prepared at no `cost') and \textit{free operators} (i.e., a closed operation on $\mathfrak F$: an operator that transforms one free state into another). A \textit{resourceful state}, the opposite of a (resource) free state, is a state that cannot be constructed from free states using free operators. Using these two definitions, Summer et al.~\cite{Summer2025} define a \textit{resource} monotone $M$ that has similar but more general properties than those Lu and Raz require~\cite{Lu2017}. The Mpemba effect is then said to have occurred if the resource monotone starting in a more resourceful state crosses a monotone starting in a less resourceful state.

For example, the Mpemba effect we previously described is defined in terms of the resource of \textit{athermality} and the set of free states is the singlet $\{\pi(x;T_c)\}$. The \textit{athermality resource monotone} can then be defined as the distance function $\mathcal D_c$. 

Similarly in quantum systems, Moroder and Teza~\cite{Moroder} defined the resource as \textit{asymmetry}, the free states on $\mathcal Z_2$ symmetry as the set of all even probability densities, and the asymmetry monotone on $\mathcal Z_2$ as 
% \marginnote{\tiny{I don't think there's anything to cite here as the preprint will not come out before I submit this thesis. What should I do?}}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_s[p(x,t)] = \mathscr D[p(x,t),\mathcal G[p(x,t)]],
\end{equation}
where the \textit{twirling operator} $\mathcal G[p(x,t)]=[p(x,t)+p(-x,t)]/2$ and $\mathscr D$ is any distance function satisfying the properties we outlined in Ch.~2. $\mathcal G[p]$ is guaranteed to have perfect reflection symmetry and must thus be an asymmetry-free state. The asymmetry monotone is thus defined as the distance between $p(x,t)$ and the symmetrised version of $p(x,t)$.

The asymmetry Mpemba effect is then said to occur if, given two initial states $p_0(x,0)$ and $p_1(x,0)$ with $\mathcal D_s[p_0(x,0)] > \mathcal D_s[p_1(x,0)]$, there exists $t^*$ for which $\mathcal D_s[p_0(x,t^*)]<\mathcal D_s[p_1(x,t^*)]$ for all $t\geq t^*$.

To see whether the effect exists in classical systems, we choose a harmonic potential, the approximate potential that an optical trap imposes on a particle\footnote{Because the Fokker-Planck equation has an exact solution for a harmonic potential, this potential is analytically quite convenient. See Sec.~2.8}, and choose the standard deviation of the motion of the particle in the trap as the length unit, where $\sigma = \sqrt{\kB T/k}$. In these units, the equilibrium distribution will be $\pi(x)=\mathcal N[0,1]$. We choose the initial states to be the sum of two off-centre Gaussians:
\begin{align}
    p_0(x,0) &= a_0\mathcal N[\mu_0,\sigma_0] + a_0'\mathcal N[\mu_0',\sigma_0']\\
    p_1(x,0) &= a_1\mathcal N[\mu_1,\sigma_1] + a_1'\mathcal N[\mu_1',\sigma_1'],
\end{align}
where $a_i+a_i'=1$. We find that for $\mu_0=0.4\sigma$, $\mu_0'=0.6\sigma$ and $\mu_1=4\sigma$, $\mu_1'=-4\sigma$, with $\sigma_0\approx\sigma_0'$ and $\sigma_1\neq \sigma_1'$, the state $p_1$ will obviously take longer to equilibrate and therefore symmetrise than state $p_0$, but $p_0$ is still more asymmetric than $p_0$. Moreover, since the harmonic potential cannot display a thermal Mpemba effect (as discussed in Sec.~2.8), the asymmetry Mpemba effect is truly distinct from the athermality Mpemba effect.

We performed this experiment by loading the CDFs for $p_0$ and $p_1$ into the optical feedback trap and using the first-passage technique described in Sec.~3.4.3 to choose positions $x_0$, scaled \textit{down} by $\sqrt{k/\kB T_c}$. We then `switch off' feedback and let the particles evolve under the natural force of the trap. We repeat this procedure $N=$ \num{1323} times, allowing each particle to equilibrate over a protocol time $\tau=$ \qty{50}{\milli{\second}}. Finally, we scale \textit{up} all position measurements by the trap standard deviation. We find that the system displays the symmetrisation Mpemba effect, and the results are consistent with the Fokker-Planck equation.

The results are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:symmetrisationMpemba}. In Fig.~\ref{fig:symmetrisationMpemba}(a), the red PDF is clearly more asymmetric than the blue PDF because it is mostly on one side of the symmetry axis $x=0$. Nonetheless it will symmetrise faster since the particles in state $p_0(x)$ travel a shorter distance to reach equilibrium. Thus, the more asymmetric PDF symmetrises faster by virtue of its more rapid equilibration. The distance curves with respect to the equilibrium distribution $\pi(x,T_c)=\mathcal N[0,1]$ show no crossing. However, the asymmetry monotones do cross, demonstrating that the symmetrisation Mpemba effect is not identical to the athermality Mpemba effect.
