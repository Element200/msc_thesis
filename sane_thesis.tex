\chapter{Introduction}
The Mpemba effect, first described by the Erasto Mpemba in 1969 with Denis Osborne~\cite{Mpemba1969}, is the counterintuitive phenomenon where a hot system cools faster than a warm system of the same dimensions, material, etc. Although some claim that the phenomenon was known as far back as Aristotle~\cite{Burridge2016}, Mpemba first discovered it while trying to make ice cream with milk that had not yet fully cooled, and his discovery has continued to mystify physicists for decades afterwards.

An equilibrium explanation of the Mpemba effect defies common sense: a hot object would have to cool to the warm temperature before it could cool to room temperature; consequently one would expect that it must take strictly longer. Newton's law of cooling clearly fails to explain the effect. Indeed, the existence of the Mpemba effect in water was contested for several decades following the publication, and Burridge and Linden argued in 2016 that the Mpemba effect did not truly exist~\cite{Burridge2016}. However, more recent work has showed the existence of the Mpemba effect in water while controlling for many of the phenomena Burridge and Linden cite~\cite{Brownridge2025}. 

Although the Mpemba effect in water remains contentious, it is uncontroversial that `Mpemba-like' effects do in fact exist in other systems, both real and simulated. We list some of the effects here.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Clathrate hydrates}: Ahn et al. found that tetrahydrofuran (THF) hydrate displays the Mpemba effect~\cite{Ahn2016}. THF hydrate is the simplest kind of clathrate hydrates are crystalline solid polymers that can trap or `enclathrate' certain guest molecules inside cages of hydrogen bonds, and Ahn et al showed that the rate of THF hydrate formation goes down with initial temperature over a certain range of initial temperatures.
    \item \textit{Magnetoresistant magnetites}: Chaddah et al. showed that a half-doped magnetite, LCMO (\ce{La0.5Ca0.5MnO3}), demagnetised faster if the initial magnetisation was higher~\cite{Chaddah2010}. 
    \item \textit{Polylactide crystallisation}: Hu et al. showed that polylactide could crystallise faster if it started at a higher temperature~\cite{Hu2018}. Polylactide is a polymer with long chains that must be in a certain conformation in order for the polymer to crystallise. The authors reasoned that the more rapid crystallisation was because the polymer chains are more mobile at higher temperatures and could therefore rearrange themselves into advantageous structures faster.
    \item \textit{Granular fluid simulations}: Lasanta et al. showed using numerical simulations of granular fluids made of inelastically colliding rigid spheres that under certain conditions, they could cool faster if they started from higher temperatures~\cite{Lasanta2017}. Unlike other systems outlined here, theirs is a system without a phase transitions. They found that if the probability density describing the velocity distribution of their system has a high enough excess kurtosis, it displays the Mpemba effect. Interestingly, they also predict an \textit{inverse Mpemba effect}, where cooler systems can heat faster. 
\end{enumerate}
The authors of all of the papers cited here propose different mechanisms to explain the Mpemba effect in their systems, but the ubiquity of this effect in so many systems suggests a general explanation for the Mpemba effect. Lu and Raz proposed such a mechanism for a \textit{Markovian} Mpemba effect, using nonequilibrium statistical mechanics to show that if a system was Markovian (i.e., the current state depends only on the previous state), there was a straightforward condition to predict the existence of a Mpemba effect~\cite{Lu2017}. Under their framework, the system is out of equilibrium for the duration of its cooling, and this is critical to their explanation of why the system would not need to pass through an intermediate temperature. Temperature characterises equilibrium (i.e., Boltzmann) distributions, and between the initial and final times the system has no well-defined single temperature. The lack of a single well-defined uniform temperature was discussed by Mpemba and Osborne in their original paper as well: they observed that the temperature of their system was not uniform but had significant spatial gradients~\cite{Mpemba1969}. 

However, since a temperature was no longer well-defined during the cooling process, Lu and Raz needed a new way to quantify how `close' a system was to equilibrium. They did this using a \textit{distance function}, a function (or functional) of two distributions that returns a positive number that gets closer to zero as one distribution converges to the other. Additionally, the distance function would have a number of properties that ensure that one could not artificially choose a distance function that would reach zero faster if the temperature was higher. Lu and Raz showed that given the conditions they required, the Mpemba effect was agnostic to the choice of distance function: if the system showed the Mpemba effect with one distance function, it would have to show the Mpemba effect with any other choice of distance function. 

The first experimental confirmation of Lu and Raz's theory came with Kumar and Bechhoefer's~\cite{Kumar2020} work in 2020, where they used a \textit{feedback optical trap}, an optical trap that could impose almost arbitrary forces on a trapped particle by measuring the position of the particle and moving the trap centre in very short times, to study mesoscopic silica beads relaxing under a specially chosen potential. Instead of heating their sample to the very large temperatures they needed, they chose an initial position $x_0$ from a Boltzmann distribution $x_0\sim\pi(x;T_h)$ at high temperature $T_0$, moving the bead to this position, and then allowing it to diffuse under an asymmetric double-welled potential $U(x)$, imposed by a feedback optical trap (a technique they first developed in~\cite{Kumar2018}). This procedure was repeated a large number of times to construct an ensemble, which could be viewed in aggregate as a colloid of noninteracting one-dimensional particles. By constructing the experiment so that the initial condition was a Boltzmann distribution at high temperature, the distribution had a high \textit{effective temperature}. Since the effective bath temperature started out at a high multiple of room temperature and immediately dropped to bath temperature, the system they studied was an \textit{instantaneous quench}. This system in particular allowed for the observation of a \textit{strong} Mpemba effect, previously predicted in~\cite{Klich2019}, where the time to cool was exponentially lower for a system that started at a higher temperature, and later the inverse Mpemba effect that Lasanta et al. and Klich et al. predicted~\cite{Kumar2022,Klich2019}. 

% However, because of certain physical constraints that their system had, their potential had a somewhat artificial `box' that limited how large the initial position was allowed to be, and the Mpemba effect depended on the size of their box as well as the initial temperature. This becomes especially problematic when one tries to extend their results beyond the infinite quenches previously considered, as a high noise temperature enables the particles to escape the region of observation in an experiment. Consequently, the work in this thesis is twofold: we outline new potentials, exploring classes of potential that display strong Mpemba effects with no need for a box; and we implement these results in previous experiments, adding finite quenches using random forces.
In this thesis, we expand on Kumar and Bechhoefer's work, considering finite quenches. The cooling of any real system involves a finite quench, since a bath is usually coupled to a system through a boundary through which temperature must diffuse. These quenches may occur at variable rates depending on factors like thermal conductivity and surface area. We know from previous work that an instantaneous quench, which can be thought of as a very fast quench, must show a Mpemba effect under certain conditions. However, if the system evolves quasistatically (i.e. if its distribution has a Boltzmann form for some temperature $T(t)$ at every time), which is essentially a very slow quench, it can never display the Mpemba effect because of the intermediate temperature problem. Thus, we postulate a \textit{critical} quench rate, above which the Mpemba effect exists and below which it does not. The process of extending previous work to incorporate time-dependent quenching creates many additional constraints on experiments, which we will explore and discuss as well.

The outline of the rest of the thesis is as follows: the second chapter of this thesis will discuss the background theory, terminology, and definitions we use throughout. The third chapter will discuss the experimental setup and procedure, including the software that controls the experiment. The fourth chapter will discuss results from simulations and experiments, including theoretical constraints that are placed on previous experiments in order that their techniques can be extended to the systems we are interested in studying.

\chapter{Theory}

In this chapter, we describe more completely the model we are studying, the theoretical approach of Lu and Raz~\cite{Lu2017} from 2017, the framework Kumar and Bechhoefer~\cite{Kumar2020} used in 2020, and some of the theoretical problems we will solve. 

\section{The Master equation}
The Master equation describes the most general framework for the dynamics of Markov probability distributions. Given a discrete probability distribution $p_n$, the evolution of $p$ is given by
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = \mathbb W p,
\end{equation}
where the linear operator $\mathbb W$ is given by
\[\mathbb W_{nm} = \begin{cases}
    W_{nm},& m\neq n\\
    -\sum_{n\neq m} W_{nm},& m = n
\end{cases} = W_{nm}-\delta_{nm}\sum_{n\neq m} W_{nm},\]
where $W_{nm}$ represents the transition rate from state $m$ to state $n$ and $\delta_{nm}$ is the Kr\"onecker delta. The off-diagonal entries $\mathbb W_{nm}$ represent influxes and the diagonal entries represent effluxes. Each column in the master equation adds to zero by construction, ensuring that $\partial_t\sum  p_n = 0$ and thus probability is conserved at all times. A master equation need not have a stationary distribution, but if one exists we can prove that the system has \textit{detailed balance}: $\mathbb W_{ij}\pi_j = \mathbb W_{ji}\pi_i$ for all pairs $i,j$, meaning the influxes and effluxes of \textit{each state} are matched rather than just the net influx and efflux~\cite{vanKampen2011}. This formulation of the master equation applies only to discrete systems but can easily be made continuous by changing all indices to continuous variables and all sums to integrals, yielding the following form of the operator $\mathbb W$:
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = \mathbb W p = \int_{\mathbb R} [W(x|x')p(x)-W(x'|x)p(x)]\dd{x'}.
\end{equation}

\section{The Fokker-Planck equation}
The Master equation can be recast by change of variables. Letting $r = x-x'$ and defining $W(x-r; r) := W(x|x')$, we have $W(x'|x) = W(x;-r)$ and $p(x-r, t) = p(x', t)$. Thus,
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{t} p(x,t) = \int_{\mathbb R} [W(x;-r)p(x,t)-W(x-r;r)p(x-r)]\dd{r}.
\end{equation}
We now assume that $W(x;r)$ is sharply peaked in $r$ and that $W(x;r)p(x,t)$ is analytic in $x$. Consequently, only small jumps are likely at small times, and $r$ is most probably small. We can then Taylor-expand $W(x-r;r)p(x-r,t)$ in $r$:
\begin{equation}
    W(x-r;r)p(x-r,t) = W(x;r)p(x,t) - r\pdv{r} [W(x;r)p(x,t)]+\frac 12 r^2\pdv[2]{r}[W(x;r)p(x,t)]+ \dots.
\end{equation}
We have
\begin{equation}
    \left[ \int_{\mathbb R} W(x;r)\dd{r}-\int_{\mathbb R}W(x-r;-r)\dd{r} \right]p(x,t) = 0 = \pdv{p}{t}
\end{equation}
since $\int_a^b f(x)\dd{x} = \int_a^b f(a+b-x)\dd{x}$. Thus, 
\marginnote{\footnotesize{I read up about King's rule. It turns out it's not named after a guy named King; it was named by a coaching class teacher in India who named properties of integrals after playing cards for easy memorisation and found its way into the Indian high school calculus lingo. I'm not sure what it's called here, but most tables of properties of definite integrals don't mention the rule. I think the property worth mentioning because the equality is not obvious without it. The rule should be found in most introductory calculus textbooks, though. Should I cite one, or just state the rule as I've done here?}}
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = \int_{\mathbb R}\left[ \sum_{\nu=1}^\infty \frac{(-r)^\nu}{\nu!}\pdv[\nu]{x}[W(x;r)p(x,t)] \right]\dd{r}.
\end{equation}
We exchange the integral and summation, and define the moment rate 
\[c_\nu(x) = \int_{\mathbb R} r^\nu W(x;r)\dd{r}.\]
Then we can recast the Master equation as
\[\pdv{p}{t} = \sum_{\nu=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^\nu}{\nu!}\pdv[\nu]{x}[c_\nu(x)p(x,t)].\]
This infinite sum is called the \textit{Kramers-Moyal expansion}~\cite{vanKampen2011}. If we keep only two terms, we are left with the Fokker-Planck equation,
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = -\pdv{x}[c_1(x)p(x,t)] + \frac 12\pdv[2]{x}[c_2(x)p(x,t)],
\end{equation}
which is used to describe the probability density of Brownian objects diffusing under a force. There is not a good reason to truncate the series like this; there is no small parameter that allows us to ignore higher-order terms. Van Kampen~\cite{vanKampen2011} provides a more rigorous derivation of the Fokker-Planck equation from the Master equation. However, the Pawula theorem~\cite{Pawula1967} shows that for the probability to be normalisable, we must either have one, two, or infinitely many nonzero $c_\nu$ in the Kramers-Moyal expansion; we cannot truncate the series at three terms, for example. The important feature, however, is that the Fokker-Planck equation is a type of Master equation, and thus when we limit our analysis to the FPE, Lu and Raz's general results on the Master equation still apply.

If we define $c_1(x)=F(x)/\gamma$ with $F$ the force and $\gamma$ the drag coefficient (characteristic of a linear drag $F_d = -\gamma \dot x$\footnote{$\gamma$ has dimensions $\mathrm{mass/time}$.}) and $c_2(x)= 2D$, we are left with an expression for the probability distribution of a Brownian particle in a medium of viscosity $\gamma$, experiencing force $F$ and diffusing with diffusivity $D$. This form will be more useful when describing actual diffusing silica microspheres. If the medium is homogeneous, $D$ is independent of $x$, and
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = -\frac 1{\gamma}\pdv{x}[F(x)p(x,t)]+D\pdv[2]{p}{x}.\label{eq:FPE}
\end{equation}
We also often use the fluctuation-dissipation relationship~\cite{vanKampen2011},
\begin{equation}
    D = \frac{\kB T}{\gamma},\label{eq:fluctuationDissipation}
\end{equation}
where $\kB $ is the Boltzmann constant and $T$ is the temperature. 

\section{Eigenfunction expansion of the $\mathbb{W}$-operator}
The Master equation suggests the separation \textit{ansatz} 
\begin{equation}
    p(x,t) = v(x)e^{-\lambda t}.\label{eq:separationAnsatz}
\end{equation}
Plugging this into the Master equation, we have
\begin{align}
    -\lambda e^{-\lambda t}v(x) &= e^{-\lambda t}\mathbb W v(x)\\
    \mathbb W v(x) &= -\lambda v(x).\label{eq:eigenvalueFPE}
\end{align}
Equation~\ref{eq:eigenvalueFPE} is an eigenvalue equation and allows us to expand the solution $p(x,t)$ in an eigenbasis:
\begin{equation}
    p(x,t) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n v_n(x)e^{-\lambda_n t}.
\end{equation}
Note that these eigenvalues need not be discrete, in which case we replace the sum with an integral. 
If all probabilities normalise to $\int_{\mathbb R} p(x,t)\dd{x} = 1$, the eigenfunctions $v_n(x)$ are members of a Hilbert space with $\ev{u_m,v_n} \propto \delta_{mn}$. If $p(x,t)$ is defined on $x\in\mathbb R$, the inner product is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \ev{u_m,v_n} = \int_{\mathbb R} u_m(x)v_n(x)\dd{x}.
\end{equation} 
Here, $u_m(x)$ denotes the \textit{left eigenfunction} of $\mathbb W$, which is equal to the right eigenfunction of the conjugate operator $\mathbb W^\dagger$, satisfying $\mathbb W^\dagger u_m = -\lambda_m u_m$. Since $\mathbb W\neq \mathbb W^\dagger$ in general, $u_m(x) \neq v_m(x)$ \footnote{We may, however, transform $v_m$ to express the Fokker-Planck equation as a Schr\"odinger equation. If we do so, the propagator of time evolution will be Hermitian, and the left and right eigenfunctions of the modified equation will be identical up to complex conjugation. A Hermitian operator has purely real eigenvalues, and since eigenvalues are preserved under similarity transformations, all $\lambda_n\in\mathbb R$. Thus, no periodicity is allowed in the solution to the FPE in one dimension. See Risken for details~\cite{Risken1996}.}. We can use this fact to isolate the coefficients $a_n$ by taking the inner product of $p(x,t)$ with $u_n(x)$:
\begin{align}
    \ev{u_n,p(x,t)} &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k \ev{u_n,v_k}e^{-\lambda_k t}\\
    &= a_n\ev{u_n,v_n}e^{-\lambda_n t}\\
    \therefore \ev{u_n,p(x,0)} &= a_n\ev{u_n,v_n}\\
    a_n &= \frac{\ev{u_n,p(x,0)}}{\ev{u_n,v_n}}\label{eq:an}
\end{align}
Thus, the boundary condition $p(x,0)$ must determines the coefficients in the series\footnote{We also require \textit{natural boundary conditions} to ensure that a stationary solution exists: $\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty} j(x,t) = 0$, where the probability current $j(x, t) := F(x)p(x,t)/\gamma-D\partial_x p(x,t)$ for all times $t$. In addition, to ensure that $\ev{u, \mathbb W v} = \ev{\mathbb W^\dagger u, v}$, we must also require the Neumann boundary conditions $\eval{\pdv{u}{x}}_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}=0$ while solving the conjugate FPE~\cite{Risken1996}.}. 

Setting the left-hand side of the Master equation to zero, we are left with the steady-state equation
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb W \pi(x) = 0 = 0\cdot \pi(x).
\end{equation}
In other words, the steady-state equation is an eigenvalue equation with zero eigenvalue\footnote{The fact that the master equation must have at least one zero eigenvalue is perhaps most obvious in the discrete case, where one can perform Gaussian elimination on $\mathbb W$ to obtain at least one column of zeroes. A matrix with at least one zero eigenvalue is singular, which implies that we cannot find an inverse matrix $\mathbb W^{-1}$ that takes a state away from equilibrium.}. Because of the $e^{-\lambda_n t}$ terms in the infinite series, the eigenfunction expansion of the Master equation is always absolutely convergent (provided that $\lambda_n \geq 0$, which is necessary for the probability to remain normalisable). Thus, we may freely order terms in the sum so that $\lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \dots$, and $\lambda_1=0$, the smallest possible value. In the particular case of the Fokker-Planck equation with uniform diffusivity in space, we are left with the equation 
\begin{align}
    \frac 1{\gamma}\pdv{x}[F(x)\pi(x)] &= \frac{\kB T}{\gamma}\pdv[2]{\pi}{x}\\
    F(x)\pi(x) &= \kB T\pdv{\pi}{x}\\
    \pi(x) &= \frac 1{Z(T)} \exp[-\frac{U(x)}{\kB T}]\label{eq:boltzmannDistro},
\end{align}
where we define the normalisation constant
\begin{equation}
    Z(T) = \int_{\mathbb R} \exp[-\frac{U(x)}{\kB T}]\dd{x},
\end{equation}
and the potential $U(x)$ is defined so that 
\begin{equation}
    F(x) = -\dv{U}{x}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:boltzmannDistro} is called the \textit{Boltzmann distribution}. In general, for any Markov system with detailed balance where each state $x$ has energy $E(x)$, the Boltzmann distribution is given by
\begin{equation}
    \pi(x;T_i) = \frac 1{Z(T_i)} e^{-\frac{E(x)}{\kB T_i}}.
\end{equation}
For brevity, we will often denote $\pi_i(x) = \pi(x;T_i)$

\section{Cooling in Markov systems}

    \label{fig:coolingTheory}

We can now explain the theory that Lu and Raz~\cite{Lu2017} first described in 2017. We say that a system has temperature $T$ if its probability distribution of states is given by the Boltzmann distribution
\begin{equation}
    \pi(x;T) = \frac 1{Z(T)} \exp[-\frac{U(x)}{\kB T}].
\end{equation}
We suppose the system has temperature $T_c$ at equilibrium ($t\rightarrow\infty$), and has temperature $T_h$ at $t=0$. The evolution of $p(x,t)$ under the master equation $\partial_t{p}=\mathbb W p$ with the boundary condition 
\begin{equation}
    p(x,0)=\pi(x;T_h)\label{eq:initialCondition}
\end{equation} 
and steady state 
\begin{equation}
    \lim_{t\rightarrow\infty} p(x,t) = \pi(x;T_c)\label{eq:equilibriumCondition}
\end{equation}
then defines what it means for a nonequilibrium system to cool. This, then, gives us a way to resolve the problem we stated in the introduction: since $p(x,t)$ need not be Boltzmann for $0<t<\infty$, the system does not have a well-defined temperature between $t=0$ and $t\rightarrow\infty$. However, we now have another problem: the Mpemba effect (and most `Mpemba-like' effects) relied on a phase transition like freezing or demagnetisation to make precise the statement that a hot system could cool `faster' (all else being the same). In the real world, phase transitions involve nucleation, which is highly sensitive to small differences in the experimental setup and thus hard to characterise reproducibly. Moreover, some Mpemba-like effects, such as the granular simulations Lasanta et al. considered, have no phase transition at all~\cite{Lasanta2017}. Indeed, since a Markov system only reaches the equilibrium temperature asymptotically and does not have a phase transition, it is not obvious how one may define what it means for a system to cool `more quickly' than another. Consequently, a more precise definition of the Mpemba effect is warranted. Lu and Raz therefore made use of another tool, a \textit{distance function}.

\section{Distance functions, and a definition of the Mpemba effect}

(a) For the double-welled potential in Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential} with $T_h=1000T_c$ and $T_w=100T_c$, the `hot' distance curve (red) crosses the `warm' distance curve (blue) at some time $t^*$. (b) With a harmonic potential, the two curves never cross. We therefore say that the potential in (a) displays the Mpemba effect and the potential in (b) does not.}
    \label{fig:mpembaFPE}

Metrics are often used in analysis to quantify how `far apart' two elements of a set are. For example, the $\sup$-norm, $\mathscr D_{\mathcal L_2}:\mathbb H\times\mathbb H\rightarrow\mathbb R_{\geq 0}$ where 
\[\mathscr D_{\text{uniform}}(f_1,f_2)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}{\abs{f_1(x)-f_2(x)}}\] 
and $\mathbb H$ is the set of all continuous functions on $\mathbb R$ quantifies how far apart two continuous functions $f_1$ and $f_2$ are and allows one to make precise statements about convergence in real function-space.

The approach Lu and Raz~\cite{Lu2017} used was similar: define a function of two probability distributions that returns a positive number whose magnitude quantifies how different they are. The distance function only returns $0$ if the two distributions are identical. Additionally, to preserve intuitive ideas of distance to equilibrium, they defined a series of criteria that distance functions must obey. More specifically, a distance function $\mathscr D$ is a function that takes in two probability distributions $p_1,p_2$ and returns a number $\mathscr D[p_1, p_2] \geq 0$ such that: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Positivity:} $\mathscr D[p_1,p_2] \geq 0$, and $\mathscr D[p_1,p_2] = 0 \iff p_1=p_2$;
    \item \textit{Monotonic increase with initial temperature:} \\$\mathscr D[\pi(x;T_h), \pi(x;T_c)] > \mathscr D[\pi(x;T_w), \pi(x;T_c)]$ if $T_h>T_w>T_c$ (i.e. systems with higher initial temperatures are always `further' from equilibrium);
    \item \textit{Monotonic decrease with time:} Given a cooling system whose distribution is $p(x,t)$, $\mathscr D[p(x,t_1),\pi(x;T_c)] \geq \mathscr D[p(x,t_2),\pi(x;T_c)]$ if $t_1<t_2$ (\textit{i.e.} systems always relax \textit{towards} equilibrium, never away);
    \item \textit{Convexity and continuity} $\mathscr D[p, \pi(x;T_c)]$ is smooth and convex in $p$. 
\end{enumerate}
Note that Lu and Raz do not require $\mathscr D$ to be a metric; $\mathscr D$ need not be symmetric in its two arguments or obey the triangle inequality. However, $\mathscr D$ serves the same purpose as a metric: it puts a number on how far two distributions are from each other, allowing us to precisely quantify how fast one distribution converges to another. The second condition captures the idea that a hot distribution is `farther' from equilibrium than a warm distribution. The third condition captures the idea that, if the system is always cooling, the system must get closer to equilibrium with time. 

Examples of the distance function $\mathscr D$ include
\begin{itemize}
    \item The $\mathcal L_1$ distance, 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathscr D_{\mathcal L_1}[p_1,p_2] = \int_{\mathbb R} |p_1(x)-p_2(x)|\dd{x};\label{eq:L1definition}
    \end{equation}\footnote{More generally, the $\mathcal L_k$ distance is defined as $\mathscr D_{\mathcal L_p}[p_1,p_2]=\left[ \int_{\mathbb R} \abs{p_1(x)-p_2(x)}^k\dd{x} \right]^{1/k}$. It is, however, rarely useful to use a higher norm than the $\mathcal L_1$ norm.}
    \item The Kullback-Leibler divergence, 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathscr D_{\mathrm{KL}}[p_1,p_2] = \int_{\mathbb R} p_1(x)\ln\left[ \frac{p_1(x)}{p_2(x)} \right]\dd{x};\label{eq:KLdefinition}
    \end{equation}\footnote{Computing the KL divergence with empirical PDFs can be tricky as $p_2(x_i)=0$ for any bin $[x_i-\Delta x/2, x_i+\Delta x/2]$ will result in $\mathscr D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ diverging. To avoid such divergences, we add a post-processing step where we add a single `pseudocount' to each empty bin. If, instead, $p_1(x_i)=0$ has any zero counts, we define $p_1(x_i)\log(p_1(x_i)/p_2(x_i)):=0$. Often while computing the KL divergence numerically, expressing the integrand of Eq.~\ref{eq:KLdefinition} as $p_1\log(p_1)-p_2\log(p_2)$ instead of using a fraction in the log avoids a number of computational bugs.}
    \item The entropic distance from Lu and Raz~\cite{Lu2017}:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathscr D_{\mathrm{ent}}[p_1,p_2] = \frac{\ev{U}_{1}-\ev{U}_2}{\kB T} - \int_{\mathbb R} \left[ p_1(x)\ln [p_1(x)]-p_2(x)\ln [p_2(x)] \right]\dd{x},
    \end{equation}
    where $\ev{f}_i = \int_{\mathbb R} f(x)p_i(x)\dd{x}$.
\end{itemize}

Since we always take the distance with respect to the steady-state Boltzmann distribution (at temperature $T_c$), we define a new functional for brevity, 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c[p(x,t)] = \mathscr D[p(x,t),\pi(x;T_c)].
\end{equation}
$\mathcal D_c$ is a \textit{functional}, an operator that takes in two functions and returns a number. 

This theoretical machinery now allows for a precise definition of the Mpemba effect: two Markov systems $p_h$ and $p_w$ that cool to the same distribution $\pi_c$ start in states $p_h(x,0) = \pi(x;T_h)$ and $p_w(x,0)=\pi(x;T_w)$, exhibit a Mpemba effect if there exists finite $t^*$ for which 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c[p_h(x,t)] \leq \mathcal D_c[p_w(x,t)],\label{eq:mpembaCondition}
\end{equation}
for all $t>t^*$, for some distance function $\mathcal D_c$. Since 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c[p_h(x,0)] > \mathcal D_c[p_w(x,0)]
\end{equation}
by construction, the distance curves of a system displaying a Mpemba effect must cross an odd number of times\footnote{As the hot system may be closer to equilibrium for a short time before the warm system overtakes it again, one must require an odd number of crossings rather than simply requiring at least one crossing.}, crossing for the last time at $t=t^*$, when $\mathcal D_c[p_h(x,t^*)]=\mathcal D_c[p_w(x,t^*)]$~\cite{Klich2019}. Figure~\ref{fig:mpembaFPE}(a) shows an example of the signature of the Mpemba effect using distance curves. The level and time of crossing can vary based on the choice of distance function. However, since the Mpemba effect describes something physical, the Mpemba effect should not depend on the choice of distance function. In the next section, we demonstrate that, provided the three properties described here hold, the Mpemba effect is agnostic to the choice of $\mathscr D$. We will later also mention a nonexample of a distance function, and how ignoring any of the three axioms described here can lead to spurious Mpemba effects.

Consider two systems, $p_h(x,t)$ and $p_w(x,t)$, for which $p_h(x,0)=\pi(x;T_h)$, $p_w(x,0)=\pi(x;T_w)$, and identical steady states $p_h(x,\infty)=p_w(x,\infty)=\pi(x;T_c)$. Both systems have eigenfunction expansions given by
\begin{equation}
    p(x,t) = \pi_c(x) + \sum_{n=2}^\infty a_n v_n(x)e^{-\lambda_n t}.
\end{equation}
As before, we have ordered our series so that $\lambda_2<\lambda_3<\dots$. As a result, the $e^{-\lambda_3 t}$ term decays exponentially faster than the $e^{-\lambda_2 t}$ term, and so for some sufficiently large time we can truncate the series to two terms:
\begin{equation}
    p(x,t) \approx \pi_c(x) + a_2(T_i) v_2(x)e^{-\lambda_2 t},
\end{equation}
for initial temperature $T_i$. Note that only $a_2$ depends on the initial temperature. The condition we require for the Mpemba effect is $\mathcal D_c[p_h(x,t^*)] \leq \mathcal D_c[p_w(x,t^*)]$. Using the approximation, we can now use the smoothness of $\mathcal D$ in $p$ to then make the following approximation, since the $a_2v_2(x)e^{-\lambda_2t}$ term will be small at a suitably large time:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c\left[ \pi_c+a_2v_2e^{-\lambda_2 t} \right] \approx \mathcal D_c\big[\pi_c\big] + a_2e^{-\lambda_2 t}\ev{\fdv{\mathcal D_c}{v_2}\big[\pi_c\big], v_2},
\end{equation}
where $\fdv{}{f}$ denotes the functional derivative with respect to the function $f(x)$. The functional derivative, analogous to a gradient, will always return a member of a dual space. Here, we make use the form of a Taylor expansion for functionals,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal F\big[ f+\epsilon g \big] = \mathcal F\big[f\big] + \epsilon \ev{\fdv{\mathcal F}{g}\big[f\big],g} + \order{\epsilon^2}.
\end{equation}
We notice that in this equation, $\mathcal D_c\big[{\pi_c}\big]=0$ by the first property. Thus,
\begin{align}
    \mathcal D_c\big[\pi_c+a_2(T_i)v_2e^{-\lambda_2 t}\big] \approx a_2(T_i)e^{-\lambda_2 t}\ev{\fdv{\mathcal D_c}{v_2}\big[\pi_c\big],v_2}
\end{align}
for initial temperature $T_i$. With $V := \ev{\fdv{\mathcal D_c}{v_2}\big[\pi_c\big],v_2}$, we have 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c\left[ \pi_c+a_2(T_i)v_2e^{-\lambda_2 t}\right] \approx a_2(T_i)Ve^{-\lambda_2 t}.
\end{equation}
Equating this to our previous expression, we see
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c\big[p_i(x,t)\big] \approx Ve^{-\lambda_2 t}a_2(T_i).
\end{equation}
The condition in Eq.~\ref{eq:mpembaCondition} for the Mpemba effect then approximately reduces to
\begin{equation}
    Ve^{-\lambda_2 t^*}a_2(T_h) \leq Ve^{-\lambda_2 t^*}a_2(T_w),
\end{equation}
for some $t^*<\infty$. However, although $e^{-\lambda_2t^*}$ is strictly positive, $V<0$ is possible if $a_2(T_h),a_2(T_w)<0$ as well. Thus, $a_2(T_w)<a_2(T_h)$ is not implied but rather
\begin{equation}
    \abs{a_2(T_h)} \leq \abs{a_2(T_w)}.
\end{equation}

Put differently, the condition reduces to the statement that $\abs{a_2(T)}$ is nonmonotonic in $T$.  Since we have not made use of any particular form of $\mathcal D_c$ except for the properties we required, the Mpemba effect is independent of the choice of distance function\footnote{This proof is not quite as rigorous as Lu and Raz's, and ignores the convexity that they required of the distance function. If the distance function is not convex, the terms that we truncated may add to something that goes to zero more slowly than $a_2Ve^{-\lambda_2t}$. See the supplementary information in Lu and Raz for details~\cite{Lu2017}.}. For example, if we choose the $\mathcal L_1$ distance, then
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c[\pi_c+a_2v_2e^{-\lambda_2t}] = \int_{\mathbb R}\abs{\pi_c+a_2v_2e^{-\lambda_2t}-\pi_c}\dd{x} = \abs{a_2}e^{-\lambda_2t}\int_{\mathbb R}\abs{v_2(x)}\dd{x}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \fdv{\mathcal D_c}{v_2} = \sgn(v_2(x)),
\end{equation}
yielding~\cite{Kumar2020}
\begin{equation}
    \ev{\fdv{\mathcal D_c}{v_2}[\pi_c],v_2} = \int_{\mathbb R}\sgn(v_2(x))v_2(x)\dd{x} = \int_{\mathbb R}\abs{v_2(x)}\dd{x}.
\end{equation}

This proof relies on the fact that the second term in the eigenfunction expansion dominates the behaviour. However, this approximation can break down if there exists $T_h^*>T_w>T_c$ for which $a_2(T_h^*)=0$ and $a_2(T_w)\neq 0$. In this case, the third term dominates the series:
\begin{equation}
    p_h \approx \pi_c + a_3(T_h)v_3e^{-\lambda_3 t}.
\end{equation}
Repeating the analysis before, but using $a_3e^{-\lambda_3 t}v_3$ as the small term, we arrive at the approximation
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c\big[p_h\big] \approx a_3(T_h^*)e^{-\lambda_3 t}\ev{\fdv{\mathcal D_c}{v_3}\big[\pi_c\big],v_3}\label{eq:strongMpembaph}.
\end{equation}
Comparing this with 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c\big[p_w\big] \approx a_2(T_w)e^{-\lambda_2 t}\ev{\fdv{\mathcal D_c}{v_2}\big[\pi_c\big], v_2}\label{eq:strongMpembapw},
\end{equation}
we see that $\mathcal D_c\big[p_w\big] \gg \mathcal D_c\big[p_h\big]$ for any time large enough that Eq.~\ref{eq:strongMpembapw} holds. Equation~\ref{eq:strongMpembaph} implies that systems with this special condition display cooling that is exponentially faster. Kumar and Bechhoefer~\cite{Kumar2020} refer to this as the \textit{strong} Mpemba effect. We display an example of a Mpemba effect, an example of a strong Mpemba effect, and a nonexample in Fig.~\ref{fig:a2vsT}.

For convenience, we always use the $\mathcal L_1$ norm, Eq.~\ref{eq:L1definition}, in this thesis. To demonstrate that the $\mathcal L_1$ norm is a valid distance function, we note that since the $\mathcal L_1$ norm satisfies the properties of a metric, positivity is automatically satisfied. To prove the third property, we use the fact that we can expand the probability density $p(x,t)$ in eigenfunctions as in Eq.\ref{eq:eigenvalueFPE}. We see that 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_c[p(x,t)] = \int_{\mathbb R} \sum_{k=2}\abs{a_k(T_h)v_k(x)}e^{-\lambda_kt}\dd{x}.
\end{equation}
Differentiating with respect to time,
\begin{equation}
    \dv{t}\mathcal D_c[p(x,t)] = \int_{\mathbb R} \sum_{k=2}\left[ -\lambda_k\abs{a_k(T_h)v_k(x)} \right]e^{-\lambda_kt}\dd{x}.
\end{equation}
Since each eigenvalue is positive, each term in the sum, and therefore the whole integrand, is strictly nonpositive everywhere. Thus, the integral is nonpositive as well, and so the $\mathcal L_1$ norm is monotonically nonincreasing with time. To see that the $\mathcal L_1$ distance is convex, we evaluate the distance function of two PDFs, $p_1(x,t)$ and $p_2(x,t)$ and use the triangle inequality:
\begin{align}
    \frac12\left[ \mathcal D_c[p_1(x,t)]+\mathcal D_c[p_2(x,t)] \right] &= \frac 12\int_{\mathbb R}\left[ \abs{p_1(x,t)-\pi_c(x)} +\abs{p_1(x,t)-\pi_c(x)}\right]\dd{x}\\
    \geq \frac 12\int_{\mathbb R}\abs{p_1(x, t)+p_2(x, t)-2\pi_c(x)}\dd{x} &= \int_{\mathbb R}\abs{\frac{p_1(x, t)+p_2(x, t)}2-\pi_c(x)}\dd{x}\\
    \frac12\left[ \mathcal D_c[p_1(x,t)]+\mathcal D_c[p_2(x,t)] \right] &\geq \mathcal D_c\left[ \frac{p_1(x,t)+p_2(x,t)}{2}\right].
\end{align}
Thus, $\mathcal D_{\mathcal L_1}$ is convex in $p$. Proving the second property for the $\mathcal L_1$ norm is more involved, but we empirically demonstrate that it is true at least over the range of temperatures that we care about in this thesis for asymmetric double-welled potentials in Fig.~\ref{fig:a2vsT}.

One important note is that although the existence of the Mpemba effect with one distance function implies its existence for every other distance function, $t^*$ need not be the same for all choices of $\mathcal D_c$; indeed, $t^*$ need not even be upper-bounded (although always finite). Vu and Hayakawa~\cite{Vu2025} recently described a \textit{thermomajorisation ordering} $\prec_{\pi_c}$ of all possible probability distributions $p(x,t)$. This ordering also qualifies how far a distribution is from $\pi_c$, and one can thus say that the \textit{thermomajorisation Mpemba effect} occurs if $p_h(x,t^*) \prec_{\pi_c} p_w(x,t^*)$ for some $t^*$. The thermomajorisation Mpemba effect implies the Mpemba effect, but the converse is not true, and the thermomajorisation Mpemba effect upper-bounds the time $t^*$ at which we can say that the hot system has cooled `more' than the warm system. Thus, at least a subset of all systems displaying the Mpemba effect must display the effect within a finite time.

The Fokker-Planck equation, Eq.~\ref{eq:FPE}, describes the probability density of Brownian particles but cannot describe the fluctuating random trajectories of individual Brownian particles. The probability densities of real particles need to be estimated from real trajectories, and will only match the results of the FPE in the infinite-particle limit. To evaluate statistics a finite number of particles, we instead use the \textit{Langevin equation}, a stochastic differential equation related to the FPE, to model our experiment. 

The Langevin equation is derived from Newton's second law applied to a Brownian particle experiencing a drag with friction coefficient $\gamma$, but with an added term to account for random noise. Thus, the particle experiences two forces, a deterministic force $F(x)$ and a random force proportional to the normally-distributed $\xi(t)\sim \mathcal N[0,1]$. The scale of the random force is set by the diffusivity, which obeys the fluctuation-dissipation relationship with the drag constant $\gamma$ 
If the force $F(x)$ were zero, we would expect that the average distance the particle travels after time $t$ is zero with a variance $2Dt=2t\kB T/\gamma$ in one dimension\footnote{In $d$ dimensions, the variance is $2dDt$}. 
\marginnote{\small{I'm not sure what you meant by using $D$ and appealing to the fluctuation-dissipation theorem to derive 2.55?}}

The Langevin equation is then given by writing out Newton's second law:
\begin{equation}
    m\ddot x = -\gamma\dot x + F(x) + \sqrt{2\gamma\kB T}\eta(t).\label{eq:underdampedLangevin}
\end{equation}
If the system is \textit{overdamped}, we may ignore the left-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:underdampedLangevin} and divide throughout by $\gamma$ to arrive at the Langevin equation
\begin{equation}
    \dot x(t) = \frac{F(x(t))}{\gamma} + \sqrt{2D}\eta(t),\label{eq:continuousLangevin}
\end{equation}
where we have used the fluctuation-dissipation relationship~\ref{eq:fluctuationDissipation}. Here, $\eta(t)$ is drawn from $\mathcal N[0,1]$, and corresponds exactly to~\ref{eq:FPE}. In addition to the requirement that $\ev{\eta}=0$, we also require that $\ev{\eta(t)\eta(t')}=\delta(t'-t)$\footnote{This relation implies that the dimensions of $\eta$ are $\mathrm{time^{-1/2}}$.}, which means that this equation does not have an exact solution, and the trajectory defined by $x(t)$ will, with probability one, be continuous everywhere and yet differentiable nowhere. To solve the Langevin equation numerically, we use \textit{Euler-Maruyama integration}, where we first discretise Eq.~\ref{eq:continuousLangevin} using a finite-difference approximation of the derivative to obtain
\begin{equation}
    x(t+\Delta t) = x(t) + \frac{F(x(t))\Delta t}{\gamma} + \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\xi(t),
\end{equation}
where $\xi(t) = \sqrt{\Delta t}\eta(t)$ is a dimensionless random number drawn from a normal distribution, $\xi(t)\sim\mathcal N[0,1]$. For small $\Delta t$, we may then restate this equation as the discrete equation
\begin{equation}
    x_{t+\Delta t} = \frac{F(x_t)}{\gamma}\Delta t + \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\xi_t. \label{eq:discreteLangevin}
\end{equation}
Using $\xi_t$ in this equation means that we implicitly use the It\^o convention\footnote{The It\^o convention preserves causality, at the cost of breaking certain integration conventions. In contrast, the Stratanovich convention preserves familiar integration rules but breaks causality; see chapter 14 of van Kampen~\cite{vanKampen2011}. However, the distinction between the two does not matter in the limit of a large number of trajectories.} for integrating stochastic differential equations. An individual trajectory is not by itself particularly insightful, but we can construct empirical probability densities if we have a large number of them. In the limit of an infinite number of trajectories, the empirical probability density solves the FPE, Eq.~\ref{eq:FPE}~\cite{vanKampen2011}. For each integration, we draw $x_0\sim \pi(x;T_h)$, which accounts for the initial condition. 

To account for experimental constraints, some modifications may be desirable. The first experimental constraint is \textit{measurement noise}, where the measured position $\bar x_t$ differs from the true position $x_t$ by some small normally distributed random number $\sigma_m\zeta_t$, where $\zeta_t\sim\mathcal N[0,1]$, $\ev{\zeta_t\zeta_{t'}}=\delta_{tt'}$, and $\sigma_m$ sets the scale of the measurement noise. If this is so, we then modify the discrete Langevin equation~\ref{eq:discreteLangevin} into the coupled equations
\begin{align}
    x_{t+\Delta t} &= \frac{F(\bar x_t)}{\gamma}\Delta t + \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\xi_t \label{eq:discreteLangevinMeasurementNoise}\\
    \bar x_{t+\Delta t} &= x_t + \sigma_m\zeta_t.
\end{align}
Implicitly, this equation also captures the features of a feedback trap, which we discuss further in Ch. 3: if we set $\sigma_m=0$ and put the two equations together we see that the argument of $F$ is $x_{t-\Delta t}$, not $x_t$. 

Putting the two equations together, we have 
\begin{equation}
    x_{t+\Delta t} = \frac{F(x_{t-\Delta t}+\sigma_m\zeta_{t-\Delta t})}{\gamma}\Delta t + \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\xi_t.
\end{equation}
Provided $\sigma_m$ is small, we may expand the force to obtain
\begin{equation}
    x_{t+\Delta t} \approx \frac{F(x_{t-\Delta t})}{\gamma}\Delta t + \frac{F'(x_{t-\Delta t})}{\gamma}\sigma_m\zeta_{t-\Delta t} + \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\xi_t.\label{eq:measurementNoiseLangevin}
\end{equation}
This equation is not quite a Langevin equation and does not have the correspondence to the FPE that the Langevin equation does. However, if $\sigma_m$ and $\Delta t$ are fairly small, simulations reveal no noticeable difference in the quantities of interest. 

Using the Langevin equation rather than the Fokker-Plank equation for simulations is particularly desirable given the similarity between the analysis on integrated Langevin trajectories and those obtained from an experiment. We will compare the results from simulations with experimental results in Ch. 3. An example of the Mpemba effect is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:mpembaLangevin}.

Once we have a large number of trajectories, we compute the empirical PDF at every timestep. The analysis outlined in the previous section is then applied to the empirical PDFs. One of the key differences between the Langevin solutions and Fokker-Planck solutions is the existence of a \textit{noise floor}, resulting from the finite ensemble size. The fact that the empirical CDF can never be equal to the true CDF results in a noise floor that scales as $\order{\sqrt{N_b/N}}$~\cite{Kumar2020} with $N_b$ the number of histogram bins and $N$ the ensemble size\footnote{The $1/\sqrt{N}$ convergence is actually a little more complicated and does not necessarily work for any choice of distance function. Kumar and Bechhoefer demonstrated $1/\sqrt N$ scaling for the $\mathcal L_1$ distance of a uniform distribution. According to the central limit theorem, the empirical \textit{cumulative density function} $P_N(x,t)$ does converge to the true CDF $P(x,t)$ with $\sqrt N[P_N(x,t)-P(x,t)] \rightarrow \mathcal N[0,P(x,t)(1-P(x,t))]$ almost surely, which does lower-bound the $\mathcal L_1$-distance since 
\[P_N(x,t)-P(x,t) = \int_{-\infty}^x \left[ p_N(x',t) - p(x',t) \right]\dd{x'},\]
and 
\begin{align*}
    \sqrt N\abs{P_N(x,t)-P(x,t)} &= \sqrt N\abs{\int_{-\infty}^x \left[ p_N(x',t) - p(x',t) \right]\dd{x'}}\\
    &\leq \sqrt N\int_{-\infty}^x \abs{p_N(x',t) - p(x',t)}\dd{x'}
\end{align*}
by the triangle inequality. Thus, $\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \sqrt N\abs{P_N(x,t)-P(x,t)}\leq \mathscr D_{\mathcal L_1}[p_N,p]$, demonstrating that the $\mathcal L_1$ distance has a best-case convergence of $1/\sqrt N$ but could potentially converge more slowly. Fig.~\ref{fig:mpembaLangevin} demonstrates that the power law scaling does hold empirically for some distance functions over any value of $N$ we can reasonably use in the lab, so the discussion moving forward will take the scaling law as a given.}. As Fig.~\ref{fig:mpembaLangevin}(a) shows, the noise floor may be so high that the crossing between the hot and cold distance curves is obscured by the noise. This fact creates constraints on our choice of potential that we address further in Ch.~4.

\section{Previous work}
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{{./Figures/chapter2/asymmetricDoubleWellPotential/asymmetricWellPotential.pdf}}
    \caption[Bechhoefer and Kumar's potential]{\textit{The potential Bechhoefer and Kumar~\cite{Kumar2020} used, described in Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential}. We also illustrate the box constraint and the maximum slopes.}}
    \label{fig:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential}
\end{figure}
We have shown that to demonstrate the Mpemba effect, one need only find a potential $U(x)$ for which $a_2(T)$ is non-monotonic. A simple quadratic potential will not do: solving the FPE with $F(x)=-kx$ and initial condition $p(x,0)=\pi(x;T_h)$ yields the analytic solution
\begin{align}
    p(x,t) &= \sqrt{\frac{k}{2\pi \kB [T_c+(T_h-T_c)e^{-2kt/\gamma}]}}\exp[-\frac{kx^2}{2 \kB [T_c+(T_h-T_c)e^{-2kt/\gamma}]}]\label{eq:harmonicFPE}\\
    &= \pi\left( x; T_c+(T_h-T_c)e^{-2kt/\gamma} \right)
\end{align}
To convert this expression into an eigenfunction expansion, we expand in powers of $e^{-kt/\gamma}$ to obtain
\begin{equation}
    p(x,t) = \sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\pi}}e^{-\alpha x^2} + \sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\pi}}\left( \sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\pi}}x^2-\frac 12 \right)\left( \frac{T_h-T_c}{T_c} \right)e^{-\alpha x^2}e^{-kt/\gamma}+\order{e^{-2kt/\gamma}},
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = k/(\kB T_c)$.

From this, we gather that $\lambda_n = (n-1)k/\gamma$, and $a_2(T_h) = (T_h-T_c)/T_c$, which is monotonic in $T_h$. Thus, the Mpemba effect cannot exist in a harmonic potential~\cite{Walker2021}. Alternatively, we note that Eq.~\ref{eq:harmonicFPE} can be expressed as a Boltzmann distribution at every point in time. Since the evolution of a Boltzmann distribution of a harmonic potential must always be equilibrium-like, we may expect that it should never yield the Mpemba effect, which relies on nonequilibrium effects. This fact is unfortunate because the potential an optical trap imposes is approximately harmonic, so we cannot use a standard optical trap to demonstrate the Mpemba effect. Instead, Kumar and Bechhoefer used a \textit{feedback optical trap} to impose more esoteric potentials. 

A feedback optical trap generates a virtual potential using an optical trap, both of which we will describe in more detail in Ch. 3. A feedback trap is a tool that allows us to generate arbitrary time-varying forcefields by measuring the position of a particle and varying the force it experiences based on the current position measurement. 
A real medium cannot be heated to any arbitrarily high temperature\footnote{Bechhoefer and Kumar's experiment required temperatures as high as $T_h=1000T_c$, which in the real world would be around \qty{3e5}{\kelvin}, significantly hotter than the hottest known star in the universe; and certainly not physically sustainable in water.}, and even if it could, it would not quench to room temperature fast enough that the time dependence of the diffusivity in the Fokker-Planck equation could be ignored\footnote{This is to say the quench to room temperature needs to be \textit{quasi-instantaneous}. We discuss quenches more in the next section.}. Instead, they used a trick to ensure that the initial state is always a Boltzmann distribution at some temperature $T_h$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Choose a position $x_0\sim\pi(x;T_h)$ and place the particle at $x=x_0$. 
    \item Allow the particle to diffuse under the potential $U(x)$ long enough that the distribution $p(x,t)$ relaxes to steady state.
    \item Repeat for a large number of times $N$ to create an ensemble.
\end{enumerate}
This algorithm is equivalent to integrating the Langevin equation, which we described in the previous section. 

While feedback optical traps allow for near-arbitrary $F(x,t)$, there are a few physical constraints placed on the force that we will need to account for when designing a potential. The exact reason for the constraints will be explained further in Ch. 3. 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The feedback optical trap cannot generate arbitrarily high forces. In reality, the maximum force is capped: 
    \begin{equation}
        \abs{F} \leq F_{\max}.\label{eq:forceConstraint}
    \end{equation} 
    \item The feedback time $\Delta t$, which also serves as the measurement interval, should be much smaller than the characteristic timescale of the system, which near a local minimum is the relaxation time. Thus, $\Delta t \ll t_r = \gamma/k = \gamma/\abs {F'(x)}$ for $F(x) \approx -kx$ near a local minimum. 
    \begin{equation}
        \abs{F'(x)} \ll \gamma/\Delta t.\label{eq:derivativeConstraint}
    \end{equation} 
    \item The measured displacement of the particle $x$ will be accurate only if $x$ is within a certain small domain $x\in [X_{\min},X_{\max}]$, where $X_{\min}<0<X_{\max}$, called the \textit{calibration region}. For potentials that grow asymptotically in either direction, it is unlikely at low temperature that a particle will ever leave the calibration region. However, if $T_h$ is very large, then $\pi_h(x)$ will be approximately uniform, and this means that there is a reasonable probability that the particle could be given an initial position that is outside the calibration region. To ensure the accuracy of all measurements, Kumar and Bechhoefer imposed a constraint on the initial distribution $p(x,0)$, where all initial positions had to be drawn from a \textit{box}: a domain $\mathcal B = [x_{\min}, x_{\max}]$ chosen such that $\mathcal B\subset [X_{\min},X_{\max}]$. This constraint is equivalent to using a potential $U_{\mathcal B}(x)$, where
    \begin{equation}
        U_{\mathcal B}(x) = \begin{cases}
            U(x),& x\in\mathcal B\\
            \infty,& x\notin\mathcal B
        \end{cases}.
    \end{equation}
    Of course, since we cannot actually impose arbitrarily high forces, we cannot actually impose $U_{\mathcal B}(x)$ for $x\notin\mathcal B$, but if $x_0\in\mathcal B$, it is very unlikely that the particle would move to a region where the potential is higher; the only particles that are likely to leave are those that have an initial state very close to the boundaries of $\mathcal B$\footnote{Kumar and Bechhoefer noted that $x\notin\mathcal B$ for only \qty{0.003}{\percent} of the total experiment time~\cite{Kumar2020}, and no meaningful difference between simulated distance curves of initial conditions prepared with and without a box.}. Since $x\in\mathcal B$ for almost all times $t$, we can approximate $U(x)\approx U_{\mathcal B}(x)$. This assumption will, however, break down when we consider finite quenches. 
\end{enumerate}

With these constraints in mind, Bechhoefer and Kumar used an \textit{asymmetric double-welled potential},
\begin{align}
    U(x) &= \begin{cases}
        F_{\max}(x-x_r)+U_0(x_r),& x > x_r\\
        U_0(x),& x_l \leq x \leq x_r\\
        -F_{\max}(x-x_l)+U_0(x_l),& x < x_l\\
    \end{cases}\\
    U_0(x) &= E_b\left[ 1-\left( \frac{x}{x_w} \right)^2 \right]^2 + \frac 12 \Delta E\left( \frac{x}{x_w} \right),\label{eq:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential}
\end{align}
plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential}.

Here, $E_b=2\kB T_c$\footnote{The natural unit of energy to use at these scales is the thermal energy $\kB T_c \approx$\qty{4}{\zepto\joule}, which scales the energy in the Boltzmann distribution. This unit is not well-defined, since the temperature may vary between experiments; but this is arguably a feature rather than a limitation, since if we were to use a fixed unit often used in the literature like \qty{1}{\pico\newton\nano\metre}$=$\qty{1}{\zepto\joule} to determine the parameters of our potential, the results between different experiments would not be the same. Similarly, we choose lengths where the distance between wells for a potential \textit{with zero asymmetry}, $2x_w=1$. We do use seconds as the unit of time, but we could also nondimensionalise time by choosing units where the \textit{relaxation time}, which we define in chapter 3, is unity.} is called the \textit{barrier energy} and determines the height of the barrier at $x\approx 0$; $\Delta E=1.3\kB T_c$ is called the \textit{tilt energy} and determines the relative depth of the two wells; $F_{\max} = 50\kB T_c/(2x_w)$ is the maximum force described earlier, and $x_l$ and $x_r$ are chosen so that $U(x)$ is $\mathcal C^1$ continuous everywhere. 
This potential has two wells, one of which is at a higher energy than the other. At equilibrium, the probability mass in the left well will not be equal to that in the right. The two dominant eigenmodes of cooling from the Fokker-Planck equation (characterised by $\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_3$) then have an intuitive physical explanation, as outlined in Ch\'etrite et al.~\cite{Chetrite2021}. The first stage of cooling involves a \textit{fast relaxation}, characterised by the high rate $\lambda_3$\footnote{Higher eigenmodes also contribute to the fast stage, but $\lambda_3$ is the slowest of them all and therefore the rate-determining step.}, where nearly all of the probability mass immediately falls into the well it is closest to. The second stage of cooling, the \textit{slow stage} characterised by the lower value, $\lambda_2$, involves particles hopping over the barrier until the equilibrium mass ratios are reached. Choosing an initial condition so that the probability masses in both wells are already at their equilibrium ratios at the end of the fast stage then corresponds to the condition for the strong Mpemba effect, $a_2=0$. Vucelja and Walker formalised this intuition in their 2022 paper~\cite{Walker2022}, where they defined the right-well equilibrium probability mass
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_R(T) = \int_{x_b}^{x_{\max}} \pi(x;T)\dd{x},
\end{equation}
where $x_b$ is the position of the barrier (the local maximum). Given that $\lambda_2/D \rightarrow 0$, the idea we outline here for the strong Mpemba effect can then be restated as
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_R(T_h) = \Pi_R(T_c).\label{eq:VuceljaMpemba}
\end{equation}
We demonstrate the equivalence of this condition to the non-monoticity of $a_2$ in Fig.~[a2figure]. 
The box $\mathcal B$ is critical to Bechhoefer and Kumar's result: without it, $\Pi_R(T)$ grows monotonically and asymptotes at $\Pi_R(\infty)=1/2$. In particular, they defined an \textit{asymmetry parameter} $\alpha = \abs{x_{\max}/x_{\min}}$, and found that for specific choices of $\alpha$, the strong Mpemba effect could be achieved when $T_h \gg T_w$. Mathematically, $a_2$ is actually a function of both $T$ and $\alpha$, and a special value of $\alpha$ is chosen so that $a_2(T_h,\alpha)=0$. 

A recent preprint~\cite{Liu2026} argued that the box is necessary to observe the Mpemba effect in a double-welled potential. We shall test their claim in Ch. 4.

\section{The average energy cannot be used to find a Mpemba effect}
\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{{Figures/chapter2/averageEnergy/averageEnergy.pdf}}
    \caption{\small{The average energy is not a good way to measure the distance to equilibrium. We demonstrate a counterexample by using a \num{10000}-particle Langevin simulation of Kumar and Bechhoefer's experiment~\cite{Kumar2020}. (a) The three inset PDFs denote the same potential, Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential} that Kumar and Bechhoefer used with $\alpha=|x_{\max}/x_{\min}|=9$. The three inset PDFs denote stages of cooling (the $x$ axis of each PDF is truncated for readability). The red PDF, the initial state, is more or less uniform, and cools to the black PDF, overlaid in all figures as dashed lines. We see that at an intermediate time, the relative heights of the taller peak in the blue PDF is very slightly higher than it would be at equilibrium. The relative numbers of particles in each well at the intermediate time perfectly lines up with the equilibrium average energy, and the energy difference is thus equal to zero \textit{even though the system has not finished relaxing}. (b) $\mathcal D_c[p(x,t)]$ for the same system. We see that the system does not actually demonstrate a Mpemba effect. This diagram is a reproduction of Fig. 7 in~\cite{Teza2026}.}}
\end{figure}
The distance function requires that we have access to the probability distribution of the system we are studying. However, finding the PDF associated with a thermodynamic system is not always tractable. For example, an $N$-spin Ising lattice has $2^N$ possible states, and so the vector containing the probabilities associated with every possible state grows exponentially in memory. Thus, many consider it desirable to use another signature for the Mpemba effect. Equilbrium kinetic theory often defines the temperature of a gas in terms of the average kinetic energy of its molecules: $\ev{\text{K.E.}}=d\kB T/2$ for $d$ degrees of freedom by the equipartition theorem~\cite{Blundell2017}. While the temperature is not well-defined between the start and end times, the average energy,
\begin{equation}
    \bar E(t) = \ev{U}_{p(x,t)}=\int_{\mathbb R} U(x)p(x,t)\dd{x}
\end{equation}
certainly is\footnote{For the sake of having a dimensionless number instead of a number that scales with $\kB T_c$, we divide $\bar E(t)$ by the \textit{equilibrium average energy}, $\bar E_{\mathrm{eq}}=\int_{\mathbb R} U(x)\pi(x;T_c)\dd{x}$.}. Moreover, the average energy of an equilibrium system does grow monotonically with temperature. Several authors have thus used crossings (or their lack thereof) to discuss the existence or nonexistence of a Mpemba effect~\cite{Ohga2024,BaityJesi2019,Vadakkayil2021}. However, the average energy does not obey the third rule we required of distance functions: it is not guaranteed to decrease monotonically with time, and can thus erroneously lead one to believe there is a Mpemba effect when none exists. To show that the average energy is a poor choice of distance function, we choose $x_{\max}\gg x_{\min}$, so that the probability mass on the domain $[0,x_{\max}]$ is greater than in the domain $[x_{\min},0]$. As we discussed in the previous section, the first stage of cooling is a fast stage where the particle falls into the closest possible well, before the slow stage, when particles hop over the barrier until equilibrium is reached. Thus, if $\Pi_R(T_h) > \Pi_R(T_c)$, amounting to using a large value of $\alpha$ in the potential Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential} that Kumar and Bechhoefer chose, there would be a transient stage of cooling where there are \textit{more particles in a low-energy well than in equilibrium}. Thus, $\ev{U}_{p(x,t)} < \ev{U}_{\pi(x;T_c)}$. We illustrate this behaviour in Fig.~\ref{fig:averageEnergy}, noting that not only can the difference in average energies between $p(x,t)$ and the equilibrium state be zero even if $p(x,t)\neq \pi(x;T_c)$, violating the first assumption of positivity, but the Mpemba effect can be falsely predicted by the average energy. Kumar and Bechhoefer demonstrated that for large enough values of $\alpha$, the Mpemba effect vanishes~\cite{Kumar2020}. Thus, the average energy cannot be used in place of a distance function.

\section{Finite-rate quenches}
Physically, the approach stated thus far corresponds to an \textit{instantaneous} quench, a system that is initially at equilibrium in a bath of temperature $T_h$ and starts relaxing after the bath instantaneously cools to $T_c$. However, real systems usually exist in baths that gradually cool, often in the Newtonian way that the temperature in Eq.~\ref{eq:harmonicFPE} evolves. The assumption of the bath diffusivity instantly dropping, potentially by several orders of magnitude, is rather aphysical. The approach outlined here thus applies to small particles in a bath, but not to bulk objects. In a bulk object, heat would have to diffuse from the bath into the bulk through a boundary, meaning that the temperature of a point within the surface would lag behind the bath temperature somewhat depending on how fast heat can flow through the object. For example, a cold piece of meat placed on a hot pan needs to cook for several minutes before the interior temperature is the same as the surface temperature. Teza et al. have previously considered how coupling to an external bath through a `boundary term' affects relaxation dynamics~\cite{Teza2023}. Thus, we consider how the dynamics of our system change if the bath temperature slowly decays with time. Since the Mpemba effect is a feature of nonequilibrium systems, one would expect that a system that is sufficiently slowly quenched should not display the Mpemba effect. Similarly, a quench that is sufficiently fast would display the Mpemba effect in a system that displays a Mpemba effect with an instantaneous quench. This therefore prompts the question of what the critical rate is where the hot and warm systems reach equilibrium at almost the same time. To implement this requires that we create a new version of Kumar and Bechhoefer's experiment.

The na\"ive approach to add finite quench rates to Bechhoefer and Kumar's~\cite{Kumar2020} result is to simply make the diffusivity $D = \kB T_c/\gamma$ a function of time:
\begin{equation}
    D(t) = \frac{\kB T_ch(t; T_0)}{\gamma},
\end{equation}
where $h(t; T_0)$ is the \textit{quench function}, a dimensionless number defining the ratio of the bath temperature to the equilibrium temperature, $h(t; T_0) = T(t)/T_c$. The quench function is always a function of the initial temperature $T_0$ at which the initial Boltzmann distribution is set to. For our purposes, the quench function must have the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $h(t; T_0)=T_0/T_c\ \forall\ t\leq 0$ (to ensure that the system is in equilibrium at temperature $T_0$ at $t=0$);
    \item $h(\infty; T_0) = 1$ (the bath must be fully quenched at a sufficiently large time);
    \item $\partial_t{h} \leq 0$ (the bath temperature strictly does not increase, otherwise it would not be a quench);
    \item $\partial_{T_0}{h} > 0$ if $h(t;T_0)>1$ (the quench function should take longer to reach one for higher $T_0$; this keeps us from using a quench function that itself displays the Mpemba effect).
\end{enumerate}
We will use a quench function corresponding to Newtonian cooling, an exponential decay given by
\begin{equation}
    h_N(t;T_0, \Lambda) = \begin{cases}
        \frac {T_0}{T_c},& t \leq 0\\
        \left( \frac{T_0}{T_c} - 1 \right)e^{-\Lambda t} + 1,& t > 0
    \end{cases}.\label{eq:newtonianQuench}
\end{equation}
The quench rate $\Lambda$ here controls the rate of cooling. In general, since the defining properties of a quench function do not specify how quickly $h(t)$ approaches one, a parameter to define a cooling rate is useful. We require one more property of the parameter $\Lambda$ to ensure that it actually does function as a cooling rate:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[5] $\partial_{\Lambda} h(t;T_0,\Lambda)<0$ (increasing $\Lambda$ makes $h$ smaller in the same time $t$).
\end{enumerate}

Note that the time dependence of $D(t)$ on the right-hand side of the Fokker-Planck equation invalidates the separation \textit{ansatz}, Eq.~\ref{eq:separationAnsatz}. We therefore cannot use Lu and Raz's condition to determine the existence of a Mpemba effect given a nonzero quench rate. We also note that as $\Lambda\rightarrow\infty$, $h_N$ approaches the instantaneous quench,
\begin{equation}
    h_I(t;T_0) = \begin{cases}
        \frac{T_0}{T_c},& t \leq 0\\
        1,& t > 0
    \end{cases}.\label{eq:instantaneousQuench}
\end{equation}
Similarly, as $\Lambda\rightarrow 0$, $h_N$ approaches an \textit{quasistatic} quench, where
\begin{equation}
    h_A(t;T_0) = T_0/T_c. \label{quasistaticQuench}
\end{equation}
The quench rate $\Lambda$ cannot be equal to zero, as this would violate our second assumption about convergence to steady state. However, for any nonzero $\Lambda$, the quench will decay to one after a sufficiently long time (even if this time is much greater than the protocol time $\tau$). We may view this quench as allowing the system to come to equilibrium at every instant in time. Since the Mpemba effect is a nonequilibrium phenomenon, no Mpemba effect can exist in this system. 

Since we can choose a potential that displays a Mpemba effect under an instantaneous quench ($\Lambda \gg 0$) and we reason that the potential must never display the Mpemba effect under a sufficiently slow quench ($\Lambda \rightarrow 0$), we deduce that some critical $\Lambda^*$ exists below which the Mpemba effect exists, and above which there is no Mpemba effect. 

If we applied this to the system Bechhoefer and Kumar used to demonstrate the strong Mpemba effect, we would, however, find that there are major discrepancies. Recall the discrete-time Langevin equation~\ref{eq:discreteLangevin} has two terms of comparable scale: the deterministic term $F(x_t)\Delta t/\gamma$ and the stochastic term $\sqrt{2\kB T(t)\Delta t/\gamma}\xi_t$. If the stochastic term is much greater than the deterministic term, we may approximate Eq.~\ref{eq:discreteLangevin} as 
\begin{equation}
    x_{t+\Delta t} \approx \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\xi_t,
\end{equation}
which is the expression for free diffusion. This means that particles are free to leave the box $\mathcal B$, and we can no longer approximate $U(x) \approx U_{\mathcal B}(x)$, and consequently $p(x,0)$ is not the Boltzmann distribution of the potential we impose. This discrepancy manifests as an increase in the distance function, which is always supposed to decrease with time. In simulations, one may normally take care of this problem by setting appropriate boundary conditions, but there is no way to do this in an experiment. We thus need to find a new potential that displays the Mpemba effect without relying on a box\footnote{The box was also somewhat aphysical in the first place, and eliminating it is aesthetically desirable.}

A one dimensional system lives on a plate of thickness $\ell$ and uniform temperature $T_h$. (a) At $t=0$, the plate is placed atop the XY plane, which is a thermal reservoir held at temperature $T_c$. (b) As the plate cools to $T_c$, heat flows upwards and reduces the bath temperature of the system.}}

We claimed earlier that the Newtonian quench, Eq.~\ref{eq:newtonianQuench}, was a natural choice of quench function. But why this particular quench function, given that the Mpemba effect in our system relies on non-Newtonian cooling? We could, for example, choose a linear quench, $h(t; T_0, \Lambda) = \max(T_0/T_c(1-\Lambda t), 1)$. We will justify our choice of quench using a physical argument for how the bath might cool if its physical temperature were actually varied. 

Consider a one-dimensional system living on top of an infinitely wide plate of height $h$, as shown in the figure, that is at equilibrium at temperature $T_0$. The plate has thermal diffusivity $\alpha_T$ and thickness $\ell$. At $t=0$, the plate is placed atop the XY plane, which is a heat reservoir held at temperature $T_c$\footnote{We may perhaps assume the thermal diffusivity of the plane $\alpha_T'\gg \alpha_T$ so that it has a constant temperature $T_c$ over the timescale of cooling.}. Heat will then flow uniformly along the $z$ axis through the plate until the surface containing the system has cooled from temperature $T_0$ to temperature $T_c$. To find how the temperature of the plate evolves, we solve the heat equation with boundary conditions:
\begin{align}
    \pdv{T}{t} &= \alpha_T\pdv[2]{T}{z}\\
    T(0, t\geq 0) &= T_c\\
    T(z>0, 0) &= T_0\\
    \eval{\pdv{T}{z}}_{z=\ell} = 0.
\end{align}
\marginnote{\tiny{I'm using $T_0$ instead of $T_h$ because I use $T_0$ implies an arbitrary initial temperature rather than $T_h$, which more implies to me the existence of a warm temperature $T_w$}}
The first boundary condition enforces that the XY plane is a thermal reservoir. The second enforces the initial temperature of the system. The final boundary condition enforces that no heat flows beyond $z=\ell$: one may imagine that the entire setup lives inside a perfectly insulating medium. 

Solving, we find that 
\begin{align}
    T(z,t) &= T_c + \frac 2\ell (T_0-T_c)\sum_{n=0}^\infty \left[ \frac{\sin\left( \frac{(2n+1)\pi z}{2\ell} \right)}{\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{2\ell}} \right]e^{-\frac{(2n+1)^2\pi^2 \alpha_T t}{4\ell^2}}.
\end{align}
Notice, when $t=0$, the series expansion is identical to the Fourier series of a square wave of height $T_0-T_c$, which represents the fact that the plate is initially uniformly at temperature $T_0$.

Defining 
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda_{2n+1} = \frac{(2n+1)^2\pi^2 \alpha_T}{4\ell^2} = (2n+1)^2\Lambda_1,
\end{equation}
we see that the series can be represented as
\begin{equation}
    T(z,t) = T_c + \frac 2\ell (T_0-T_c) \left[ \frac{\sin\left( \pi z/2\ell\right)}{\pi/2\ell}e^{-\Lambda_1 t} + \frac{\sin\left( 3\pi z/2\ell\right)}{3\pi/2\ell}e^{-9\Lambda_1 t} +\dots\right].
\end{equation}
We evaluate this equation at $z=\ell$ to get the temperature of the system, $T(t)=T_ch(t)$:
\begin{align}
    T(\ell,t) -T_c &= \frac 4\pi (T_0-T_c) \left[ \sin\left( \pi /2\right)e^{-\Lambda_1 t} + \frac 13\sin\left( 3\pi/2\right)e^{-9\Lambda_1 t} +\dots\right]\\
    &= \frac 4\pi (T_0-T_c) \left[ e^{-\Lambda_1 t} - \frac 13e^{-9\Lambda_1 t} + \frac 15 e^{-25\Lambda_1 t}-\dots\right].
\end{align}
The term in the brackets has the form of a series $x-x^9/3+x^{25}/5-\dots$. We note that for all $t\geq 0$, the ratio test reveals that the series is absolutely convergent for all times. We also note that at $t=0$, the series is identical to the Taylor series expansion of $\arctan(1)=\pi/4$, which cancels the prefactor. Since $\Lambda_3 = 9\Lambda_1$, the second term is expected to be much smaller than the first for sufficiently large times, so the series may be truncated to one term at sufficiently large times, yielding the expression
\marginnote{\tiny Should I add a plot of $T(z,t)$ at different times?}
\begin{equation}
    T(\ell,t) -T_c \sim (T_0-T_c)e^{-\Lambda_1 t}.
\end{equation}
Dividing throughout by $T_c$, we find
\begin{equation}
    h(t; T_0, \Lambda_1)-1 \sim (T_0/T_c -1) e^{-\Lambda_1 t}.
\end{equation}
This scaling relation justifies the form of $h_N(t; T_0, \Lambda)$ we used earlier, and suggests that the natural extension to the quench protocol would be to add other, faster exponential decay terms.

In this chapter, we describe the theory, setup, and experimental protocol for an optical feedback trap, as well as some specifics about how we apply the theory from Ch. 2 to this system and derivation of the constraints from Sec. 2.10. \section{Optical traps}
Optical traps, first described by Ashkin et al in 1986, allow one to hold objects in place without physically touching them, using a focussed laser~\cite{Ashkin1986}. The working principle of an optical trap is not the same at different length scales: two different explanations exist based on the ratio of the length scale $L$ of the trapped object to the wavelength $\lambda$ of the laser~\cite{Jones2015}. 

In the limit where the characteristic length scale $L \gg \lambda$, an optical trap functions because of Newton's laws and the momentum of photons. As photons refract inside optically denser media, their direction --- and therefore their momenta --- change. By Newton's second law, a change in the momentum of photons implies that a force acted on them, and by Newton's third law, the photons must also exert a reaction force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. The net reaction force is given by~\cite{Jones2015}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf F = \sum_{k} \left[ \frac{n_iP_i^{(k)}}{c}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_i^{(k)} - \frac{n_tP_{t,0}^{(k)}}{c}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{t,0}^{(k)} - \frac{n_iP_{t,1}^{(k)}}{c}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{t,1}^{(k)} + \dots\right],
\end{equation}
for a sphere of diameter $L$ of optical density $n_t$, with incident power of the $k^{\mathrm{th}}$ ray $P_i^{(k)}$, refracted power $P_{t,0}^{(k)}$, transmitted power (after the ray leaves the sphere) $P_{t,1}^{(k)}$, and other contributions from reflections and the reflected rays' refraction and further reflection. The net forces can be split into a \textit{scattering force}, which pushes the sphere away from the beam along the optical axis due to radiation pressure, and a \textit{gradient force}, which acts against the displacement of the sphere. For distances less than one particle radius from the trap centre, the reaction force always opposes the displacement of a sphere from the focus of a beam. This length regime is called the \textit{Mie regime}.

If $L \ll \lambda$, light can be thought of as an electromagnetic wave, and the sphere is thought of as a dipole. Solving Maxwell's equations for such a system yields~\cite{Jones2015}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf F_{\mathrm{DA}} = \underbrace{\frac 14 \Re{\alpha_d}\nabla \abs{\mathbf E}^2}_{\text{Gradient}} + \underbrace{\frac{2\pi\Im{\alpha_d}}{\lambda\epsilon_0c}\mathbf S}_{\text{Scattering}} - \underbrace{\frac {\pi c\Im{\alpha_d}}{\lambda\epsilon_0}\nabla\times\mathbf s_d}_{\text{Spin-curl}},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_d$ is the complex polarisability, $\mathbf E$ the electric field, $\mathbf S = \Re{\mathbf E\times\mathbf H}/2$ the Poynting vector, and $\mathbf s_d = i\epsilon_0\mathbf E\times\mathbf E^*/(2\omega)$ the time-averaged spin density. The spin-curl term, a non-conservative force arising from a non-homogeneous electric field polarisation, is usally small and can be ignored. Thus, we are left with a gradient and scattering term, as before. This length regime is called the \textit{Rayleigh scattering regime}. 

The systems we consider are \textit{mesoscopic}: the characteristic length, the diameter of the silica beads we trap, is $d = $ \qty{1.49}{\micro\metre}. The laser we use to trap the beads has a wavelength $\lambda = $ \qty{0.572}{\micro\metre}. Thus, the radius of the beads is similar to the wavelength of the laser, and so neither the Mie regime nor the Rayleigh regime completely explains the behaviour of the bead. More complete theories, not within the scope of this thesis, must exactly solve the Maxwell equations with appropriate boundary conditions to completely explain the gradient and scattering forces.

However, in all cases, the gradient force exceeds the scattering force for small displacements\footnote{The scattering force is the reason the trap centre differs slightly from the focus of the beam}, and a potential that has the following properties is generated:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For \textit{small} displacements from the trap centre, the potential is approximately quadratic, behaving like a spring with constant $k$
    \item For \textit{very large} displacements from the trap centre, the beam does not pass through the sphere at all. Thus, the force must be zero.
\end{enumerate}
The precise form of $F(x)$ depends on the beam profile and the length regime; for a Gaussian beam in the Rayleigh regime, a simple dipole approximation of the bead yields~\cite{Harada1996}
\begin{equation}
    F(x, y=0, z=0)\propto -x\exp[-2(x/w_0)^2], 
\end{equation} 
$w_0$, called the `beam waist', is related to the width of the Gaussian beam. Thus, $U(x)\sim -e^{-x^2}$. We plot a sketch of the trap potential in Fig.~\ref{fig:opticalFeedbackTrap}(b).
These properties suggest that there is a maximum force $F_0$ beyond which the force reduces to zero. 

\section{Feedback forces: a toy model}

The carefull designed potentials in Ch. 2 are made possible using feedback. To understand how this is possible, suppose we have a system that allows one to impose a fixed force $F_m(x|X)$, but we can control the value of $X$. If we wanted to use this system to impose any arbitrary force $F(x)$, we must choose $X$ such that $F_m(x|X)=F(x)$. Based on measurements of $x(t)$, $X(x)$ is chosen so that $F_m(x|X(x))=F(x)$, where $F(x)$ is some arbitrary `target' force. 

A toy example is an overdamped cart connected to a movable wall, as shown in fig.~\ref{fig:feedbackCart}. The force the spring imposes on the cart is given by Hooke's law,

\begin{equation}

The general experimental setup for a feedback trap thus requires four features:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A system to generate a force (the spring in the toy example);
    \item A system to measure the position of the trapped object (the eye with which the position of the cart is observed);
    \item A system to calculate how much force is required for a given position, within a sufficiently fast time $\Delta t$ (the brain of the human observing the cart); and
    \item A system to move the centre to a new position within $\Delta t$ (the hand of the human moving the wall).
\end{enumerate}

The approach outlined here is highly oversimplified. It assumes that measurements are made continuously --- in practice, the delay time $\Delta t$ affects measurement, not just force application --- and ignores the effects of noise on the system. For a more comprehensive discussion, including a discussion of how the nonlinearities associated with discrete measurements lead to instabilities, see Jun and Bechhoefer~\cite{Jun2012}. However, the approach here does outline the essential fact that for a feedback potential to function equivalently to a true potential, we must have $\Delta t \ll t_r$, which remains true in all subsequent analysis. 

\section{Optical feedback traps}

Virtual potentials using feedback traps were first realised in microscopic systems by Cohen and Moerner with their `Anti-Brownian ELectrokinetic' (ABEL) trap to cancel the Brownian motion of a trapped Brownian object~\cite{Cohen2005, Cohen2006}. Jun and Bechhoefer extended their results to generate near-arbitrary virtual potentials~\cite{Jun2012}. Their system generated feedback using electrostatic forces: two electrodes were connected across a sample cell filled with a dilute colloid of \qty{200}{\nano\metre} fluorescent beads in water. Feedback was enabled with a camera observing the trapped bead, and an image processing algorithm to determine the required feedback force. However, since the system used electrodes to generate forces, there were nontrivial electrochemical effects that needed to be accounted for. Moreover, the feedback time of \qty{10}{\milli\second} limited the performance of their trap. 

To address these issues, Kumar and Bechhoefer built an \textit{optical} feedback trap using an optical trap to generate the force, a Quadrant PhotoDiode (QPD) for measuring the position, and a Field-Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) for computing the feedback force, implemented using an Acousto-Optical Deflector (AOD) to move the beam by very small angles. The setup is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:opticalFeedbackTrap}. Two lasers are used in the setup: a high-power (\qty{500}{\milli\watt}) \qty{572}{\nano\metre} \textit{trapping laser} to trap a Brownian particle, a \qty{1.49}{\micro\metre} silica bead in water, and a low-power ($\sim$\qty{2.5}{\milli\watt}) \qty{700}{\nano\metre} \textit{detection laser}, which is deflected by the bead and falls on the QPD. Dichroic filters ensure that the lasers' respective paths are appropriately contained. Additionally, a blue LED is used to illuminate the sample, so that a microscope camera can view the setup, although the camera output is not used in the feedback loop. The FPGA is a circuit made of programmable logic gates, which allows for extremely fast execution of code, with a feedback time of \qty{10}{\micro\second}. We program the FPGA using LabVIEW's FPGA module.

Because of the maximum force constraint on optical trap forces, the optical trap does not perfectly satisfy the requirement of Eq. ~\ref{eq:feedbackRequirement} for any general $F:\mathbb R\rightarrow\mathbb R$. However, if the codomain of the target force is restricted to $[-F_{\max}, F_{\max}]$ as we did in Ch. 2, the trap can be used for the restricted set of potentials that we discussed. In practice, we choose $F_{\max}$ to be a value where the quadratic approximation of the trap potential is good\footnote{Slight deviations from quadraticity can also be corrected for with the calibration steps we outline here.}.

\subsection{Sample preparation and trapping}
A sample is prepared using a colloid of beads in a solution of water and sodium azide. Sodium azide ensures that the samples are not contaminated by \textit{E. coli}, which may otherwise attach themselves to beads and cause spurious trap calibration. A sample cell is prepared using spacer wires about \qty{100}{\micro\metre} in diameter and a coverslip on a microslide, using nail polish to ensure a waterproof seal. Imperfect sealing will result in evaporation, which will cause drifts that manifest as $1/f$ noise in the power spectrum of a trapped bead. Roughly \qty{35}{\micro\litre} of the colloid is pipetted into the cell, ensuring no air bubbles remain in the cell. The sample cell may then be loaded into the trap, set between the two objectives. 

The sample cell is moved by an XYZ stage until the focal point of the trapping laser lies inside the sample cell, just above the coverslip. A bead may then be trapped. The camera is helpful both in finding the focus --- using the interference pattern the laser makes on the coverslip --- and in finding a bead to trap. Care must be taken to ensure that only a single bead is trapped at once. The stage is then adjusted in the $z$ direction so that the bead is well above the coverslip, to remove surface convection effects. The dilution is chosen so that the probability of another bead falling into the trap by happenstance over the duration of an experiment is low. The dilution of a new sample is about \qty{50}{\micro\litre} of the master solution in a \qtyrange{1}{1.5}{\milli\litre} of \qty{0.8}{\molar} aqueous solution of sodium azide, but the dilution is adjusted based on whether too many beads are in the field of view at any given time. Ideally, a bead should be relatively easy to find but the field of view should only contain one bead. 

Since the sample cell is placed vertically, a coordinate system may be defined, with the $z$ axis along the beam path and the $y$ axis against the direction of gravity. The $x$ direction, assuming the optics are aligned correctly, is used for all experiments in this thesis.

The QPD outputs three signals: $V_x$, $V_y$, and $V_{\mathrm{sum}}$. We first block the QPD and find the dark noise $\ev{V_x}$, $\ev{V_y}$, and $\ev{V_{\mathrm{sum}}}$, and subtract these values from the QPD signal to get $\Delta V_x$, $\Delta V_y$, and $\Delta V_{\mathrm{sum}}$. We then scale the responses to $s_x=\Delta V_x/\Delta V_{\mathrm{sum}}$ and $s_y=\Delta V_y/\Delta V_{\mathrm{sum}}$ to compensate for potential variability in the intensity of the diode laser. 

The deflection produced by the AOD, as well as the response of the QPD, are not linear. Additionally, as we discussed earlier, the harmonic approximation for the potential is imperfect. Therefore, a linearisation step is required to compensate. The AOD is given a triangle wave voltage input signal, $V_{\mathrm{in}}(t)$, moving the bead along a trajectory that should everywhere be piecewise linear in time. However, since the response of the bead $x(t)$ is in general not linear with $V_{\mathrm{in}}$, the transfer function must be estimated by fitting $\Delta V_{\mathrm{in}}$\footnote{$V_{\mathrm{in}}$ is a voltage signal with an offset of \qty{5}{\volt}; the \textit{deviation} of $V_{\mathrm{in}}$ is more relevant to computing the response.} against $\Delta s_x$ with a fifth-order polynomial $\Delta V_{\mathrm{in}}(t)\sim P_5(\Delta s_x(t))$. The nonlinear fit only works if the amplitude of $V_{\mathrm{in}}$ is chosen so that $V_{\mathrm{in}}$ is injective in $s_x$. For our experiments, the maximum amplitude happens to be around \qtyrange{0.10}{0.15}{\volt}. If the input amplitude is too large, the peaks of $V_{\mathrm{in}}$ will correlate with a drop in $s_x(t)$ as the bead leaves the area through which the detection laser passes. It is also important that the response $s_x(t)$ is symmetric about the $y$ axis with zero offset: if this is not the case, the alignment mirrors are slightly adjusted. The signal is also multiplied by a calibration constant $C'$ converting the scaled voltage output of the QPD to a length unit, $x=C'P_5(\Delta s_x)$. The precise length unit does not matter since every quantity will be scaled by the same length; thus, the length unit is chosen for convenience to be a pixel, which corresponds to about\qty{40}{\nano\metre} in length; $C'\approx$\qty{67}{\pixel}. Provided no optical components are moved between experiments, it is unnecessary to find the voltage-to-length factor during each experiment. The physical meaning of $P_5(s) = C_0+C_1s+C_2s^2+C_3s^3+C_4s^4+C_5s^5$ is unimportant as it is only important to ensure that the voltage measurements have a fixed correspondence with length measurements; consequently, some of the coefficients in the polynomial are expected to vary quite widely between different calibrations. $x$ now varies linearly with $V_{\mathrm{in}}$ and thus we can safely use the results from section 3.2. As we perform the fit, we pay particular attention to the area around $s_x=0$; the particle spends most of the time close to the origin, so it is important that the calibration is best near the origin. Once calibration is complete, the segments of the signal should look like sharp peaks and valleys, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:nonlinearCalibration}. If the peaks are not sharp enough, calibration is repeated. 

\subsection{Measurement of trap constant and diffusivity}

The inverse variance of the data yields the equipartition estimate $k_{\mathrm{eq}}$; fitting the negative log of the empirical PDF yields the estimate $k_{\mathrm{PDF}}$, and fitting the PSD to the aliased Lorentzian from Berg-S\o renson and Flyvbjerg~\cite{BergSoerensen2004} yields the final estimate $k_{\mathrm{PSD}}$. In the toy model, we assumed that the spring constant $k$ was known: this is essential to knowing precisely how far to move the wall. The spring constant is equivalent to the trapping constant, which is not known in the scaled and calibrated length units. Furthermore, knowledge of the diffusivity is also necessary, since the energies associated with the virtual potential are scaled with $\kB T_c$, which is proportional to $D$. Since units are chosen so that $\kB T_c := 1$, finding $D$ amounts to finding $1/\gamma$. Recalling that $k/\gamma = t_r$, and defining a \textit{corner frequency} $f_c = 1/t_r$, we may then find the corner frequency as well as the trap constant in order to determine the diffusivity.

To measure $k$ and $D$, we record the trajectory of the bead over \qtyrange{20}{30}{\second}. The trajectory, $x(t)$, is expected to be an \textit{Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process}~\cite{Risken1996}. We may thus extract the trap constant in three separate ways. 

\subsubsection{Variance estimation}
By the equipartition theorem, the variance $\sigma_x^2$ of a particle in thermal equilibrium at temperature $T_c$ is given by 
\begin{equation}
    E = \ev{\frac 12 kx^2} = \frac 12 k\sigma_x^2 = \frac 12 \kB T_c.\label{eq:equipartition}
\end{equation}
Thus, the trap constant is estimated to be $k = \kB T_c/\sigma_x^2$. However, the equipartition theorem lumps together measurement noise of the QPD as well as fluctuations in the particle's motion, and so the trap constant estimated using this technique is somewhat flawed. In practice, the position $x_m$ we measure is the sum of the true position $x$ and a measurement noise $\chi\sim\mathcal N[0,\sigma_m]$. The variance we measure is then the sum of variances: $\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}^2 = \sigma_x^2+\sigma_m^2$, which leads to a biased estimate of $k$.

\subsubsection{Probability densities}
If the trap force is assumed to be quadratic, $U=kx^2/2$, the equilibrium probability density 
\begin{align}
    \pi(x;T_c) &= \sqrt{\frac{\kB T_c}{\pi k}}\exp[-\frac{kx^2}{2\kB T_c}]\\ \label{eq:harmonicEquilibrium}
    kx^2/2
    &= U_0-\ln[\pi(x;T_c)]\\
    U_0 &= \frac 12 \ln\left[ \frac{\kB T_c}{\pi k} \right].
\end{align}
Thus, computing the empirical probability density from the trajectory $x(t)$ and fitting its logarithm to a quadratic will yield estimates of $k$. This method also leads to biased estimates of $k$ due to measurement noise.

\subsubsection{Power spectral density estimation}
Since the particle follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, the Power Spectral Density (PSD) of the particle's trajectory,
\begin{equation}
    S_n = \frac 1\tau\ev{\hat x_n^2},
\end{equation}
where $\hat x$ denotes the Fourier transform of $x$, and the index $n$ implies that the discrete Fourier transform is used, evaluated at $f_n=n/N\Delta t$ for sampling time $\Delta t=$ \qty{10}{\micro\second} and $N$ being the total number of data (since the fast Fourier transform returns as many $\hat x_n$ as there are $x_n$). For an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process for a particle in a trap with stiffness $k$, friction coefficient $\gamma$, and diffusivity $D$, the PSD is given by a Lorentzian~\cite{BergSoerensen2004}:
\begin{equation}
    S(f) = \frac 1{2\pi^2}\frac{D}{f_c^2+f^2},
\end{equation}
where the corner frequency $f_c = k(2\pi\gamma)$
using the fluctuation-dissipation relation~\ref{eq:fluctuationDissipation}. However, since there is a finite sampling frequencing, the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem applies and thus the signal is aliased at or above $f_s/2 = 1/(2\Delta t) = $\qty{50}{\kilo\hertz}. The formula must therefore be modified to account for aliasing. Berg-S\o renson and Flyvbjerg showed that the modified formula was~\cite{BergSoerensen2004}
\begin{equation}
    S_n = \frac 1{2\pi}\frac{(1-c^2)D\Delta t}{1+c^2 +2c\cos(2\pi n/N)},~\label{eq:aliasedLorentzian}
\end{equation}
with $N$ the number of samples, and $c=e^{-2\pi f_c/f_s}$. 

The power spectral density is calculated from the discrete position data using \textit{Bartlett's method}: the data (consisting of $N$ position measurements) are divided into $n=$\num{20} nonoverlapping segments, each of size $\lfloor N/n\rfloor$~\cite{Press2007}. 

We then fit the PSD to Eq.~\ref{eq:aliasedLorentzian}. The parameters we obtain are multiplied by a factor of $n/(n-2)$ in order to correct for the bias linked to the number of degrees of freedom~\cite{BergSoerensen2004}.

The three sets of parameters we obtain should ideally agree to within about \qty{5}{\percent} relative error. The aliased Lorentzian is the most accurate way of estimating the trap constant, and we therefore use the estimates we get from fitting the aliased Lorentzian as parameters in the software. If the aliased Lorentzian differs greatly from the fit over the range of \qtyrange{0.6}{20}{\hertz}, $1/f$ noise is significant and so the parameters obtained from any of the methods outlined here are unreliable and cannot be used. The cause of such a discrepancy may be
\marginnote{\small{I don't think my simulations can handle Ornstein Uhlenbeck processes without a lot of work --- the noise is pregenerated for efficiency and thus delta-correlated.}}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Sample contamination}: If the bead sample is contaminated with \textit{E. coli}, individual bacteria may get caught in the trap. The individual bacteria are visible as small rodlike objects floating around the bead. In this case, a new bead sample must be prepared.
    \item \textit{Improper sealing}: If the sample cell is not completely sealed, evaporation will cause drifts that manifest as $1/f$ noise. In this case, a new sample cell must be prepared.
    \item \textit{Multiple trapped beads}: If multiple beads are trapped at once, the interaction of the beads will cause low-frequency noise, visible as a large `bump' in the PSD at $f<$\qty{5}{\hertz}. In this case, the best strategy is to briefly block the trapping laser, retrap a single bead, and repeat the calibration steps outlined earlier. The bead sample should be diluted so that the probability of two beads being in the field of view of the camera at once should be low for the entire duration of the experiment.
\end{enumerate}
Additionally, there may be spikes in the PSD at \qty{60}{\hertz} with small peaks at higher multiples. These spikes are electrical noise from the mains frequency that the QPD can pick up through mutual inductance and optical pickup from the lights. To mitigate electrical noise, the lights in the room are turned off and the setup is covered for the duration of the experiment\footnote{Covering the experiment is also useful for safety reasons as it creates a secondary barrier between the experimentalist's eyes and the \qty{500}{\milli\watt} trapping laser.}. Furthermore, if optical components need to be realigned, we must wait for at least several hours (or better yet, over a day) for transient low-frequency creep from the components' springs to completely die down. Airflow in the room from an air conditioner may also be a source of noise as it may vibrate the sample cell very slightly. Finally, acoustic pickup from sources close to the experiment can create another spike at around \qty{160}{\hertz} in the PSD.

Once the calibration is complete and the various important constants are estimated, the experiment can begin. The Mpemba feedback protocol involves repeating the same sequence of steps several thousand times so that we have enough data to get meaningful statistical results. Each protocol takes \qty{150}{\milli\second} or less, and consists of three steps. 
\subsection{Drift correction}
Over the course of the protocol, very low frequency noise, or drifts caused by the optics or vapourisation of the sample, may cause the offset of the beam to be nonzero. The effect manifests as the mean position of the bead increasing or decreasing over periods of time much greater than the protocol time $\tau$. If the drift is too large, the bead will leave the region of sensitivity of the detector laser; this cannot be corrected for with software. However, if drifts are small, we can correct for the drift for a time by finding the mean of the particle's position when it experiences no feedback and subtracting the mean from all position readings for the rest of the protocol. Over the roughly \qty{50}{\milli\second} this part of the protocol takes, the recursive average is computed from position readings, logged, and subtracted. After an experiment is complete, the drift velocities can be estimated (by subtracting the current drift from the previous drift and dividing by the average protocol time), and histogrammed to check whether drift was significant in the experiment. Usually, the drift values will be normally distributed. If the mean is near zero, drift was negligible.

\subsection{Positioning}

In previous iterations of the experiment, a cumulative density function was computed from the Boltzmann distribution of the potential we choose at the initial temperature. The CDF $\Pi(x;T_0)$ is evaluated over the domain $\mathcal B$ for a mesh size of \num{512} and loaded to the FPGA as an array, $\mathtt{CDF}_n$. To select an initial position, the transformation method~\cite{Press2007} is used. A uniformly distributed random number $r\sim\mathcal U[0,1]$ is first generated. We then find the index $n$ for which $\mathtt{CDF}_n < r \leq \mathtt{CDF}_{n+1}$ using bisection search. These values correspond to the entries $\mathtt{x}_n$ and $\mathtt x_{n+1}$ respectively. We then use linear interpolation to approximately solve for $x_0\in[\mathtt x_n, \mathtt x_{n+1}]$:
\begin{equation}
    x_0 = \frac{\mathtt x_{n+1}-\mathtt x_n}{\mathtt{CDF}_{n+1}-\mathtt{CDF}_n}(r-\mathtt{CDF}_n)+\mathtt{x}_n.
\end{equation}
We rely on linear interpolation to estimate $x$, and so the accuracy of the position chosen will depend on the size of the mesh used to generate $\mathtt{CDF}_n$. A higher mesh size will cause the initial distribution to be much closer to the target distribution $\pi(x_0;T_0)$, but the memory on the FPGA is limited and so the mesh size must be capped at a certain maximum. 

Once $x_0$ is selected, the bead needs to be positioned at $x=x_0$. The trap centre is moved to $x_0$ and the stiffness is increased to a larger value $k'$ so that the bead moves to the new position faster. The particle is allowed to equilibrate in this position for \qty{30}{\milli\second} before the next part of the protocol is active.

However, we found that this method proved inaccurate. If the trap stiffness is not infinite, the bead would occupy a position normally distributed with mean $x_0$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{\kB T_c/k'}$. Consequently, the initial probability density would not be $\pi(x; T_0)$ but actually $\pi_0*\mathcal N[x_0, \sqrt{\kB T_c/k'}]$, where $*$ denotes convolution:
\begin{equation}
    (p*q)(x) = \int_{\mathbb R} p(y)q(x-y)\dd{y}.
\end{equation}
The effect of the nonzero standard deviation would be to slightly flatten out the initial distribution (see figure). The effect is particularly noticeable when $T_0$ is low.

To avoid the flattening, we instead use a first-passage approach. A small value $\epsilon$ is chosen, and the trap centre is moved to $x_0$. When the position of the bead is $x \in [x_0-\epsilon, x_0+\epsilon]$, the bead is `released' and the next part of the protocol begins. Choosing $\epsilon$ is a tradeoff between precision and time: if $\epsilon$ is too low, the bead is unlikely to fall into the domain within the maximum number of timesteps allocated for this purpose; if it is too high, the initial PDFs will be distorted by the aforementioned convolution.

\subsection{Protocol}
The feedback force $F(x)$ is now applied for the remainder of the protocol. If we want to test a non-instantaneous quench, a noise force $\eta(t)\sim\mathcal N[0,\sigma(t)]$ is chosen with $\sigma(t)\propto \sqrt{\kB T_ch(t)}$ and added to $F(x)$. The force is applied for a fixed number of feedback loops, and the state is then set to the calibration step again. We can repeat this for a long time. Over the course of the experiment, we watch the recorded drifts and the particle on the camera to ensure the quality of the data. The trajectory of the particle is also visible, and we can get a loose sense of whether the experiment is going well by observing the trajectory: a particle in a double well will spend most of its time around one of the two wells, occasionally hopping between them. If we do not see this behaviour, there is likely a problem with the calibration.

\section{Data analysis}
The data we have collected is one very long trajectory with several columns.
Throughout the protocol, we record the following variables:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Position}: the position of the bead, in pixels.
    \item \textit{Time}: the current loop number. Previous versions of the experiment directly recorded the time in seconds, but since we use 32-bit floating point numbers to represent data, floating point error is not insignificant.
    \item \textit{Drift}: the recursively averaged position while the protocol is in the harmonic well calibration state.
    \item \textit{Force}: the force in $k_BT_c/$\unit{\pixel} sent to the AOD. This is useful for checking whether the force is accurate.
    \item \textit{$x_0$ or $\eta$}: the initial position found by inverse transform sampling if we use the first-passage technique, or the noise at each time if we use a noisy potential to determine the initial position. If we use first-passage methods, this variable is useful for checking whether inverse transform sampling is working correctly; if we use noise, this variable is useful for checking if the noise force is properly distributed.
    \item \textit{State}: an integer indicating the current status. We need this variable to ensure that any analysis routines can separate the protocol from the calibration and positioning steps.
    \item \textit{Temperature}: an integer indicating which initial temperature we are using. This variable was unnecessary in previous iterations of this experiment as we would take separate datasets for each temperature, but we found that interweaving the temperature data together minimised the risk that drifts would affect one dataset but not the others.
\end{enumerate}
The data takes the form of one very long trajectory. To run analysis on it, we need to split the data into `chunks' with a more readable matrix-like format. The \texttt{Xarray} library in Python is particularly helpful in this regard\footnote{\texttt{Xarray.DataArrays} and \texttt{Xarray.Datasets} are multidimensional spreadsheet-like objects labelled by `dimensions', of which there are three: $T$, the temperature; $n$, the ensemble index; and $t$, the time. These are often used by climate scientists to track multiple variables like temperature, pressure, etc. at various geographical coordinates over time.}. After the data are properly formatted, it is saved to disk for later use. The raw output of the optical trap is quite large, containing a lot of redundant information and variables that we do not need after the first processing steps and is therefore slow to load; preprocessing the data thus makes analysis much more convenient. Simulations use the same data structure to store their output, so the analysis pipeline for simulations and data is identical. We histogram the data using a custom \texttt{Numba}-optimised function designed for the data format we use to compute the empirical PDF and calculate the distance function at every point in time. 

We always take the distance function with respect to the empirical steady-state PDF, which is calculated by histogramming all or most of the data at $T_0=T_c$ into one single low-noise histogram, not the theoretical PDF calculated from the Boltzmann distribution. While the empirical PDF should in theory very closely match the theoretical PDF, in practice we find that the crossing is more apparent when we use this technique.

Once analysis is complete, the quality of the data is easily assessed by looking at the distance curve for $T_0=T_c$. If the forces were accurately computed, the distance should not change significantly beyond noise. The first-passage approach often displays a phenomenon where the $T_0=T_c$ curve very slightly reduces in the first few milliseconds, and if this is the case it is likely that the trap calibration was inaccurate or drifts were significant over the course of the protocol.

We estimate the drifts by histogramming the drift values with time. If drifts are insignificant, the histogram should be Gaussian with a mean near zero. Otherwise, the mean will be a few standard deviations away from zero. We can estimate drift velocities by subtracting consecutive drift values from each other and dividing by the average protocol time.

\section{Requirements for a boxless potential}
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{{./Figures/chapter2/asymmetricDoubleWellPotential/asymmetricForcePotential.pdf}}
    \caption[The modified potential]{The modified potential, with variable slopes, that we found. One way to ensure that the potential imposed can always be approximated as a true potential is to consider a potential $U(x)$ and demand that at the highest temperature $T_h$, the probability mass outside of a region of interest be negligible. In other words, we define a \textit{calibration} domain $\mathcal B' \subset \mathbb R$ such that 
\begin{equation}
    \int_{\mathcal B'} \pi(x;T_h)\dd{x} > 1-\frac 1N,~\label{eq:probabilityCondition}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of Langevin integrations for some potential $U(x)$\footnote{$\pi(x;T_h)$ must be normalised over $\mathbb R$, not $\mathcal B'$, otherwise the inequation will trivially always be true.}. If the inequation~\ref{eq:probabilityCondition} is true, then all of the $N$ initial positions $x_0$ are likely within $\mathcal B'$. Thus, the empirical PDF we estimate by histogramming all values of $x_0$ is indistinguishable from the empirical PDF we would obtain if $x_0$ were sampled from the true Boltzmann distribution $\pi_h$. We may thus apply the quench as we please. This estimate, however, is a worst-case heuristic, since it assumes the distribution is at temperature $T_h$ over the entire protocol time $\tau$, so we may choose potentials whose probability masses are slightly below $1-1/N$ without affecting the results too much. In practice, this means that $\mathcal B'$ is as large as the voltage-position calibration will allow and $T_h$ is small, since the distribution $\pi(x;T_h)$ has its mass spread out over a larger area as $T_h$ increases. 

To identify candidate potentials, we perform a number of tests before running a simulation. The first is to check the non-monotonicity of the $a_2$ coefficient associated with the potential. Since we seek a strong Mpemba effect, we search for potentials where $a_2(T_h) = 0$ for some sufficiently small $T_h$. The asymmetric double-welled potential fails to satisfy these criteria, as $a_2(T)$ is not monotonic if $x_{\min}$ and $x_{\max}$ are sufficiently large. We find the $a_2$ coefficients by discretising the Fokker-Planck operator into a $N_m\times N_m$ $\mathbb W$-matrix, following Grima and Newman's technique~\cite{Grima2004}: 
\begin{align}
    \mathbb W_{n,n+1}(t) &= \frac{D(t)}{\Delta x^2}\exp[\frac{U(x_{n+1})-U(x_n)}{2\kB T(t)}]\\
    \mathbb W_{n,n-1}(t) &= \frac{D(t)}{\Delta x^2}\exp[\frac{U(x_{n-1})-U(x_n)}{2\kB T(t)}]\\
    \mathbb W_{n,n} &= -\mathbb W_{n,n+1}-\mathbb W_{n,n-1}\\
    \mathbb W_{1,0} = \mathbb W_{N_m,N_m+1} &= 0\\
    x_n &= x_{\min}+n\Delta x\\
    \Delta x &= \frac{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}{N_m}\label{eq:GrimaNewman}
\end{align}
Here, $N_m$ is the mesh size of the domain $[x_{\max}, x_{\min}]$. We then use standard numerical libraries to find eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the $\mathbb{W}$-matrix if $D(t) = \kB T(t)/\gamma$ is constant, and use Eq.~\ref{eq:an} to find $a_2(T)$. We can also use the discretised $\mathbb W$-matrix to solve the Fokker-Planck equation: expressing $p_n(t) = p(n\Delta x, t)$, we can solve for an arbitrary time using the Euler method $p_n(t+\Delta t) \approx p_n(t)+\sum_{m}\mathbb W_{n,m}p_{m}\Delta t$ or fourth-order Runge-Kutta methods to solve for $p(x,t)$~\cite{Press2007}\footnote{The success of the numerical methods described here depends heavily on the choice of time discretisation, $\Delta t$, and often requires very small values of $\Delta t$ to ensure that the probability remains properly normalised throughout. However, $\Delta t$ is often so small that solving the Fokker-Planck equation analytically is more computationally expensive than solving the Langevin equation with $N>$\num{100000}. Further work, not within the scope of this thesis, could make massive improvements in efficiency by discretising space and time with adaptive mesh sizes.}. Since $\mathbb W$ is sparse, it is fastest to explicitly compute the matrix-vector product manually in a loop, which can later be optimised by a library like \texttt{Numba}:
\begin{align*}
    p_1(t+\Delta t) &= p_1(t) + [\mathbb W_{1,2}(t)p_2-\mathbb W_{2,1}(t)p_2]\Delta t\\
    p_k(t+\Delta t) &= p_k(t) + [\mathbb W_{k,k+1}(t)p_{k+1}-(\mathbb W_{k-1,k}(t)+\mathbb W_{k+1,k}(t))p_k + \mathbb W_{k,k-1}(t)p_{k-1}]\Delta t,\quad 2\leq k \leq N_m-1\\
    p_{N_m}(t+\Delta t) &= p_{N_m}(t) + [\mathbb W_{N_m,N_m-1}(t)p_{N_m-1}-\mathbb W_{N_m-1,N_m}(t)p_{N_m}]\Delta t.
\end{align*}
However, since $N_m=\order{1000}$, we use standard numerical algorithms to compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, since sparse eigenvalue algorithms such as those implemented in \texttt{scipy} are probabilistic and can introduce very slight inaccuracies; besides, the standard numerical algorithms are almost always fast enough. It is also useful for the sake of efficiency to precompute
\begin{align}
    S_{n,n+1} &= \exp[\frac{U(x_{n+1})-U(x_n)}{2\kB T_c}]\\
    S_{n,n-1} &= \exp[\frac{U(x_{n-1})-U(x_n)}{2\kB T_c}]\\
    S_{i,j} &= 0, \quad j\notin\{i-1,i+1\}.
\end{align}
and then calculate
\begin{align}
    \mathbb W_{n,m}(t) &= \frac{D_ch(t)}{\Delta x^2}S_{n,m}^{1/h(t)}
\end{align}
in each loop.

The analytic curves for the harmonic and quartic potentials in Fig.~\ref{fig:mpembaFPE} and Fig.~\ref{fig:mpembaLangevin} were computed with this technique.

However, simply finding potentials with $a_2(T_h^*)=0$ for low $T_h$ is insufficient, as we must reconstruct the Fokker-Planck solutions using a finite number of integrations or trials, $N$. If the crossing predicted by the Fokker-Planck equation occurs below this noise floor, the Mpemba effect will be invisible. The largest $N$ achievable in an experiment is $\order{10^4}$, and we therefore cannot make achieve an arbitrarily low noise floor. To ensure that the Mpemba effect is as prominent as possible, we therefore require more criteria that the potential should satisfy.

The first criterion is to choose only potentials that demonstrate a strong Mpemba effect, because exponentially faster cooling accentuates the Mpemba effect. We can satisfy the criterion simply by checking whether there exists $T^*>T_c$ such that $a_2(T^*)=0$ for some sufficiently low nonzero $T^*$. However, this criterion alone proves insufficient for a large class of potentials, because the crossing between the hot and warm curves may occur at a distance lower than the noise floor. A crossing that occurs in this region would be undetectable with a reasonable number of Langevin integrations, even if the exponentially faster nature of the cooling would be visible in the long run. 

Secondly, we will also choose $T_w$ that maximises $a_2(T_w)$. While not strictly necessary, ensuring that the second component of the eigenfunction expansion is as large as possible will make the difference between the two distance curves most promiment. Thus, we try to make the following optimisations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maximise $\int_{\mathbb B'} \pi(x;T_h)\dd{x}$ subject to the constraints that $\mathcal B'$ should be as small as possible for fixed $F_{\max}$;
    \item Minimise $T_h>T_c$ for which $a_2(T_h)=0$;
    \item Maximise $a_2(T_w)$, where $T_c<T_w<T_h$.
\end{itemize}

To modify the potential used previously, the intuition for the strong Mpemba effect that we outlined in Eq.~\ref{eq:VuceljaMpemba} needs to be slightly modified. Because of the constraints on maximum slopes, we may view any potential we choose as a perturbation on the absolute value function $U_0(x)=F_{\max}|x|$. The details of $U(x)$ matter progressively less at higher and higher temperatures; consequently, we may approximate
\begin{align}
    \Pi_R(T_h) &\rightarrow \frac{\int_{0}^{\infty} \exp(-F_{\max}|x|/\kB T_h)\dd{x}}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp(-F_{\max}|x|/\kB T_h)\dd{x}}\\
    &= \frac 12.
\end{align}
If, however, we changed the right slope so that it was some fraction $f=\frac{F_{\mathrm{right}}}{F_{\max}}$ of the maximum slope, this formula is modified:
\begin{align}
    \Pi_R(T_h) &\rightarrow \frac{\int_{0}^{\infty} \exp(-fF_{\max}x/\kB T_h)\dd{x}}{\int_{-\infty}^{0} \exp(F_{\max}x/\kB T_h)\dd{x}+\int_{0}^{\infty} \exp(-fF_{\max}x/\kB T_h)\dd{x}}\\
    &= \frac 1{1+f},
\end{align}
as $T_h\rightarrow\infty$. The rate $\lambda_3$ corresponds to the `fast' mode of cooling discussed earlier, where all of the probability mass falls into the closest well to it. Recall that if this ratio matched with the equilibrium mass ratio, we could expect a strong Mpemba effect. Indeed, we find that this condition alone allows us to reduce the high temperature necessary from $T_h = 1000T_c$ to $T_h \approx 12T_c$. 

The potential we thus choose is defined as follows:
\begin{align}
    U(x) &= \begin{cases}
        fF_{\max}(x-x_r)+U_0(x_r),& x > x_r\\
        U_0(x),& x_l \leq x \leq x_r\\
        -F_{\max}(x-x_l)+U_0(x_l),& x < x_l\\
    \end{cases}\\
    U_0(x) &= E_b\left[ 1-\left( \frac{x}{x_w} \right)^2 \right]^2 + \frac 12 \Delta E\left( \frac{x}{x_w} \right).\label{eq:asymmetricSlopesDoubleWellPotential}
\end{align}
As before, we choose $x_r$ and $x_l$ to satisfy $U_0'(x_r)=-fF_{\max}$ and $U_0'(x_l)=F_{\max}$. We find that $\Delta E=0.6\kB T_c$, and $f=0.3$ more or less optimise the conditions we outlined earlier, with $T_h\approx 12T_c$ and $T_w\approx 3T_c$ for a strong Mpemba effect and visible with about \num{5000} trials. All other parameters are identical to those of Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricDoubleWellPotential}. We plot the modified potential and the $a_2$ values in Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetricForcePotential}.

\section{The boxless potential displays the Mpemba effect}
For the potential Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricSlopesDoubleWellPotential}, $T_h=12T_c$, which is low enough that force clipping does not significantly affect the results: simulations show no significant distortions in the clipped and unclipped data. The probability mass outside a fairly small box $\mathcal B'=[-3,5]$~\unit{\pixel} from Eq.~\ref{eq:probabilityCondition} is about $0.9996$, which is high enough that no particles will likely leave the box even in a fairly slow finite quench. We plot the $a_2$ coefficient in Fig.[figure], noting that the potential not only displays the strong Mpemba effect with a fairly low maximum but also crosses the $T$ axis more than once, suggesting that there are multiple temperatures that would allow for exponentially faster cooling. This phenomenon, called a \textit{double Mpemba effect} was first documented via simulations in a system much like ours by Malhotra and L\"owen in 2024~\cite{Malhotra2024}. 

On solving the Fokker-Planck equation, we see the signature of the strong Mpemba effect: the curve for the warm temperature has a secondary `bump' that arises from the dominant $\lambda_2$ term, whereas the hot curve decreases much more rapidly\footnote{Actually, the solution to the FPE reveals that the $\lambda_2$ term \textit{does} eventually dominate the behaviour, since $a_2(T_h^*)= \epsilon_{a_2}$ where $\epsilon_{a_2}\approx 0$ is set by the solver to . However, the distance level at which the $\lambda_2$ term starts to become relevant is so small that it is far below the noise floor for any realistic number of Langevin integrations and even close to the zone where errors from the finite mesh size start to dominate the distance curves for the FPE solver. Thus, more precisely, our experiment would be \textit{indistinguishable from a strong Mpemba effect in any experiment} if we choose the stated value of $T_h$.}.

We confirm this with Langevin simulations. We find that about \num{5000} integrations are necessary for the effect to be unambiguous, few enough that an experiment is possible with this potential.

\section{Finite-rate quenches: simulations}
We now use Langevin simulations to see the effects of a finite-rate quench. The natural values of $\Lambda$ one may start with are multiples of the eigenvalues of the instantaneously quenched Fokker-Planck operators, $\lambda_k$. We find that $\Lambda=\lambda_2$ consistently displays no Mpemba effect. For $\Lambda=\lambda_3$, the hot and warm systems seem to approach the noise floor at roughly the same time. $\Lambda=\lambda_4$ and higher always display a Mpemba effect. This result matches our expectation, as $\lambda_2$ is related to the Kramers rate, which should not affect the fast dynamics of the particle, but $\lambda_3$ is the rate-determining mode in the fast dynamics of the particle. Thus, we hypothesise $\Lambda^*\approx \lambda_3$. However, this result is only qualitative, and possibly depends on the choice of parameters we used for the potential.

The piecewise nature of the potentials used here may be of some concern. Although we have shown here that infinite walls are not necessary for the existence of the Mpemba effect, our potential is nonanalytic because it has two points, $x_l$ and $x_r$, where the potential is not twice-differentiable. Such nonanalyticities may be present in effective potentials of coarse-grained models but not fundamental potentials, which are the sum of analytic terms. One may yet wonder whether a fully analytic potential on $\mathbb R$\footnote{\textit{Analytic} on some open set $\mathcal S$ here means that a function is equal to its infinite Taylor series expansion $\forall\ x\in\mathcal S$.} shows the Mpemba effect or whether the Mpemba effect is an artefact of the piecewise nature of the potential. A recent preprint, Liu et al~\cite{Liu2026} has suggested to this end that the Mpemba effect does not exist in analytic potentials. In this section, we show that the Mpemba effect is more a consequence of the physical shape of the potential, and to this end show that a fully analytic approximation to the potential defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricSlopesDoubleWellPotential} yields the Mpemba effect.

To test whether analytic potentials can show the Mpemba effect, we first rewrite Eq.~\ref{eq:asymmetricSlopesDoubleWellPotential} as a single-line definition:
\begin{equation}
    U(x) = \mathrm{rect}\left( \frac{x}{X_r-X_l} \right)U_0(x)+\mathrm{ReLU}(fF_{\max}(x-X_r))+\mathrm{ReLU}(-F_{\max}(x-X_l))
\end{equation}
Here, we have used two \textit{non-analytic} functions to define $U(x)$, the \textit{rectangle} function and the \textit{Rectified Linear Unit} function\footnote{$\mathrm{ReLU}$ is popular as a choice of nonlinear function in many modern machine learning applications.}:
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{rect}(u) &= \begin{cases}
        1, u\in [-1,1]\\
        0, u\notin [-1,1]
    \end{cases}\\
    \mathrm{ReLU}(u) &= \begin{cases}
        u, u > 0\\
        0, u \leq 0
    \end{cases}.
\end{align}
We also use $X_l$ and $X_r$ as parameters instead of $x_l$ and $x_r$. While they are related, these allow us to avoid having extra terms to ensure $\mathcal C^0$ continuity.

To construct an analytic approximation $U_a(x)\approx U(x)$, then, we need only construct analytic approximations to the two nonanalytic functions\footnote{Constructing (potentially nonanalytic) functions or distributions as the limit of a series of analytic functions is familiar in physics: the Dirac delta $\delta(x)$, for example, can be expressed as the limit of a sequence of progressively narrower Gaussians $\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0}e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}/Z(\sigma)$, and the step function $\theta(x)$ can be expressed as the limit of a sequence $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\tanh(kx)$.}. One way of approaching this is as follows:
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{rect}_n(u) &= e^{-u^{2n}}\\
    \mathrm{ReLU}_n(u) &= \frac 1n\ln(1+e^{nu})
\end{align}
We see that while
\begin{align}
    \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{rect}_n(u) &= \mathrm{rect}(u)\\
    \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{ReLU}_n(x) &= \mathrm{ReLU}(x),
\end{align}
meaning that the \textit{limit} is non-analytic, even though the functions are analytic for finite $n\in\mathbb N$. Thus, we can simply replace the nonanalytic functions in the potential definition with their analytic counterparts and use some sufficiently large value of $n$\footnote{We could also choose a low value of $n$ but leave $X_l$ and $X_r$ as free parameters. We then sample $U(x)$ and choose $X_l$ and $X_r$ using a curve fit to be the parameters that make $U_a(x; X_l, X_r)$ very close to $U(x)$.}. When we construct these analytic approximations, we find that they closely match the piecewise potentials and the $T_h$ value found by calculating $a_2(T_h)=0$ is approximately the same. If non-analyticity were necessary for a potential to display the Mpemba effect, we would expect that $a_2(T)$ would be monotonic for the analytic approximation. Therefore, a potential need not be analytic nor have infinite walls to display the Mpemba effect\footnote{Liu et al.~\cite{Liu2026} used a slightly non-standard definition of analyticity that also required an infinite radius of convergence on $\mathbb C$. This definition excludes any potential that is not a pure polynomial. The functions described here do converge everywhere on $\mathbb R$ but can diverge at certain values in $\mathbb C$. Thus, they do not have infinite radii of convergence. Their result demonstrated that no pure polynomial potential can demonstrate the Mpemba effect; rather, the potential must either have a box $\mathcal B$, be defined piecewise, or (as we have shown) use non-polynomial functions such as logarithms, exponentials, or inverses.}.

\chapter{Conclusions}
In this thesis, we have outlined the techniques we can use to achieve finite-rate quenches. We have shown via simulation and solutions to the Fokker-Planck equation that the finite-rate quench creates a number of constraints on the potential we use, because of the finite maximum force output of an optical trap and because of the inaccuracy of the previously-used `box' in finite quenches. \section{Other methods for imposing quenches}
\subsection{Imposing noise experimentally with a Johnson resistor}
The noise clipping constraint only applies to our system if noise is programmatically generated and added to the force of the trap. Saha et al.~\cite{Saha2023} used a Johnson resistor with an amplifier to create a noisy electric field across a sample cell, allowing them to achieve effective temperatures as high as $T_h=\order{100T_c}$ since the forces on the particle are no longer constrained by the physical limitations of the trap we outlined earlier. However, passing an electric field through water would cause electrolysis over time, creating bubbles that would create high drifts.

\subsection{Coordinate transformations}
Alternatively, we could use a coordinate transformation technique. Weitsman~\cite{Weitsman1976} considered the problem of the diffusion equation with time-varying diffusivity:
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{p}{t} = D_ch(t)\pdv[2]{p}{x}.
\end{equation}
He showed that using the time transformation $\tilde t(t) = \int_0^t h(t')\dd{t'} = H(t)$, the equation can be recast as $\partial_{\tilde t} p = D_c\partial_x^2p$, which is much easier to solve. We apply the same trick to the Fokker-Planck equation:
\begin{align}
    \pdv{p}{t} &= -\pdv{x}\left[ \frac{F(x)}{\gamma}p(x,t) \right] + D_ch(t)\pdv[2]{p}{x}\\
    \pdv{\tilde p}{\tilde t} &= -\pdv{x}\left[ \frac{F(x)}{\gamma H^{-1}(\tilde t)}\tilde p(x,\tilde t) \right] + D_c\pdv[2]{\tilde p}{x},
\end{align}
where $\tilde p(x,\tilde t) = p(x,t)$. Here, we must be careful to use $h(t(\tilde t)) = h(H^{-1}(\tilde t))$, passing as the argument of $h$ the inverse function $H^{-1}(\tilde t(t))=t$. In doing this, we may define a new force $\tilde F(x,\tilde t) =F(x)/h(H^{-1}(\tilde t))$, yielding the Fokker-Planck equation $\partial_{\tilde t}\tilde p = -\partial_x(\tilde F(x,\tilde t) \tilde p)/\gamma + D_c\partial_x^2\tilde p$. Since $h(t)\geq 0$ for all $t>0$, $H(t)$ is monotonically increasing and therefore guaranteed to be invertible. While we have not eliminated the time dependency on the right-hand side, we have made the problem considerably easier to tackle with the experimental constraints we have: instead of needing a high diffusivity, we only need a low force for a longer time, and the feedback optical trap can generate low forces easily. The time we measure in the lab is relabelled to $\tilde t$ and converted to $t$ after the experiment is complete.

Basak et al.~\cite{Basak2026} showed that higher effective temperatures yielded faster equilibration, provided the forces are proportionally increased; we have shown here that the converse is also true: decreasing the force and increasing the time of the experiment has the same effect as increasing the bath temperature. Indeed, it is easily seen that choosing Eq.~\ref{eq:instantaneousQuench} as the form of $h(t)$, $\tilde F(x)=F(x)/(T_h/T_c)$ and $\tilde t = t/(T_h/T_c)$, which is exactly what they found. We need only perform a post-hoc transformation on the time to convert a much easier force to impose in the lab\footnote{Such techniques are already familiar to eliminate space-dependent diffusivity. By making the transformation $\tilde x(x)=\int_{x_0}^x \sqrt{D/D(x')}\dd{x'}$, space-dependent diffusivity $D(x)$ is replaced with an arbitrary positive constant $D$~\cite{Risken1996}.}. For the form Eq.~\ref{eq:newtonianQuench}, we find that for all $t>0$, 
\begin{equation}
\tilde h(\tilde t; T_0, \Lambda) = W\left( \frac{\Delta T}{T_c} e^{\Delta T/T_c-\Lambda\tilde t}\right)+1,
\end{equation}
where $\Delta T=T_0-T_c$ and $W$ is the Lambert $W$-function, defined by $W(xe^x):=x$.If we want a protocol time $\tau$ (chosen so that $\Lambda\tau \gg 1$) in transformed time units, we must use a protocol length $\tilde\tau$ in laboratory frame units with $\tilde h(\tilde \tau; T_0, \Lambda)=h(\tau; T_0, \Lambda)$. Thus,
\begin{align}
    W\left( \frac{\Delta T}{T_c} e^{\Delta T/T_c-\Lambda\tilde\tau}\right)+1 &= \frac{\Delta T}{T_c} e^{-\Lambda\tau}+1\\
    \frac{\Delta T}{T_c} e^{\Delta T/T_c-\Lambda\tilde\tau} &= \frac{\Delta T}{T_c} e^{-\Lambda\tau}\exp[\frac{\Delta T}{T_c} e^{-\Lambda\tau}]\\
    \frac{\Delta T}{T_c}-\Lambda\tilde\tau &= -\Lambda\tau+\frac{\Delta T}{T_c} e^{-\Lambda\tau}\\
    \tilde\tau &= \tau-\frac{\Delta T}{T_c}\left( \frac{e^{-\Lambda\tau}-1}{\Lambda} \right).
\end{align}
By construction, $\Lambda\tau\gg 1$. Thus, the exponential vanishes and we are left with
\begin{equation}
    \tilde\tau \approx \tau+\frac{\Delta T}{T_c}\Lambda^{-1}.
\end{equation}
If $T_0\gg T_c$, then $\tilde\tau$ might be intractably large. However, we have found potentials in this thesis for which $T_h/T_c=\order{10}$. The slowest quench we might try in the lab is $\Lambda=\lambda_2$. Usually, $1/\lambda_2\approx\tau/20$ is fairly typical. Thus, the time we would need to conduct the experiment would be at worst only about twice as long as usual, or roughly \qty{120}{\milli\second} for $\Delta T=20T_c$.

\section{Two-dimensional potentials}
The original toy model Lu and Raz used was a particle in a periodic triple-well landscape, which they approximated as a three-state system. In one dimension, we cannot impose a periodic landscape, but if the functionality of the optical feedback trap is extended to use both the $x$ and the $y$ directions, we can use a potential where we vary the polar angle of the particle instead of the $x$-displacement. We can thus apply the much more theoretically tractable results of a three-state system to inform the parameters we choose, allowing us to test theories of quenching on discrete master equations.

\section{The symmetrisation Mpemba effect}
We considered throughout this thesis a phenomenon wherein an initially hotter system cools faster than a warm system. But in the years since Lu and Raz first published their theory~\cite{Lu2017}, there has been considerable work on the \textit{quantum Mpemba effect}, first proposed by Ares et al.~\cite{Ares2023} in 2023, and demonstrated experimentally in a trapped-ion quantum simulator by Joshi et al.~\cite{Joshi2024} in 2024. In their construction, an isolated system is considered rather than a system coupled to a bath. The Hamiltonian operator that evolves the system obeys a certain symmetry, eg. $\mathcal U(1)$, $\mathcal{SU}(2)$, or --- particularly relevant to the discussion here --- $\mathcal Z_2$\footnote{$\mathcal Z_2$ denotes symmetry under reflection.}. The system is initialised in an asymmetric state, often a pure state. The asymmetry of the system is quantified by some \textit{entanglement asymmetry} $\Delta S$, analogous to the  and the Mpemba effect is said to occur if $\Delta S$ goes to zero faster for an initially `more asymmetric' state. 

Recently, Summer et al.~\cite{Summer2025} proposed that the different Mpemba effects could be united using a tool from quantum information theory, \textit{resource theories}. A detailed discussion of resource theories are beyond the scope of this appendix, but suffice to say for our purposes that they provide a very general definition of the Mpemba effect, and define the Mpemba effect of a system like ours in terms of an \textit{athermality monotone} and a Mpemba effect in quantum systems in terms of an \textit{asymmetry monotone}. 

Moroder and Teza realised that the asymmetry monotone they defined was not unique to quantum systems and could be applied to classical systems like the ones we study. To this end, they defined the asymmetry monontone on $\mathcal Z_2$ symmetry as follows: consider a probability density $p(x,t)$, and the \textit{twirling operator} $\mathcal G[p(x,t)]=[p(x,t)+p(-x,t)]/2$. $\mathcal G[p]$ is guaranteed to have perfect reflection symmetry. The asymmetry monotone is then defined as the distance between $p(x,t)$ and the symmetrised version of $p(x,t)$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal D_s[p(x,t)] = \mathscr D[p(x,t),\mathcal G[p(x,t)]],
\end{equation}
for some choice of distance function $\mathscr D$. The asymmetry Mpemba effect occurs if, given two initial states $p_0(x,0)$ and $p_1(x,0)$ with $\mathcal D_s[p_0(x,0)] > \mathcal D_s[p_1(x,0)]$, there exists $t^*$ for which $\mathcal D_s[p_0(x,t^*)]<\mathcal D_s[p_1(x,t^*)]$ for all $t\geq t^*$.

To verify that the effect exists in classical systems, we choose a harmonic potential, the approximate potential that an optical trap imposes on a particle\footnote{Moreover, the Fokker-Planck equation has an exact solution for a harmonic potential, as we described earlier, so this potential is analytically convenient.}, and choose length units so that $\kB T/k=1\sigma$. Thus, the equilibrium distribution will be $\pi(x)=\mathcal N[0,1]$. We choose the initial states to be the sum of two off-centre Gaussians:
\begin{align}
    p_0(x,0) &= a_0\mathcal N[\mu_0,\sigma_0] + a_0'\mathcal N[\mu_0'+\sigma_0']\\
    p_1(x,0) &= a_1\mathcal N[\mu_1,\sigma_1] + a_1'\mathcal N[\mu_1'+\sigma_1'],
\end{align}
where $a_i+a_i'=1$. We find that for $\mu_0=0.4\sigma$, $\mu_0'=0.6\sigma$ and $\mu_1=4\sigma$, $\mu_2=-4\sigma$, with $\sigma_0\approx\sigma_0'$ and $\sigma_1\neq \sigma_1'$, the state $p_1$ will obviously take more time to equilibrate, and therefore symmetrise, than state $p_0$ but $p_0$ is still more asymmetric than $p_0$. Moreover, Vucelja and Walker demonstrated that the harmonic potential cannot display a thermal Mpemba effect~\cite{Walker2021}, so the asymmetry Mpemba effect is truly a distinct phenomenon.

We performed this experiment by loading the CDFs for $p_0$ and $p_1$ into the optical feedback trap and using Kumar's first-passage technique to choose positions $x_0$, scaled \textit{down} by $\sqrt{k/\kB T_c}$. We then `switch off' feedback and let the particles evolve under the natural force of the trap. Finally, we scale \textit{up} all position measurements by the same factor so that the length units are in trap-sigmas. We very straightforwardly find that the system displays the symmetrisation Mpemba effect, and the results are consistent with the Fokker-Planck equation.
